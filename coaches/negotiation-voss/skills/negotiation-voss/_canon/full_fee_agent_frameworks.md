## Core Premise — "Full fee" is a posture, not a price

Voss + Shull argue that most service providers lose negotiations before the negotiation starts — by internalizing the prospect's pricing frame. The moment the agent (or agency) starts thinking "they'll never pay full price, I should pre-discount," the agent has lost.

**Full fee is a state of mind:**
- You charge what you charge because of the value you deliver.
- You don't apologize for the number.
- You don't pre-discount to "win" the prospect.
- You walk away from prospects who won't pay it — calmly, without resentment.
- The prospects who DO pay are the ones who got actual value.

This is why pre-discounting kills service businesses: it filters IN the wrong clients (the ones who don't value the work) and filters OUT the right ones (the ones who would have paid full fee if you'd asked properly).

## Framework A — The "Go Directly" Principle

**What it is:** Have the hard conversation about price, scope, and expectations UP FRONT — not at the close, not during delivery, not at renewal.

**Mechanism:**
- Prospects always have unspoken concerns about price, scope, or fit. If you don't surface them in the first 10 minutes, they surface as objections at the close — when you have no time to handle them properly.
- "Going directly" means naming the elephant in the first meeting. Use the accusation audit format from NSTD Ch 3.

**Script architecture for service-business intake:**
1. Open with the accusation audit ("You're probably worried that this is going to cost more than you wanted, and that you're going to end up disappointed like you have been before.")
2. Use a calibrated question to surface their real worry ("What's the biggest concern you have about working with us?")
3. Label what they say ("It sounds like the last agency overpromised and underdelivered, and you're trying to figure out if we're going to do the same thing.")
4. Wait for "that's right."
5. Only THEN talk price.

**Why this works at DR:** law firm managing partners have almost always been burned by an agency before. The accusation audit lets them put that history on the table — which means it stops controlling the call invisibly.

## Framework B — The "No" Gateway

**What it is:** Engineering the prospect to say "no" early as a way to signal safety, control, and seriousness.

**Service-business application:**
- "Is it ridiculous to think this might be a fit?" — invites a "no" that means "no, it's not ridiculous."
- "Have you given up on finding an agency that actually delivers?" — invites a "no" that means "no, I haven't given up — I'm still looking."
- "Would it be unreasonable to walk you through the engagement structure?" — invites a "no" that means "no, it's not unreasonable, go ahead."

**Why "no" sells full fee:** prospects who feel in control — who feel they can say no any time — relax. Relaxed prospects pay full fee. Cornered prospects pre-discount themselves (you offer first, or they walk).

## Framework C — The "What Makes You Think We Discount?" Stance

**What it is:** Refusing to acknowledge the discount frame at all.

**Mechanism:**
When a prospect says "what's your best price?" or "we have other agencies coming in lower" or "the budget is tight, what can you do?", most service providers either:
(a) flinch and discount
(b) defend the price ("here's why we're worth it")
(c) split the difference ("I can do 10% off")

All three lose.

**Voss + Shull's move: calibrated question that puts it back on them.**
- "How am I supposed to do that without changing the scope of what you'd get?"
- "What about our work makes you think the price is the issue?"
- "Help me understand — what's driving the question about price specifically?"

These do not engage with the discount premise. They force the prospect to articulate the real concern (which is almost never price — it's risk, fit, or speed-to-value).

## Framework D — Fee Architecture for Service Businesses

**Setup the book argues for:**
1. **One full-fee tier.** No discount tier, no "starter" tier that undercuts your own fee.
2. **Walk-away discipline.** Be willing to lose the deal. The prospects you lose to discount-shoppers were never going to be good clients.
3. **Scope is the only lever.** If they "can't afford" the full fee, reduce the scope — never the rate. Same hourly value, less work.
4. **Don't bundle discounts with deliverables.** "If you sign today" is a discount tell — burned. Use "if we both commit by Friday, here's how the timeline shifts" — a TIME concession, not a price concession.

## Framework E — "Fish Where the Fish Are" (ICP Discipline)

**What it is:** A negotiation problem is often a positioning problem upstream. If you're constantly defending price, you're talking to the wrong prospects.

**Diagnostic questions:**
- Are these prospects pre-qualified for budget, or are they self-selected from your widest funnel?
- Do they understand the value of what you deliver, or are they comparing on inputs (hours, deliverables)?
- Are they the economic decision-maker, or are they running a procurement gauntlet for someone else?

**Fix:**
- Narrow the ICP to prospects who already accept your fee category as table-stakes.
- Use content/positioning to filter OUT discount-seekers before the sales call.
- Stop selling to the procurement floor — sell to the partner who owns the P&L.

**DR application:** the discount-pressure DR feels most often is on the entry-tier Lead Qualification System ($4,000/mo). Voss + Shull's diagnosis: that's a positioning problem, not a negotiation problem. Either the prospect is wrong (cut them) or the offer isn't differentiated enough that price comparison feels obscene (fix upstream — /dunford handoff).

## Framework F — The Cancel/Renegotiate Move (Retention Negotiation)

**Scenario:** an existing client says they want to pause, cancel, or "renegotiate the retainer."

**Wrong moves:**
- Immediately offer a discount to retain
- Justify the existing rate by listing what they got
- Argue the results were good

**Voss + Shull's protocol:**
1. **Label the underlying emotion.** "It seems like something happened recently that made the retainer feel like the wrong investment." Wait.
2. **Calibrated question.** "What changed for you between the original engagement and now?"
3. **Listen for the Black Swan.** Is it really price? Or is it: new boss, budget freeze, partner pressure, jealousy of competing agency's pitch, internal politics, a single bad month they're overweighting?
4. **Once you find the Black Swan, the negotiation is about that — not about the retainer.**
5. **Only then propose** — and propose scope adjustment, not price adjustment.
