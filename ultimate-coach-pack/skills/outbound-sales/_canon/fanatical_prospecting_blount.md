## The operator's worldview

Blount's thesis across the corpus: **pipe is life.** Sales careers don't die because the rep can't sell — they die because the pipe is empty. And the pipe is empty because prospecting is *emotionally hard*, not because it's tactically hard. The dials work. The emails work. The DMs work. Reps just don't make them.

Therefore: the master variable in a sales career is the rep's ability to do uncomfortable things on a daily, calendar-blocked, non-negotiable schedule. Blount calls this **fanatical prospecting**. The "fanatical" is doing the work.

## Framework 1 — The 30-Day Rule (*Fanatical Prospecting*, 2015)

**What it is:** What you put into the top of the pipeline today determines what closes in roughly 90 days. The gap between cause (prospecting activity) and effect (closed revenue) is wide enough that most reps don't connect them — until the cliff arrives.

**Mechanism:**
- Skip prospecting for 30 days, and the impact doesn't show in this quarter. It shows two quarters out.
- By the time the rep feels the pain (empty pipeline → no closes), it's months too late to fix.
- This is why Blount frames prospecting as a **daily, non-negotiable** discipline, not a "when I have time" task.

**DR application:** if outbound stops for any 30-day window, Q+1 starves. Partnership inflow is variable — it cannot be the only oxygen.

---

## Framework 2 — The Law of Replacement (*Fanatical Prospecting*)

**What it is:** For every opportunity that closes, you must replace it in the pipeline with **2-3 new opportunities**, not one. Because not every opportunity closes.

**Math:** if your close rate is 33%, a 1:1 replacement guarantees pipeline shrinkage to zero. A 3:1 replacement keeps the pipe flat. A 4:1 replacement grows it.

**Behavioral implication:** the rep can't celebrate a close until they've refilled. The mental model is "every close puts me one step closer to a dry pipe unless I act *today*."

## Framework 3 — The Prospecting Block (*Fanatical Prospecting*)

**What it is:** dedicated, calendar-defended blocks of pure prospecting activity. Phone + list + dial. Nothing else.

**Rules Blount enforces:**
- **Morning beats afternoon.** Decision-makers answer earlier.
- **Short intense blocks beat marathon blocks.** 60-90 minutes of pure dials produces more conversations than a 4-hour drift.
- **Defended blocks beat optional blocks.** If the block can be moved, it will be moved. Calendar it as immovable.
- **No email, no Slack, no "research" during the block.** All of these are emotional avoidance dressed up as productivity.

**The named failure mode:** "pipe-cleaning." The rep tidies the CRM, researches the account, "preps" the call, updates notes — and never dials. Looks busy. Produces no pipe. Blount sees this constantly.

## Framework 4 — The 5-Step Telephone Prospecting Framework (*Fanatical Prospecting*)

Cold call structure. Under 30 seconds before the ask.

1. **Get their attention.** Use their first name. "John —"
2. **Identify yourself and the company.** Brief. No résumé.
3. **State the reason for the call.** One sentence. Why them, why now.
4. **Bridge.** A short, specific value statement tied to their world. Not a feature list.
5. **Ask for what you want.** A specific micro-commitment. "I'd like 15 minutes Thursday at 2." Not "want to learn more?"

**Why "ask for what you want" is the make-or-break step:** most reps trail off into "thoughts?" or "make sense?" — both of which give the prospect an exit. The rep has to be willing to ask for the calendar slot. That ask is where call reluctance shows up most.

---

## Framework 5 — The 4-Step Email Framework (*Fanatical Prospecting*)

Cold email structure. 75-125 words.

1. **Hook.** First line. Earns the second line. Specific, relevant, them-not-me.
2. **Because.** The reason this email exists today, tied to a real signal about them.
3. **Ask.** One specific micro-commitment. 15-minute call. Reply yes/no. A specific time.
4. **Cap.** A short, low-friction closer. No P.S. about awards or social proof bullets.

**The named failure mode:** the brochure email. Long. Feature-heavy. Three paragraphs about the company. The prospect skims, deletes. Cold email is not a sales letter — it's a door-knock.

## Framework 6 — Sales EQ (Four Dimensions) (*Sales EQ*, 2017)

Blount's argument: technical sales skill is necessary but not sufficient. The reps who win long-term are emotionally regulated. The ones who flame out are not.

The four dimensions:
- **Empathy** — read the prospect's emotional state in real time.
- **Self-awareness** — read your own. Notice your own call reluctance, ego, defensiveness.
- **Self-control** — don't react to rejection, ghosting, or disruption. Stay neutral.
- **Achievement drive** — internal motor that survives the daily grind of the pipe.

The fifth implicit dimension is **delayed gratification** — accepting that today's prospecting pays off in 90 days, and doing it anyway.

## Framework 7 — The Five Emotional Sales Killers (*Sales EQ* + *Fanatical Prospecting*)

The five emotions that destroy sales careers, in Blount's taxonomy:
1. **Fear of rejection.** Avoiding the no.
2. **Call reluctance.** Specific subtype of #1 — the dial-tone fear.
3. **Disruption anxiety.** "I don't want to bother them."
4. **Need to be liked.** Soft-pedaling the ask to preserve the relationship.
5. **Fear of failure.** Avoiding the activity to avoid measurable proof of failure.

**Operator-level use:** when a rep (or Ivan) is dodging the phone, the prescription isn't "try harder." It's name the emotion that's driving the dodge.

## Framework 8 — The Objection Taxonomy (*Objections*, 2018)

Blount's central thesis: three different objections, three different handlings. Most reps blur them and respond to all three the same way. That's the bug.

**Prospecting objections** (at the interruption stage):
- "Not interested." "Send me an email." "We're already with someone."
- Handling: **ledge + disrupt + re-anchor.** Acknowledge, then disrupt the pattern, then re-anchor with a different micro-commitment. The goal is NOT to convert on the call. It's to keep the line open.

**Micro-commitment objections** (mid-conversation, advancing to next step):
- "Send me info first." "I'm busy right now."
- Handling: **agree, then advance with a smaller ask.** Move the commitment down a notch but keep momentum.

**Buying objections** (at the close):
- "It's too expensive." "I need to think about it." "I need to talk to my partner."
- Handling: **diagnose what's underneath.** Surface objection is almost never the real one. /voss tactical empathy applies here.

**The named bug:** treating a prospecting objection like a buying objection. You can't "overcome" a prospecting objection by pitching value — they haven't bought yet. You're trying to get a foot in the door, not close.

## Framework 9 — Virtual Selling Adjustments (*Virtual Selling*, 2020)

Adaptations for video/remote selling:
- **Video on, always.** Camera-off prospects buy less. Mirror what you want them to do.
- **Pre-call set design.** Light, frame, background. Treat the call like a stage.
- **Engagement intervals shorter.** Virtual attention spans halve. Re-engage every 5-7 minutes with a question or a screen-share moment.
- **The "psychological close distance"** — virtual feels far. Compensate with more direct asks, more named labels, more silence.

---

## Framework 10 — Inked (*Inked*, 2020) — The Closing Layer

Blount's closing book. The frameworks are mostly about *negotiation under sales conditions*, so they sit at the seam where /sales hands off to /voss. The most operator-relevant pieces:
- **Buying signals are physical, not verbal.** Watch posture, pace, pen-pickup, screen-lean.
- **Trial closes** before the real close — small commitment requests that surface objection layers before the price conversation.
- **Pricing pushback at the close** is a *buying* objection, not a prospecting one. Don't ledge it. Diagnose it. (For deep handling — /voss.)
