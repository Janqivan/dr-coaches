# Entrepreneur Revolution — Framework Map

**Author:** Daniel Priestley
**Book:** *Entrepreneur Revolution: How to Develop Your Entrepreneurial Mindset and Start a Business That Works* (Capstone, 2013; revised 2018)
**Source treatment:** Framework structures described in own words for synthetic-coach use. Book text is NOT reproduced. Each framework is named, mapped to the section of the book where it lives, and described mechanically so the AIPriestley skill can apply it. To read Priestley's prose — including the cultural-historical thesis on the post-industrial economy, the founder stories, and the practical worksheets — get the book.

---

## Why this book exists (the operator's view)

*Entrepreneur Revolution* is Priestley's first major book and the worldview foundation for everything that follows. Written in 2013 (revised 2018), the thesis is that the industrial-age economy — built around large corporations, lifelong employment, and standardized careers — is structurally giving way to an entrepreneur-led economy of small, global, asset-light, technology-leveraged ventures. The book makes two claims: (1) this transition is irreversible, and (2) individuals who develop entrepreneur-character traits and follow a specific 9-step path can capture disproportionate value during the transition.

This book is "why" — *Key Person of Influence*, *Oversubscribed*, and *24 Assets* are the "how". Without the worldview installed by *Entrepreneur Revolution*, the later books read as marketing tactics. With it, they read as the operating manual for a specific historical moment.

Position in the Priestley sequence: **Entrepreneur Revolution (worldview)** → *Key Person of Influence* (authority engine) → *Oversubscribed* (demand engine) → *24 Assets* (scale engine).

## Framework 1 — Industrial-Age vs Entrepreneur-Age Thesis

**What it is:** Priestley's macro frame for why entrepreneurship has become structurally easier and more valuable this century.

**The shift:**

| Industrial age (pre-2000) | Entrepreneur age (post-2000) |
|---------------------------|------------------------------|
| Capital-heavy | Capital-light |
| Centralized infrastructure | Distributed cloud/platform |
| Mass production | Niche micro-markets |
| Local audience | Global audience |
| Big company = safer | Big company = exposed |
| 40 years one employer | Portfolio of ventures |

**Operator implication:** the rules an operator (or their parents) learned about safety are calibrated to a world that is ending. "Go get a safe job" is structurally backwards in 2026. The safer move is to own assets that produce income independent of any single employer.

**Diagnostic principle:** when instincts feel "safe but stuck", the operator is usually optimizing for industrial-age incentives in an entrepreneur-age economy. Change the frame, not the tactic.

## Framework 2 — The Entrepreneur Character vs the Employee Character

**What it is:** Priestley's behavioural and psychological contrast between the two operating modes. Not a moral judgment — a functional description of what each mode optimizes for.

**The employee character:**
- Trades time for money.
- Looks upward for permission and direction.
- Defines success as climbing the ladder of someone else's company.
- Risk-averse on capital, risk-tolerant on time (will spend 40 years on a single bet).
- Identity tied to title and employer.
- Reward delayed (promotion, pension).

**The entrepreneur character:**
- Trades risk for asymmetric reward.
- Looks outward for problems to solve.
- Defines success as building assets that produce value without their continuous input.
- Risk-tolerant on capital, risk-averse on time (will not spend 40 years on the wrong bet).
- Identity tied to creation and impact.
- Reward variable but uncapped.

**Priestley's claim:** the two characters are learnable, not innate. Most adults are operating in default-employee mode because schooling and early career trained them into it. Switching modes is mostly a frame-shift, supported by a structured path (Framework 3).

**Diagnostic principle:** when an operator describes themselves as "stuck", check which character they are running in this specific decision. Operators often switch into employee-character around major risk decisions even when they have run entrepreneur-character for years.

## Framework 3 — The 9-Step Entrepreneur Path

**What it is:** Priestley's structured sequence from "I want to start something" to "I run a small giant that doesn't need me". The path is sequential — skipping steps causes predictable failure.

**The nine steps:**

1. **Find your tribe.** Identify the specific community / niche you want to serve. Not everyone — a defined group. This is the ICP precursor to *Oversubscribed*.
2. **Spot a real opportunity.** Find a problem in that tribe that is painful, frequent, and under-served. This is Framework 4 below.
3. **Develop your offer.** Build the first version of the product or service that solves the problem. Minimum viable but real.
4. **Build your micro-niche brand.** Stand for something specific in the tribe. The lighthouse signal from *KPI*.
5. **Become a Key Person of Influence in your niche.** This is where the *KPI 5 P's* sequence enters. Pitch, Publish, Product, Profile, Partnerships — within the chosen niche.
6. **Get oversubscribed.** Use *Oversubscribed* mechanics to engineer demand > supply for the niche offer.
7. **Build the 24 assets.** Layer the asset stack so the business detaches from the founder's hours.
8. **Become a small giant.** Operate as a 7-8 figure business on lean headcount, premium pricing, structurally oversubscribed.
9. **Scale, sell, or repeat.** Either scale to a larger small giant, sell the asset, or use the now-mature small giant as the platform for the next venture (the Vitruvian Entrepreneur stage).

**Diagnostic principle:** when an operator is stuck, find the lowest unfinished step. Most operators jump to step 5 or 6 (visibility, launches) without completing steps 1-3 (tribe, opportunity, offer). The bottleneck is almost always earlier than they think.

**DR mapping:** DR is at step 6-7 — has tribe (immigration law, franchise, vocational), has KPI authority (NO BS), is running campaigns, is building the 24 assets. The next move is structural completion of steps 6-7 to reach small giant (step 8).

## Framework 4 — How to Spot a Real Opportunity

**What it is:** Priestley's filter for distinguishing a real entrepreneurial opportunity from a vanity idea or a hobby.

**The five filters of a real opportunity:**

1. **Is it a real problem?** Does someone actively suffer from it, with consequences they can articulate? (Not "wouldn't it be cool if...")
2. **Is the problem frequent?** Does it occur often enough that solving it generates a repeating sale, or a referrable pain? One-off problems make weak businesses.
3. **Is it expensive?** Does the customer currently pay for the problem in money, time, or stress? If the problem is free, the solution will not be paid for.
4. **Is the buyer reachable?** Can you identify, find, and communicate with the people who have the problem? An invisible buyer is not a buyer.
5. **Can you solve it disproportionately well?** Do you have unfair access — expertise, network, IP, location, history — that lets you solve the problem better than alternatives?

**Diagnostic principle:** a real opportunity scores 4-5 out of 5 on these filters. A vanity idea scores 1-2. Most failed startups fail filter 1 (the problem was not real) or filter 4 (the buyer was not reachable at viable cost).

**Failure mode:** operators talk themselves into low-scoring opportunities by emotional attachment. The filter is unemotional on purpose.

## Framework 5 — The Global Small Business Thesis

**What it is:** Priestley's frame for the optimal business shape in the entrepreneur age. Not big, not local — small AND global.

**The core insight:** the internet collapsed the geographical constraint on niche businesses. A niche that was too small to support a local business (e.g. "immigration marketing for EB-5 law firms") becomes economically viable when the addressable market is global.

**Characteristics of a global small business:**
- Niche: narrow enough to be authoritative.
- Global: distribution is not geographically bounded.
- Digital-first: delivery, marketing, and operations primarily online.
- Lean: headcount under 30, often under 10.
- Premium: pricing matches authority position, not local-market average.
- Asset-leveraged: revenue scales without proportional headcount (the *24 Assets* setup).

**Implication for niching decisions:** an operator who feels they cannot niche because the local market is too small is solving the wrong problem. The fix is not to broaden the niche — it is to broaden the geography. Niche down + globe up.

**DR mapping:** DR's vertical strategy (immigration / franchise / vocational) is the global-small-business thesis applied. Each vertical is narrow enough for authority, broad enough geographically to support 7-8 figure scale.

## Framework 6 — The Small Giants Thesis (precursor to 24 Assets)

**What it is:** Priestley's argument that the optimal business size is not maximally large, but a "small giant" — high profit per employee, premium positioned, oversubscribed, founder-led without being founder-dependent.

**Why small giants beat scale-at-all-costs businesses:**
- Profit per employee 5-10x industry average.
- Decisions made faster (no committees, no consultants).
- Culture coherent (small enough to know everyone).
- Pricing power preserved (oversubscription holds).
- Founder retains meaningful equity (no dilution treadmill).
- Sellable at higher multiples (lean = clean).
- Lifestyle compatible (the founder has a life).

**Why most operators do not aim for small giant:**
- Cultural conditioning ("bigger = better").
- Vanity metrics (headcount as success).
- Misunderstanding of valuation (revenue ≠ EV; profit per employee drives EV).
- Lack of an alternative model (the unicorn playbook is loud; small giants are quiet).

**Diagnostic principle:** when an operator says "we need to scale", probe whether they mean revenue, headcount, or impact. Often they mean "I want more impact" but assume headcount is the path. The path may be more leverage (the 24 assets), not more people.

**DR's small-giant target shape (likely):** 15-25 staff, $5-15M revenue, $2-5M profit, founder-led but not founder-dependent in delivery, structurally oversubscribed, recognized authority in 3 verticals.

## Framework 7 — The Entrepreneur Seasons (cross-referenced with KPI canon)

**What it is:** Priestley's seasonality frame for the operator's year. Treated in detail in `key_person_of_influence_frameworks.md` Framework 10 — referenced here because the seasons concept originates in *Entrepreneur Revolution*.

**The four seasons, briefly:**
- **Spring** — growth, launch, expand.
- **Summer** — deliver, harvest, monetize.
- **Autumn** — review, audit, sunset.
- **Winter** — build, IP, strategy.

**Use:** map the operator's quarter into a season. The wrong activity in the wrong season is the most common founder energy leak — building IP in summer (when delivery should be peaking), trying to launch in winter (when no one is buying), etc.

## Framework 8 — Four Levels of Value Creation (the value ladder)

**What it is:** Priestley's hierarchy of how an operator creates value — and how compensation scales with each level. Roughly aligned to the 5-stage business ladder (`key_person_of_influence_frameworks.md` Framework 2) but framed by value-creation type rather than business stage.

**The four levels:**

1. **Labour value.** Trading time for money. Hourly worker, contractor billing hours. Compensation capped at hours × rate.
2. **Service value.** Trading expertise applied to specific customer problems. Consultant, agency, professional services. Compensation tied to outcomes and expertise, but still capped by time.
3. **Product value.** Building products / IP / methods that can be sold many times without being made many times. Software, books, courses, frameworks. Compensation decouples from time.
4. **Asset value.** Owning assets (the 24) that produce value independently. Equity, licenses, recurring revenue, brand assets. Compensation is structural, not transactional.

**Diagnostic principle:** the financial outcome of an operator's year is largely determined by which level they are operating at. Moving up one level often produces a 5-10x compensation increase. Moving up two levels is a step-change in life shape.

**Failure mode:** operators try to grind harder at the level they are on, when the unlock is moving up a level. Working twice as hard at Level 2 produces 2x. Moving to Level 3 produces 10x.

**DR mapping:** DR is between Level 2 (service value — agency retainer) and Level 3 (product value — productized Case Flow, NO BS content as IP). The strategic move is structural Level 3.

## How to use this file inside the /priestley skill

1. **Mindset / frame / "should I" → load *Entrepreneur Revolution*.** Worldview-level. Tactical questions go to KPI, *Oversubscribed*, or *24 Assets*.
2. **Framework 1 to reframe risk.** When an operator hesitates citing safety, surface the industrial-vs-entrepreneur frame. The "safe" instinct is usually outdated.
3. **Framework 3 (9-step path) as the master map.** Position the operator on the path. The next step is almost always one earlier than they think.
4. **Framework 4 (real-opportunity filter) before any new venture or product line.** Score 1-5 on each filter; low scores get killed early.
5. **Framework 5 (global small business) when operator wants to broaden.** Broaden geography (usually right), not the niche (usually wrong).
6. **Framework 6 (small giant thesis) as the target shape.** Gives operators a concrete target for "successful".
7. **Framework 8 (value-creation levels) for compensation diagnosis.** "Working harder but not earning more" = level-locked. Prescribe the level move.
8. **Never substitute these summaries for the book.** For Priestley's prose, the historical-cultural argument, and founder stories, point at *Entrepreneur Revolution* (Capstone, 2013/2018) and the Dent Global YouTube channel.
