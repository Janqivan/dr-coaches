# Secret Tradecraft of Elite Advisors — Framework Map

Synthesis of David C. Baker's 2022 book "Secret Tradecraft of Elite Advisors:
Covert Techniques for a Remarkable Practice," reconstructed from his public
corpus (2Bobs episodes, Punctuation articles, LinkedIn posts, public
interviews). Frameworks expressed in own words. No book text reproduced.

This is the operating-an-expert-practice canon. If the incoming question is
about financial benchmarks, client portfolio surgery, pricing without hours,
new business mechanics, sellability, or the day-to-day discipline of running
an elite advisory firm — this is the file.

---

## 1. The Seven Traits Always True of an Expert

Baker's checklist for whether someone (or a firm) is actually operating as
an expert vs. claiming to be one.

1. **Point of view.** Has an actual opinion on the field, not a neutral
   summary. Will say which approaches are wrong, by name.
2. **Concise.** Can compress complex insight into a sentence. Long answers
   are usually a tell of shallow expertise.
3. **Believable.** The voice is calm because the evidence is dense — they
   have seen this 200 times.
4. **Handles follow-up without choking.** The second and third question deep
   is where pretenders break.
5. **Confident.** Not arrogant. Not hedging. Comfortable with "I don't know,
   here is how I would find out."
6. **Holds principles loosely.** Knows their current model is provisional,
   updates when the evidence shifts.
7. **Treats the "how" as equal to the "what".** Process discipline is part
   of the substance, not separate from it.

Operational use: when hiring senior people, evaluating a partner, or
auditing your own firm, run the seven. Most "expert" firms fail on 2, 4,
and 7.

## 2. The Eight-Gauge Financial Dashboard

Baker's stated minimum instrumentation. If a principal cannot answer all
eight, the diagnosis is "instrument first, decide second."

1. **AGI per FTE** (Agency Gross Income per Full-Time Equivalent).
   Baker's stated benchmarks: ~$160–180K ordinary, $200K good, $220K
   excellent, $275K+ elite. AGI = revenue minus pass-through cost of goods
   sold (3rd-party media, contractors-tied-to-projects, etc.), not minus
   salaries.

2. **Effective Hourly Rate (EHR).** What you actually realize per billable
   hour, regardless of how you bill. Baker's range: $160–$200 is typical;
   elite firms push $250+. Effective is what hit the bank, not what was
   quoted.

3. **Utilization.** ~60% of full-time-staff hours should be billable. Below
   55% the firm bleeds. Above 70% the firm is burning out. If they don't
   measure it, they don't manage it.

4. **Operating profit before principal compensation.** Target 20%+ on AGI.
   This is the gauge that separates "lifestyle business with a payroll"
   from "actual firm."

5. **Client concentration.** Largest client <25% of AGI (15% safer).
   Top three <50%. Above that, the firm has a single point of failure.

6. **Facility cost** (~6% of AGI is the benchmark). Anything above 10% and
   the office is eating margin.

7. **New business hit rate.** Close rate on qualified opportunities. If
   below 25%, the qualification is broken or the pitch is. If above 60%,
   the firm is undercharging or under-saying-no.

8. **% of clients fired in trailing 12 months.** Should be 5–15%. Zero
   means demand-constrained and emotionally attached. Above 25% means the
   intake filter is broken.

References:
- "Eight Important Gauges On Your Financial Dashboard," https://punctuation.com/eight-important-gauges-on-your-financial-dashboard/
- "Transcending Timesheets," https://2bobs.com/podcast/transcending-timesheets

## 3. Why Clients Come, Stay, and Leave — Three Different Questions

Most principals collapse these. They are different mechanisms.

- **Why they come:** demonstrated expertise + referral path + findability
  in the specialization. Marketing's job.
- **Why they stay:** delivery quality + relationship continuity + perceived
  forward value. Account management's job, but only after the work is
  competent.
- **Why they leave:** rarely the work. Usually relationship attrition,
  principal disengagement, internal client politics, or they outgrew you.

Operational use: map each existing client across the three columns. If
patterns emerge in why they leave, fix the upstream cause once, not the
symptom 30 times.

Reference: "Distinguishing Between Why Clients Come, Stay, and Leave" on
2Bobs (search by title at https://2bobs.com/podcast).

## 4. Innoficiency — The Innovation/Efficiency Tradeoff

Coined by Blair Enns, dissected with Baker. Innovation and efficiency are
opposed forces, not complementary ones.

- **Innovative firms** tolerate waste. Failed experiments are the price of
  the breakthroughs. Pricing must capture upside, because half the work
  produces no shippable result.
- **Efficient firms** tolerate sameness. Repeat the playbook, drive the
  cost down each cycle. Pricing is closer to commodity, margin comes from
  operational discipline.

You cannot maximize both. Most agencies claim "innovative, original
thinking" in the pitch and run a checklist operation in delivery — that
gap is where margin disappears. Pick the lane, structure the firm to
match.

Reference: "Innoficiency in Your Agency,"
https://2bobs.com/podcast/innoficiency-in-your-agency

## 5. Pricing Without Hours — The Five Moves

Baker's stated stance: hourly billing is a tax on the principal's
sophistication. Five sequenced moves to get off it.

1. **Stop revealing the hourly rate.** Internally you have one for
   estimating. Externally, clients only see fixed fees for defined scope.
2. **Front door is a paid diagnostic.** Fixed fee, written deliverable.
   Conversion to implementation is qualified, not sold.
3. **Project work is fixed-fee with named scope.** Out-of-scope triggers
   a change order, not absorption.
4. **Retainers only when there is a real recurring outcome.** Otherwise
   they train the client to pay for presence, not value (Baker's
   "9 Ways Recurring Revenue Hurts Your Business").
5. **Annual repricing.** Every renewal moves up 10–25% on the relationships
   that have grown in scope or risk. The ones that resist are the next
   firing candidates.

References:
- "Never Reveal Your Hourly Rate," https://punctuation.com/never-reveal-your-hourly-rate/
- "Different Pricing Models," https://2bobs.com/podcast/different-pricing-models
- "9 Ways Recurring Revenue Hurts Your Business" (interview), https://creative-boost.com/recurring-revenue-david-c-baker/

## 6. The Account-People-Close-New-Business Doctrine

Conventional wisdom: principal closes new business or you hire a head of
sales. Baker's contrarian read: the account managers already running the
existing relationships should close most new business, because:

- They understand the work substantively.
- The prospect can smell sales from a salesperson; cannot from an operator.
- It forces the firm to train account managers as commercial owners, not
  ticket-shufflers — which is what produces the next layer of partners.

The principal stops being the only closer. The firm gets sellable. The
head-of-sales hire is usually a way to avoid this harder cultural shift.

Reference: "Why Account People Should Close New Business,"
https://2bobs.com/podcast/why-account-people-should-close-new-business

## 7. The More-Opportunity-Than-Capacity Rule

The core supply/demand position of an elite firm.

You must engineer more qualified opportunity than you have capacity to
serve. The delta is where pricing power, client selection, and saying no
all come from. Without it, every "should we take this" question is
contaminated by cash anxiety.

Operationally:
- Pipeline coverage targeting 3–4x capacity.
- Saying no to 30–50% of qualified opportunities is normal.
- Caring less about winning the business than the prospect cares about
  winning you is the only stable seller's posture.

This is also the inversion: if the principal is "chasing leads," there is
no leverage, regardless of how strong the firm seems. The math is doing
something else.

## 8. Owner Dependence — The Sellability Gauge

The single biggest predictor of whether the firm will sell at a real
multiple, per Baker's M&A advisory work.

Three measures, all uncomfortable:
1. **% of new business closed by the principal.** Above 50% and the firm
   has no commercial engine without you.
2. **% of client work the principal personally delivers.** Above 25% and
   you are a freelancer with employees.
3. **Days the principal is unreachable per year without revenue impact.**
   If you can't be gone for 30 consecutive days without things degrading,
   you have not built a firm.

The pre-sale work is reducing all three to acceptable ranges over 18–36
months before a transaction. Trying to sell before the work is done is
why most agencies sell for 3–5x EBITDA instead of 6–8x.

- "Valuing and Selling Your Firm," https://2bobs.com/podcast/valuing-and-selling-your-firm

## 9. The "If I Started A New Firm Now" Playbook

Baker's public list of what he would design in from day one if launching
today. Use as a retrofit checklist.

- Pick the specialization on day one. Vertical or horizontal, but pick.
- Launch with a written newsletter. Not optional.
- Build the firm around 8–15 clients at high fees, not 40–60 at mediocre
  fees.
- Use paid diagnostic as the only entry point.
- Hire generalists at junior, specialists at senior — not the reverse.
- Do not take office space until it is forced.
- Do not hire a head of sales before account managers can close.
- Build proprietary IP from year one — frameworks, assessments, scorecards.
- Newsletter > LinkedIn > podcast > conferences > paid ads, in that order
  for the expertise stack.

Reference: "If I Started A New Firm, Now," on 2Bobs (search at
https://2bobs.com/podcast).

## 10. The Firing-A-Client Mechanic

How to actually do it (not just the principle that you should).

- **Trigger.** Behavior toward your team, scope creep without repricing,
  payment lag, or strategic misfit emerging at renewal.
- **Conversation.** In person or on video. "We have realized we are no
  longer the right firm for what you need." Not "we are pivoting." Not
  "we are at capacity." Direct.
- **Transition.** 30–90 days, with named handover deliverables and a
  short list of better-fit firms. Reputation matters more than the last
  invoice.
- **Internal signal.** Tell the team. Firing a bad-fit client is the
  strongest culture signal a principal can send — stronger than any
  values poster.

References:
- "Firing a Client," https://2bobs.com/podcast/firing-a-client
- "Things Principals Should Do More Of," https://2bobs.com/podcast/things-principals-should-do-more-of
