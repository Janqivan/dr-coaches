# Wiebe's Conversion Copywriting — The 3-Part Process

Source canon: the Copyhackers blog (2011-present), Copy School curriculum,
10x Landing Pages / 10x Sales Pages / 10x Emails, Tutorial Tuesdays library,
Wiebe's conference talks (CTAConf, MozCon, INBOUND, Business of Software,
CXL Live), and *The Copyselling System* (Simon & Schuster, July 2026).

Conversion copywriting is the discipline of moving the reader to "yes" with
research-led, testable, accountable-to-a-conversion-rate copy. It's a cousin
of direct response, but it's not the same thing — direct response is
mail-and-letter-shop heritage; conversion copywriting is web-native,
analytics-native, A/B-test-native. Wiebe coined the term in 2011 and built
Copyhackers around it.

The process is three parts. Most of the work is upstream.

---

## PART 1 — RESEARCH (60-80% of the work)

The single most distinctive thing about conversion copywriting vs. every
other school of copywriting: research is the majority of the job.

If you skip research, you guess. Guessing on a landing page is expensive —
the bad headline doesn't just cost you the conversion, it costs you the ad
click that paid to bring the visitor.

### What research is actually for

Research answers four questions, in this order:
1. **Who is the One Reader?** (segment, role, awareness stage, trigger event,
   stakes)
2. **What pain or desire is acute enough to make them act?** (verbatim, not
   paraphrased)
3. **What objections will fire when they hit your page?** (verbatim)
4. **What language do they use to describe the problem, the solution, and
   the desired outcome?** (verbatim)

If you can't answer all four with real quotes from real people, you're not
ready to write copy.

### The sources, ranked by yield

**Tier 1 — sales / customer call recordings**
Highest yield, lowest effort. Gong, Fathom, Otter, Loom recordings of:
- Sales discovery calls (objections + trigger events)
- Onboarding calls (desired outcomes)
- Cancellation calls (failed promises, gap between sales and product)
- QBRs (what they're actually getting value from)

Pull the verbatim. The first ten calls usually give you the headline.

**Tier 2 — public reviews**
- **G2, Capterra, TrustRadius** — B2B SaaS. Mine 4-star reviews (3-star
  are sometimes broken users; 5-star are sometimes fanboys; 4-star
  reviewers describe both pain and outcome with the most precision).
- **Amazon** — for products with an adjacent book or product. The famous
  Wiebe technique: pick a book or product that solves the same problem
  your offer solves, mine the reviews.
- **TrustPilot, Yelp, Google reviews** — local service businesses,
  e-commerce.
- **Reddit** — search subreddits where your One Reader hangs out for
  the problem in their words. The advantage: Reddit users explain
  themselves at length. Mine threads, not just titles.

**Tier 3 — internal sources**
- **Support tickets** — recurring problems, recurring objections.
- **Exit surveys** — why they left, what they expected vs. got.
- **On-page polls** — Hotjar / Survicate exit-intent or 5-minute polls.
- **Sales follow-up emails** — what the rep said to close the deal, what
  the prospect said back.

**Tier 4 — prospect surveys**
The slowest. Useful for net-new offers with no customers yet. Caveat:
prospects describe what they think they want, not what they buy. Trust
behavior data over stated preference.

### The Amazon Review Mining shortcut (Wiebe's signature technique)

Used when your offer doesn't have enough reviews yet, or when you want to
mine adjacent solutions.

1. Identify a book or product on Amazon that solves the same problem your
   offer solves.
2. Use Google to filter Amazon reviews for emotional triggers. Example
   search queries:
   - `site:amazon.com inurl:"product-reviews" "tired of" [keyword]`
   - `site:amazon.com inurl:"product-reviews" "finally" [keyword]`
   - `site:amazon.com inurl:"product-reviews" "I wish" [keyword]`
   - `site:amazon.com inurl:"product-reviews" "the worst" [keyword]`
3. The phrases `tired of`, `finally`, `I wish`, `the worst`, `for once`,
   `actually` reliably surface pain + transformation in customer language.
4. Pull 30-100 quotes into a spreadsheet.
5. Organize by theme.

Wiebe's famous case study: she used Amazon review mining for a Florida rehab
center (a fully service-based business — no product on Amazon at all). The
technique works because addiction-and-recovery memoirs on Amazon contained
verbatim pain language she could lift directly into the rehab center's
landing page. The headline came from the reviews. Conversions jumped.

### The Google Review Mining shortcut

For local businesses and consumer apps. Same principle: filter for emotional
phrases, pull verbatim. Faster than Amazon for service businesses with local
competitors.

### How to organize the spreadsheet

Columns:
| Verbatim quote | Source (URL or call ID) | Theme | Stage of awareness |
Pain / Desire / Objection / Transformation | Notes |

Themes emerge as you tag. Don't pre-decide the themes. Let them surface.

A good VoC spreadsheet has 50-300 rows. Below 50, you're guessing. Above
300, you're procrastinating.

### What you do with the spreadsheet

- **Headlines** come from pain + desire quotes that show up repeatedly.
- **Subheads** come from objections handled in customer language.
- **Bullets** come from transformation quotes ("I used to X, now I Y").
- **CTAs** come from action language the customer already uses.
- **Body proof** comes from before/after stories in the quotes themselves.

The page writes itself if the spreadsheet is right.

## PART 2 — WRITE / WIREFRAME (15-30% of the work)

You don't write a landing page front-to-back. You wireframe it, then fill
it in.

### The wireframe order (landing page / sales page)

1. **Hero** — the four questions in order: what is it / what does it do
   for me / why believe it / what do I do next.
2. **Above-the-fold proof bar** — logos, ratings, social proof anchor.
3. **Problem agitation** — PAS or BAB depending on awareness stage.
4. **Solution introduction** — your offer, named, with the mechanism
   that makes it work.
5. **Features → advantages → benefits (FAB)** translated into copy.
6. **Proof stack** — testimonials, case studies, stats, data, demo.
7. **Objection handling** — the top 3-5 objections, answered in the
   customer's language.
8. **Offer + risk reversal** — what they get, what it costs, what the
   guarantee is.
9. **CTA** — repeated, with button copy that previews the next step.
10. **FAQ** — the residual objections.
11. **Final CTA + risk reversal restated.**
12. **P.S.** (long-form only) — restated offer + biggest objection
    flipped + urgency / deadline.

This is the architecture. The formulas (PAS, BAB, FAB) fit inside it.

### PAS — Problem-Agitate-Solve (deep dive)

Wiebe's most-used formula. PAS pre-dates Wiebe by decades (Robert Collier
era), but she popularized it for the web.

**Problem:** name the pain in the reader's exact words. NOT "perspiration
concerns." Yes — "sweating through your shirt at your nephew's wedding."
The verbatim phrase comes from VoC research.

**Agitate:** push the pain so the reader feels it. List the consequences.
List the scenarios. List the things they avoid because of the pain.
Wiebe's example for SweatBlock (antiperspirant): they didn't just say
"you sweat." They listed:
- not liking to hug
- wearing layers even in heat
- only wearing black
- avoiding handshakes
- not raising your hand in meetings

That's agitation. Most marketers skip agitation because it feels
"pushy." Wiebe's frame: if the agitation is rooted in real VoC, it's
not pushy, it's empathetic. The reader is RELIEVED that someone finally
named what they've been quietly avoiding.

**Solve:** introduce your offer as the resolution. Not the feature list.
The resolution to the specific pain you just agitated.

PAS works best for problem-aware and solution-aware readers. Doesn't work
for unaware readers (no pain to name yet) or most-aware readers (already
convinced).

### PAS-on-Steroids — Wiebe's extension (Why / Try / Buy)

The classic PAS doesn't fully close on a high-ticket offer. Wiebe's
extension for long-form sales pages:

- **Why** — why the reader has the problem (mechanism of the problem).
- **Try** — what they've tried, and why it failed (anti-objection layer).
- **Buy** — why your offer succeeds where the alternatives failed
  (mechanism of the solution), plus the close.

The Why and Try layers are where you handle the "I've tried that before"
objection without ever letting the reader voice it.

### BAB — Before-After-Bridge (Wiebe-coined)

Best for: visual or lifestyle transformations, solution-aware readers,
emails (short-form).

**Before:** describe the current pain or chaos. Specific, sensory.
**After:** describe the future state. Equally specific, sensory.
**Bridge:** your offer is the bridge between the two.

Example Wiebe uses (spice rack):
- Before: "Your spice rack is a chaotic mess and you can never find the
  paprika when you need it."
- After: "Every spice perfectly organized in labeled, airtight jars."
- Bridge: "Our magnetic spice jars attach to any metal surface, keeping
  everything visible and within reach."

BAB is gentler than PAS — there's no agitation step. Use BAB when the
pain is mild but the desire is clear. Use PAS when the pain is acute.

### FAB — Features-Advantages-Benefits (pre-dates Wiebe, she uses it)

For each feature, name the advantage it creates, then the benefit (in the
reader's life).

| Feature | Advantage | Benefit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 256-bit encryption | Bank-grade security | Your client data stays private — no breach calls, no embarrassed emails to the board |
| 24/7 support | Always-on help | The 2am production outage gets fixed before your CEO sees it |
| Drag-and-drop builder | No code required | Your marketing team ships landing pages without waiting on dev |

Caveat from Wiebe: benefit-only copy with no feature anchor is
unbelievable. ("Save time and stress less!" — nobody believes that
without the feature underneath.) Pair them.

### The 4 U's headline stress test (Michael Masterson, AWAI — Wiebe uses)

Run every headline through:
1. **Useful** — does it promise something the reader actually wants?
2. **Urgent** — is there a reason to act now, not someday?
3. **Unique** — does it sound different from competitor headlines?
4. **Ultra-specific** — does it name numbers, names, places, dates?

Two out of four is mediocre. Three out of four is good. Four out of four
is rare and worth shipping. Attribute correctly — Masterson, not Wiebe.

### The button copy rule

Buttons are the conversion site. "Submit" is malpractice. The button
previews the next step:
- "Get my free audit" (value-first)
- "Start my 14-day trial" (specific, low-stakes)
- "Send me the pricing" (relevant, low-friction)
- "Book my strategy call" (next step is clear)

Wiebe's experiments: changing the button from "action language" to "value
language" reliably moves conversion rate 5-30% on B2B SaaS pages.

## PART 3 — TEST (the part everyone skips)

A page that didn't get tested is opinion, not conversion copywriting.

### The test stack

- **A/B test** — for high-traffic pages with statistical power. Tool:
  Convert, Optimizely, VWO, or whatever your CRO team uses.
- **Ship-and-watch** — for low-traffic pages without statistical power.
  Push the new copy live, watch the conversion rate over 30-90 days.
- **5-second test** — for hero clarity. Show the hero for 5 seconds, ask
  the visitor what the page is offering. If they can't say, the hero is
  broken.
- **Wynter-style ICP message test** — show three message variants to
  100-200 ICP buyers, get qualitative + quantitative response on which
  resonates. Better than A/B for pages with low traffic. Peep Laja's
  Wynter is the tool of record here.
- **Heatmap + scroll map** — Hotjar / Microsoft Clarity. Where do
  visitors scroll to? Where do they drop off? Where do they click
  things that aren't buttons (signal of confusion)?

### What to test, in priority order

1. **Hero headline** — biggest leverage, fastest signal.
2. **Hero subhead** — second-biggest leverage.
3. **Primary CTA button copy** — fastest test, highest yield for the
   effort.
4. **Offer construction** — pricing display, anchor, guarantee.
5. **Social proof anchor** — logo bar vs. star rating vs. customer count.
6. **Page length** — short vs. long for the same offer.
7. **Form length** — fewer fields almost always beats more, but not
   always.

Don't test buttons until the headline is fixed. Don't test page length
until the offer is fixed. Order matters.

### What "winning" actually means

A 5-10% lift on a moderate-traffic page is real. A 50% lift on a
low-traffic page is probably noise — wait for more data. A test that
needs three months to reach significance isn't a test, it's a fishing
expedition.

If the lift is real, ship the winner, then iterate on the next thing.
Don't ship the winner and stop. Conversion copywriting is iterative.

## VOICE-OF-CUSTOMER MINING — PROCESS DEEP DIVE

Because VoC mining is the single highest-leverage skill in conversion
copywriting, it deserves its own protocol.

### Step 1 — Choose your One Reader

Before you mine, decide who you're mining for. A landing page for
"product-aware mid-market SaaS buyers" is mined differently than a
landing page for "problem-aware solopreneurs."

If you skip this, you'll end up with a spreadsheet that pleases nobody
because it's averaged across all readers.

### Step 2 — Pick three sources, minimum

Always cross-source. One source = bias. Three sources = signal.

For a B2B SaaS landing page targeting product-aware mid-market buyers:
- Source 1: G2 4-star reviews of your product (or closest competitor)
- Source 2: Sales discovery call recordings (last 20)
- Source 3: Cancellation survey + cancellation call recordings

### Step 3 — Pull the verbatim, don't paraphrase

Copy / paste the exact phrasing. Even the typos. The exact words are the
product — paraphrasing strips out the texture that makes the page sound
human.

### Step 4 — Tag every quote

Theme (pain / desire / objection / trigger / transformation), stage of
awareness, role / segment, source.

### Step 5 — Cluster

Read the spreadsheet end-to-end after 50 quotes. Themes will repeat.
The repeated themes are your message hierarchy.

### Step 6 — Lift the headline

The headline is almost always a verbatim or near-verbatim of the
most-repeated pain quote OR the most-repeated desired-outcome quote.
You don't write it. You find it.

### Step 7 — Lift the subhead

The subhead handles the biggest objection in the customer's words.

### Step 8 — Lift the bullets

The bullets translate features into the customer's transformation
language.

### Step 9 — Lift the proof stack

The proof stack pulls the highest-impact customer quotes verbatim. Yes,
verbatim. Don't clean them up. Don't make them sound corporate.

## THE OUTPUT

After the 3-part process, you have:
- A VoC spreadsheet (50-300+ verbatim quotes, tagged)
- A wireframe of the page, section by section
- Each section uses a named formula (PAS, BAB, FAB) with the formula
  inputs lifted from the VoC spreadsheet
- A test plan (control vs. variant, metric, sample size, success criteria)
- The headline, subhead, hero CTA, and primary objection-handling lines
  written and rationalized against specific VoC quotes

That's conversion copywriting. That's the work.

Everything else — polish, "tone," "brand voice," cleverness — is the
optional final pass. The page already converts before you polish it,
because the research carried it. Polishing without research is rearranging
deck chairs.

---

## STANDING RULES (operator vocabulary, Wiebe canon)

- Research is the work. Writing is the visible part.
- Don't write copy. Find it.
- Specificity beats cleverness.
- Evidence beats adjectives.
- Plain language beats fancy talk.
- The customer already wrote your headline.
- A page that didn't get tested is opinion.
- Polishing kills conversion. Sweep instead.
- The hero does 80% of the conversion. Spend 80% of your edit time there.
- The button copy is part of the offer.
- Awareness stage decides page length. Not your preference. Not the
  designer's preference.
- VoC mining is uncomfortable, slow, and the highest-leverage skill in
  the discipline.
