# The Big 5 and The Selling 7 — Frameworks Deep

These are the two content frameworks every TAYA implementation runs on.
Big 5 is the article taxonomy. Selling 7 is the video taxonomy. Together
they cover roughly 80% of what a buyer searches and watches during the
consideration phase of a purchase.

---

# PART ONE: THE BIG 5 (Article Topics That Actually Drive Sales)

After 15+ years of looking at content that converts vs content that
collects dust, five categories show up over and over as the ones that
actually produce buyers. Every industry. No exceptions yet.

The Big 5 are: **Cost, Problems, Comparisons, Best-Of, Reviews.**

If your website doesn't have content in each of these five buckets,
you are leaving money on the floor — full stop.

---

## Big 5 #1: COST & PRICING ARTICLES

The most-searched, most-clicked, and most-avoided of the five.

### What buyers actually type

- "How much does [thing] cost?"
- "Average price of [thing] in [year]"
- "[Thing] cost breakdown"
- "Why is [thing] so expensive?"
- "[Thing] price by [variable]" (e.g. "EB-5 visa cost by attorney")

### Why companies avoid it

Three reasons, all wrong:

1. "Our pricing is too variable" — wrong. Publish ranges and the
   factors that move the range.
2. "Competitors will see what we charge" — wrong. They already know.
   Your existing customers tell their friends and their friends Google.
3. "We'll scare prospects away" — wrong. You're scaring away the wrong
   prospects, which is what you want. The right ones thank you for
   the honesty.

### What makes a cost article work

- **Real numbers.** Ranges are fine. Vague is not. "$30K to $200K" is
  honest. "It depends on your needs" is a non-answer that destroys
  trust.
- **The factors.** What makes a price go up. What makes it go down.
  Treat the buyer like the intelligent adult they are.
- **Where you fall.** Are you the premium, mid, or value option in your
  space? Say so. The buyer is figuring this out anyway.
- **Why some companies charge less (and the trade-off).** This is the
  honest version of competitive positioning.
- **A live calculator if you can manage it.** Self-service pricing is
  pillar 3 of the Endless Customers model.

### The single article that made $35 million

At River Pools we published "How Much Does a Fiberglass Pool Cost?"
in 2009. It listed the price range ($45K-$100K at the time), the
factors that drove cost up (size, options, site conditions, deck
material, accessories), and what a typical install looked like.
That ONE article, over the years that followed, generated more than
$35 million in attributable revenue and was the single biggest reason
the company survived 2009.

If you remember one example, remember that one. One article. One
honest answer. $35M.

### Cost article templates for the three DR verticals

**Immigration law:** "How Much Does an EB-5 Visa Actually Cost in
[Year]?" — break down USCIS filing fees, attorney fees range,
regional center investment, processing time costs, admin fund.

**Franchise:** "How Much Does It Cost to Open a [Franchise X]
Franchise?" — franchise fee, equipment, real estate, working capital,
total investment range.

**Vocational training:** "How Much Does HVAC School Actually Cost?" —
tuition range, certification fees, tools, financing options, what you
earn after vs the investment.

## Big 5 #2: PROBLEMS ARTICLES

Articles that name the downsides, failure modes, and "what could go
wrong" of your product, service, or category.

### What buyers actually type

- "Problems with [thing]"
- "[Thing] disadvantages"
- "Is [thing] worth it?"
- "Should I avoid [thing]?"
- "[Thing] regret" / "[Thing] complaints"

### Why this works

Two things happen when you publish honest problems:

1. **You rank for high-intent searches** that almost nobody in your
   space wants to compete for. "Problems with fiberglass pools"
   brings in serious buyers doing serious research. They are NOT
   tire-kickers. Tire-kickers don't research downsides.

2. **You build the credibility to sell anything else you say.** If a
   company is willing to admit downsides, the buyer trusts the upsides.
   If a company hides downsides, the buyer assumes everything is
   suspect.

### The structure of a problems article

- Honest list of real problems with your product/service category
- For each: who it actually affects (not everyone)
- For each: how to mitigate or work around it
- Honest conclusion: who this is genuinely a bad fit for (and that's
  OK — go work with someone else and good luck)

### What it is NOT

It's not a competitor hit-piece dressed as a problems article. "The
biggest problem with X is that it isn't us." Buyers see through that
in 2 seconds. Be honest about your own category, your own service,
your own approach. THAT is what builds trust.

## Big 5 #3: COMPARISONS ARTICLES (Versus content)

"X vs Y" content. The single highest-intent search behavior on the
internet. If someone is typing "[Vendor A] vs [Vendor B]," they are
within hours or days of buying.

### What buyers actually type

- "[Brand A] vs [Brand B]"
- "[Material A] vs [Material B]"
- "[Service A] vs [Service B] which is better"
- "[Approach A] or [Approach B] for [use case]"
- "[Competitor] alternatives"

### Why companies avoid it

Fear of mentioning competitors by name. "We don't want to give them
free traffic." Wrong frame. If buyers are searching the comparison,
the comparison is being written somewhere — by an affiliate site,
a Reddit thread, a competitor with more guts than you. The traffic
is leaving the building either way. The only question is whether
YOU shape the comparison or whether someone else does.

### What makes a comparison article work

- **Name the competitor by name.** Use their actual brand. "Our top
  competitor" is cowardly and ranks for nothing.
- **Be fair.** List real strengths of the alternative. If the buyer
  reads a hatchet job, they'll go find a balanced source. If the
  buyer reads a balanced piece from YOU, you've built more trust
  than the alternative just did.
- **Be specific about who each option is best for.** "If you value
  X over Y, go with them. If you value Y over X, that's where we
  shine."
- **End with a clear recommendation, hedged honestly.** Not "we're
  obviously best." That's a brochure. Something like: "For most
  buyers in [situation X], we believe we're the right fit because
  [specific reasons]. If you're in [situation Y], honestly, the
  other option will probably serve you better."

## Big 5 #4: BEST-OF ARTICLES

"Best [thing] in [year]" or "Top 5 [category] for [use case]" lists.
These are the listicle articles people pretend are unsophisticated
but in fact drive enormous amounts of decision-stage traffic.

### What buyers actually type

- "Best [thing] in [year]"
- "Top [N] [thing] for [use case]"
- "Best [thing] for [type of buyer]"
- "Most [adjective] [thing]" (most affordable, most reliable, etc.)

### Why this works

Buyers in the final phase of consideration want a curated shortlist.
If you publish that shortlist — honestly, including yourself but not
just yourself — you become the trusted source the buyer uses to make
the final decision.

### The hard part

You have to include competitors. You have to. A best-of list that's
just your own product with five fake "honorable mentions" reads as
biased and ranks for nothing because Google's algorithm has gotten
extremely good at sniffing that out.

The TAYA way: write the genuinely best-of-category list. Yes, include
yourself. Yes, include real competitors. Be honest about strengths
and weaknesses of each. Trust that being the most honest source on
the shortlist gets you the bookings.

### Example: pools

At River Pools we published "Top 5 Fiberglass Pool Manufacturers."
We were one of them. We weren't always listed first. We listed
strengths and weaknesses honestly. That article ranked #1 for years
and converted prospects to consults at higher rates than any
purely-self-promotional article ever did.

## Big 5 #5: REVIEWS

Honest reviews of products, vendors, services, and approaches in your
category. Including competitors. Sometimes especially competitors.

### What buyers actually type

- "[Brand X] review"
- "[Brand X] reviews [year]"
- "Is [Brand X] legit?"
- "[Brand X] complaints"
- "Honest review of [Brand X]"

### Why this works

Buyer brains in the final phase of buying are flooded with marketing
hype. They actively seek out third-party-feeling reviews to balance
that. If your site is the place where they find balanced reviews —
including reviews of competitors — you become the trusted reference
point. And the trust transfers.

### The brave version

Write an honest review of your own service. Where it falls short.
Who it doesn't serve well. Who shouldn't hire you. Almost no one in
your industry has the courage to do this. That courage is the moat.

# PART TWO: THE SELLING 7 (Video Types That Produce Sales)

If the Big 5 are the written content engine, the Selling 7 are the
video equivalent. These are NOT awareness videos. NOT branding videos.
NOT explainer videos. These are sales videos — they are designed to
move opportunities through the pipeline.

Order matters. Do them in this sequence.

---

## Selling 7 #1: THE 80% VIDEO

The single most important video your business will ever make.

### What it is

A single video — usually 5-15 minutes — that answers the 7 to 10
questions that 80% of your prospects ask in 80% of your sales calls.

### Why it works

Ask your sales team: "What percent of the questions you get on a
sales call are the same questions you got last week?" Across thousands
of companies, the answer is 70-90%. The 80% video pre-answers them.

### How to make it

1. Sales team lists every question they've heard in the last 30 days.
   Cluster into themes.
2. Pick the 7-10 most common.
3. Film a real expert (a senior salesperson, a technical lead, the
   founder) answering them on camera. Not a teleprompter. Not an
   actor. The real person.
4. Edit lightly. Chapter markers for each question. Clear B-roll.
5. Put the video at the top of the homepage and every major service
   page.

### What it does to the sales process

Prospects who watch the 80% video before the first sales call show
up with the basic questions already answered. Sales calls become
discovery and qualification conversations instead of FAQ recitations.
Calls shorten. Close rates rise. Sales reps are happier.

---

## Selling 7 #2: BIO VIDEOS

Short (60-90 second) video bios of the actual humans on your team.
Especially: sales team, leadership, anyone the prospect will
interact with.

### Why it works

Buyers want to know who they're going to be on a call with. A real
human face raises trust dramatically. Stock photos lower it. LinkedIn
photos are neutral. Video is the high-trust signal.

### What goes in a bio video

- Real person, real voice
- Why they do this work
- Something genuinely personal — kids, hobby, hometown
- Why they're good at what they do (specific, not "I love helping clients")

### Where it goes

On the sales rep's email signature. On the "meet the team" page. As
a follow-up to the first sales email. Embedded on the booking
confirmation page.

---

## Selling 7 #3: PRODUCT/SERVICE VIDEOS

Short videos explaining what your actual product or service IS. Not
the marketing version. The actual version, from someone who actually
delivers it.

### Why it works

Marketing copy describes the product. Video shows it. Buyers want
to see the thing they're buying. Stock photography and abstract
illustrations don't do that.

### Examples

- Immigration law: a video walking through what an EB-5 case actually
  looks like, week by week, from filing to green card.
- Franchise: a video walking through a real franchisee's day — what
  they actually do, what the support looks like, what the unit
  economics actually feel like.
- Vocational: a video walking a prospect through the actual classroom,
  the actual instructor, the actual hands-on lab, and what graduation
  day looks like.

---

## Selling 7 #4: LANDING PAGE VIDEOS

A short (60-90 second) video at the top of every key landing page,
delivered by a real human, explaining what the page is about and
what the buyer will get if they fill in the form.

### Why it works

Conversion rate on landing pages with a real-human video routinely
outperforms text-only equivalents by 30-80% in TAYA case data. The
video doesn't have to be fancy. It has to be human.

---

## Selling 7 #5: COST AND PRICE VIDEOS

The video companion to the Big 5 cost article. A real person on
camera talking honestly about money.

### Why it works

Writing about pricing is brave. Saying it on camera is braver.
Buyers reward bravery with trust.

### What goes in it

- The honest range (with factors)
- Why some companies in this space charge less and the trade-off
- Why your company falls where it falls
- Who is and isn't a good fit at your price point

---

## Selling 7 #6: CUSTOMER JOURNEY VIDEOS

Video case studies. Real customers. Real before-and-after.

### Why it works

Written case studies feel marketing. Video case studies feel human.
The same client saying the same words on camera carries 3-5x the
trust weight of the same words on a PDF.

### Structure

- Where the customer was before they bought (the pain)
- Why they chose you (and what they considered)
- What the process was actually like (good and bad)
- Where they are now (the outcome)
- Who they'd recommend you to (and who they wouldn't)

---

## Selling 7 #7: CLAIMS-WE-MAKE VIDEOS (a.k.a. Proof videos)

Every claim your marketing makes — back it up with a video that
proves it.

### Why it works

Every company says "best customer service" or "fastest turnaround"
or "highest quality." If everyone says it, it's noise. If you SHOW
it on camera — the actual support call being answered in 8 seconds,
the actual production line, the actual quality check — the claim
becomes proof.

If you don't show it, it doesn't exist in the buyer's mind.

### Examples

- Claim: "Same-day response." Show: clock-on-screen video of an
  inbound lead, the actual call being placed back within an hour.
- Claim: "100% in-house manufacturing." Show: a 3-minute tour of
  the actual facility.
- Claim: "Senior lawyer on every case." Show: video bios of every
  attorney and a written promise that any case touches one of them.

---

## How To Sequence Production

Year 1 priority order if you're starting from zero:

1. The 80% video (week 4)
2. Bio videos for sales team (weeks 5-8)
3. Landing page videos for top 5 landing pages (weeks 9-16)
4. Cost video (week 17)
5. Customer journey videos (one per month from month 6)
6. Claims-we-make videos (one per month from month 8)
7. Product/service videos (continuous — there's always another one)

By month 12 you have 30-50 sales videos in the library and a sales
team that uses them as homework before every call.

That's the engine. That's how you become the most known and trusted
voice in your space — on camera, in writing, on the internet, in front
of the buyer at the moment they actually decide.
