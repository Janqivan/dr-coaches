## Framework 1 — The A/B/C Player Taxonomy (Topgrading, Ch 1-2)

Brad's research-backed split, replicated across industries and across thousands of ghSMART placements:

- **A-Player** — top 10% of talent available for the role at the comp on offer
- **B-Player** — middle 80%
- **C-Player** — bottom 10%

**Critical operator distinction:** A/B/C is **relative to the role and the comp on offer**, not absolute. A $60K A-player is different from a $200K A-player. A field-sales A is different from an enterprise-AE A.

**The hard rule Brad documents:** a C-player in an A-seat is **more expensive than an empty seat**. They consume management attention, repel A-players from joining the team, damage output, AND require severance to remove. The empty seat at least doesn't damage retention.

**The corollary:** **A's hire A's. B's hire C's.** Hiring managers unconsciously screen for candidates who don't threaten them. A B-player hiring manager will reject A's as "not a fit" because the A makes them feel exposed.

**Operator use:** apply the taxonomy to your CURRENT team. Every seat, every person. Force the verdict. Tolerating C's in A-seats is the single most expensive operator error Brad documents.

## Framework 2 — The Topgrading Interview (Topgrading, Ch 5-7)

The structured chronological interview. The single highest-information step in hiring. Geoff defers to Brad on this entire framework.

### Structure

Chronological walk through **every job** the candidate has held, in order, from first job (often part-time / college) through current role.

### Per-job question battery

For each job, the same battery in the same order:

1. **What was the job?** (role, company, dates)
2. **What were you hired to do?** (the implicit Scorecard at hire)
3. **What were your major accomplishments?** (outcomes, with metrics)
4. **What were the low points or biggest mistakes?** (signal on self-awareness + recovery patterns)
5. **Who was your boss?** (full name, role)
6. **What was the boss's style, and how would you rate them as a manager 1-10?** (signal on relationship patterns)
7. **How would that boss rate your performance 1-10 when we talk to them?** (the TORC-loaded question)
8. **Why did you leave?** (the real reason — patterns emerge across jobs)

### Format

- **Two interviewers (Tandem Interview).** One asks questions, one writes notes. Switch roles every 60-90 minutes.
- **3.5-4 hours total** for a senior seat. Shorter is malpractice — the patterns don't surface in less time.
- **No interruptions.** Calendar protected, room booked, phones off.
- **Notes are verbatim where possible.** Capture the candidate's own language, especially for "why I left" answers.

### Why chronological?

Patterns are the entire point. A candidate who left every job at the 18-month mark, blaming the boss, is showing you a pattern they cannot hide across a 4-hour interview. A 1-hour interview hides the pattern under "fit" questions.

### What it surfaces

- Truth-vs-narrative gaps (verbose justifications = potential lie, per Brad's research)
- Pattern of bosses' ratings (1-10 self-reported) and the gaps from job to job
- Reasons-for-leaving as a single longitudinal arc
- The relationship between hired-to-do (job 5) and accomplishments (job 5) — execution under defined Scorecards
- Where the candidate's career trajectory accelerated and where it stalled, and why

### The deliverable

A **behaviorally-grounded judgment** of probability against the Scorecard's specific outcomes. Not a personality impression. Not a "vibe". Probability of hitting outcome 1, outcome 2, outcome 3, on the seat being hired for.

## Framework 3 — TORC (Threat of Reference Check)

Brad's invention. Deployed at the **start** of every Topgrading interview, before any chronological questions begin.

### The exact script

> "At the end of this process, if we move forward, we'll ask you to personally arrange reference calls with every boss you've had — and we'll do those calls. So if you'd tell us anything in this interview that's different from what those bosses would say, we'd rather know now."

### Why it works

The threat alone — before any reference is actually called — recalibrates candidate honesty across the entire 4-hour interview. Brad's documented finding: candidates routinely reveal weaknesses, conflicts, and reasons-for-leaving they would otherwise have hidden.

The mechanism is psychological, not procedural. The candidate cannot risk that DR will discover a divergence in the reference call. So they preempt by telling the truth in the interview.

### Critical execution detail

**You MUST actually do the reference calls.** If candidates in the candidate's network learn that DR doesn't follow through on TORC, the signal degrades fast. TORC's power is in the credibility of the threat.

### The reference-call mechanic

- Candidate arranges the calls (not DR's recruiter — the candidate). This is part of TORC's signal: a candidate unwilling to set up the calls is signaling something.
- Bosses, not peers. (Peers = noise. Bosses = signal.)
- 45+ minute calls, structured.
- Ask the same per-job battery: rate them 1-10, what were their accomplishments, why did they leave?
- The 1-10 from the boss vs the 1-10 the candidate said the boss would give — that delta is the most diagnostic number in the process.

## Framework 4 — The Cost of a Mis-Hire (Topgrading, Ch 3)

Brad's research, replicated across industries and decades. The economic engine that justifies 60+ hours of process per senior hire.

### The headline number

**A mis-hired manager costs ~15x their annual salary.** Conservative estimate. Some of Brad's case studies put the multiplier at 24x.

### The composition

The 15x breaks down across:

1. **Direct comp paid during failed tenure** — salary + bonus + benefits + equity vested
2. **Severance** — varies by jurisdiction, often 3-6 months at exit
3. **Recruiting cost (twice)** — once for the failed hire, once for the replacement (typically 20-30% of first-year comp through search, plus internal time)
4. **Lost productivity (candidate's)** — output that didn't happen, deals not closed, projects not shipped
5. **Lost productivity (team's)** — direct reports working around the failed manager, or paralyzed by them
6. **Opportunity cost** — what the company didn't pursue because the seat was occupied by the wrong person
7. **Team damage** — A-players who left because the mis-hire stayed. Each lost A-player triggers its own 15x cascade.

### Operator implication

For any hire above $80K USD/year, the math justifies the full Topgrading process (multi-stage interview, 4-hour chronological, reference calls, full Scorecard). At $100K base, the mis-hire bet is $1.5M. Spending 60 hours on the process is a 4% process-cost-to-mis-hire-cost ratio. Not spending the 60 hours is the malpractice.

**For DR's Integrator hire:** the mis-hire cost is materially higher than 15x salary, because the Integrator either accelerates or stalls every other hire and process decision in the company. A failed Integrator drags 18 months. Brad's 15x is the floor, not the ceiling, for compounding seats.
