# Small Firm Roadmap — Full Methodology

The Lawyerist worldview, expressed through The Small Firm Roadmap (2019),
The Small Firm Roadmap Revisited (2023), the Small Firm Operating System,
the Small Firm Scorecard, and 600+ Lawyerist Podcast episodes.

This is the canonical reference for stage diagnosis, focus-area routing,
the three owner roles, the client-centered vs lawyer-centered split, and
the five essential traits.

---

## 1. THE CORE PREMISE

Most small law firms are built by accident, by lawyers who became lawyers
because they wanted to practice law — not because they wanted to run a
business. The default form is: solo or small partnership, billable hour,
intake-by-vibe, hiring-by-emergency, owner-as-bottleneck, no documented
systems, no real pricing strategy.

Lawyerist's argument since 2007: that default form is the past. The
healthy small firm of the future is:

- **Client-centered** (the experience is designed around how the client
  actually decides, pays, and feels)
- **Future-oriented** (the firm adopts technology, evolving fee models,
  remote/hybrid work, and AI as they emerge)
- **People-centered for the team** (built around a healthy team using
  efficient systems, not around the owner's heroics)
- **Profitable as a business** (real margin, real owner pay, real
  retained earnings, not just "the lawyer takes home what's left")
- **Intentionally designed** (the owner makes choices on purpose,
  not by default)
- **Inclusive and access-to-justice-aware** (Lawyerist's mission
  language)

The Small Firm Roadmap is the map from the default form to the healthy
form. The Small Firm Operating System (SFOS) is how you actually run
the healthy form day to day.

## 2. THE SIX FOCUS AREAS

Lawyerist's Small Firm Operating System sorts the work of running a small
firm into six focus areas. These are the canonical six (current 2024–2026
naming on the Lawyerist Lab and Scorecard pages). The 2019 book used
slightly different language ("strategy / team / clients / systems /
profits" plus owner) — same idea, refined wording.

### Focus Area 1 — Strategy

The choices about where you play and how you win.

- Vision: what does the firm look like in 3 years, in 10 years?
- Niche: who do you serve, what matter types, in what jurisdictions?
- Positioning: why you, not the firm down the street?
- Goals: what are you trying to build? Lifestyle firm? Growth firm?
  Multi-attorney scale? Eventual sale?
- Annual + quarterly priorities (Lab uses quarterly planning explicitly)

Diagnostic questions:
- Have you written down a 3-year vision?
- Is your niche specific enough that an ideal client would recognize
  themselves in your homepage in 5 seconds?
- Do you have 3-5 quarterly priorities, or 47 things on a wish list?

### Focus Area 2 — Finances

The numbers that make the firm sustainable.

- Pricing model: hourly, flat fee, value-based, subscription, hybrid?
- Owner pay: real salary at market rate, paid first, not "what's left"?
- Margin: are you profitable after market-rate owner pay, or only on
  paper?
- Cash flow: trust accounting, retainer replenishment, AR aging?
- Tax + retained earnings strategy?

Diagnostic questions:
- What's your average matter revenue?
- Is your hourly rate (if you bill hourly) a fair price for the value
  you deliver, or a leftover from your last firm?
- Are you paying yourself a real salary, or just taking distributions?
- Could the firm survive a 90-day cash gap?

### Focus Area 3 — Team

The people who do the work and the structure they work inside.

- Org chart / accountability chart: who owns what?
- Core values: written down, used in hiring, used in firing?
- Hiring scorecards: defined role outcomes before posting the job?
- Onboarding: documented, repeatable, less than 4 hours of owner time?
- Performance reviews + 1:1 cadence?
- Compensation philosophy?

Diagnostic questions:
- Could you list your firm's core values right now without checking?
- Did you write a scorecard before your last hire — or did you hire
  in a panic because you were drowning?
- If you took two weeks off, would the team know what to do?

### Focus Area 4 — Client Experience

Everything from first contact to closed matter.

- Intake: response time, intake script, consultation booking flow
- Engagement: how the fee agreement reads, how it's signed, how
  payment is set up
- Communication: cadence, channel, who responds, in how long
- Delivery: how matter work is staged, status updates, milestones
- Closure: closing letter, asks for review, asks for referral

Diagnostic questions:
- What's the average time from inbound lead form → first human contact?
- Do you have a written intake script, or does it depend who answers?
- When was the last time you asked a closed client for a review?

This is also where "Intake as Marketing" lives — see the operations canon.

### Focus Area 5 — Systems

The documented, repeatable infrastructure.

- Operations manual / SOP library
- Practice management software (Clio, MyCase, PracticePanther, etc.)
- Document automation
- Automated client communication
- Recurring meeting cadence (weekly team meeting, monthly review,
  quarterly planning, annual offsite)
- KPIs and dashboards

Diagnostic questions:
- If your top paralegal left tomorrow, how much of their job lives in
  their head vs. in a document?
- How many of your processes are "I just know how to do it"?
- Do you have a weekly team meeting with a real agenda?

### Focus Area 6 — Owner Sustainability

The thing most consultants skip.

- Owner energy: hours worked, hours of deep work, hours of admin
- Owner health: sleep, exercise, mental health
- Owner role clarity: how many hours as entrepreneur, manager, technician?
- Time off: do you take vacation that's actually vacation?
- Succession / exit: what's the long game?

Diagnostic questions:
- How many hours did you work last week, honestly?
- When was your last week off with no email check?
- If you got hit by a bus, what happens to the firm and your family
  in 90 days?

A firm that hits its revenue target but breaks the owner is not healthy.
Lawyerist names this as a focus area on purpose.

## 3. THE FOUR STAGES — Small Firm Scorecard

The Small Firm Scorecard (introduced in 2025, podcast episode #570) sorts
firms into four stages. The stage determines what's appropriate to work
on next. Wrong-stage advice is the most common failure mode.

### Stage 1 — Launch

State: Just opened. Often just the owner. Maybe a part-time admin or
contract paralegal. Revenue irregular.

Pain: No clients. No cash. Trying to prove the business model works.

Where the focus goes:
- Strategy: pick a niche, even a temporary one
- Finances: get cash flowing, set first rates, open trust account
  properly
- Client experience: have *some* intake process, even if it's a Google
  Form and a phone number
- Owner sustainability: just survive the first 12 months without burning
  out

Wrong moves at Stage 1:
- Building a 50-page operations manual before you have 10 paying clients
- Hiring a marketing agency before the offer is proven
- Switching pricing models 4 times in the first year

### Stage 2 — Grow

State: Owner + 1 to 3 people. First real revenue. Beginning to feel
the constraints of the default form.

Pain: Owner-as-bottleneck. Intake leaks. Ad-hoc pricing. Hires are
emergency hires. Owner working 60+ hours and feeling guilty about all
of it.

Where the focus goes:
- Systems: document the top 5 processes. Intake first.
- Team: write the first real scorecard. Hire on purpose.
- Client experience: speed-to-lead protocol, written intake script,
  consultation flow, fee agreement template
- Finances: stop billing hourly for matters that have predictable
  scope. Start flat-fee experiments.

Wrong moves at Stage 2:
- Hiring more lawyers when the constraint is admin/paralegal
- Doubling ad spend when intake conversion is unknown
- Ignoring owner pay because "we'll fix it next year"

### Stage 3 — Scale

State: Team of 4 to 10+. Real operations layer. Owner spending less
time on every matter.

Pain: Leadership. Hiring. Owner role definition. Culture starting to
feel different. Decisions that used to be the owner's are getting made
without them — sometimes well, sometimes badly.

Where the focus goes:
- Team: leadership team, real management cadence, performance reviews
- Strategy: explicit vision and 3-year picture
- Systems: scorecards, dashboards, KPIs
- Owner sustainability: owner transitions toward CEO role

Wrong moves at Stage 3:
- Owner continuing to take every consultation
- Refusing to hire a real operations/admin lead
- Trying to scale on the same pricing model that worked at Stage 1

### Stage 4 — Mature

State: Owner is CEO or close to it. Leadership team owns functions.
Firm runs without the owner doing daily work.

Pain: What's next? Exit? Sale? Multi-firm? Intentional standstill?
Owner role redefinition (you may still want to lawyer a few hours a
week — but it's a choice now, not a trap).

Where the focus goes:
- Strategy: 10-year picture, succession, exit
- Owner sustainability: legacy, health, what you want the rest of
  your life to look like
- Finances: distributions, retained earnings, eventual valuation

Wrong moves at Stage 4:
- Owner inserting themselves back into operations and breaking
  the team
- Selling the firm before it can survive the owner's absence
- Not preparing for transition because "I'll figure it out later"

## 4. THE THREE OWNER ROLES (E / M / T)

Borrowed from Michael Gerber's E-Myth, woven through Lawyerist content
since 2007.

- **Entrepreneur** — designs the business. Sets strategy. Makes the
  big bets. Decides what to build and what to kill.
- **Manager** — runs the business. Builds and leads the team.
  Maintains the systems. Holds people accountable.
- **Technician** — does the work. Practices law. Closes the file.

Every owner plays all three roles. The dysfunction is the proportion.

**Default dysfunction:** ~90% technician, ~10% manager, ~0% entrepreneur.
This is how most small firms run. It is also why they plateau.

**Healthy proportion by stage:**
- Stage 1: maybe 70% technician, 20% manager, 10% entrepreneur
- Stage 2: 50/30/20
- Stage 3: 20/40/40
- Stage 4: 5/25/70 (or less technician, by choice)

The shift from technician-heavy to entrepreneur-heavy is the central
journey of the small firm owner. It does not happen automatically. It
requires explicit time-blocking and a willingness to let the team do
work the owner used to do — sometimes worse than the owner would do it,
at first.

Stephanie Everett's framing: lawyers get stuck doing $20/hour work
(filing, scheduling, drafting boilerplate) instead of $500/hour CEO
work (pricing, hiring, strategy). The $20/hour work feels productive
because something is getting done. The $500/hour work feels
uncomfortable because nothing visible is happening. The math is
brutal — and obvious once you see it.

## 5. CLIENT-CENTERED VS LAWYER-CENTERED

This is the cultural dividing line in Lawyerist's worldview.

**Lawyer-centered firm (the default):**
- Hourly billing because that's how lawyers learned to bill
- Office hours 9-to-5 because that's when the lawyer works
- "We'll call you back within 48 hours" because that's convenient
- 12-page engagement letter that protects the firm
- Communication on the firm's cadence
- Process designed around what the lawyer wants to do

**Client-centered firm (the healthy form):**
- Pricing model matched to how the client wants to pay (flat fee for
  predictable scope, subscription for ongoing needs, value-based for
  high-stakes matters)
- Availability designed around when the client can talk (evenings,
  weekends, async via text/portal where appropriate)
- Response time measured in minutes for new inbound, hours for active
  matters
- Engagement letter written in plain English
- Communication on the client's cadence
- Process designed around the client's experience of the matter

The market is moving. The lawyer-centered firm is the legacy form.
Lawyerist's argument since 2007 is that the firms that survive the
next decade will be the client-centered ones.

## 6. THE FIVE ESSENTIAL TRAITS

From Lawyerist's Healthy Firm content. The five traits the owner needs.

1. **Intentional** — you design the firm on purpose, instead of letting
   it happen to you.
2. **Entrepreneurial** — you treat the firm as a business worth
   investing in and improving, not just a job you happen to own.
3. **Empathetic** — you remember that being a client is unnerving, and
   you design the experience accordingly.
4. **Self-aware** — you don't pretend the overworked-unfulfilled-lawyer
   thing is normal. You invest in your own well-being.
5. **Adaptable** — change is constant. The firm that adapts wins. The
   firm that defends the old model dies slowly.

These are not aspirational poster values. They are the operating
prerequisites for getting through the four stages without burning out.

## 7. HOW THIS METHODOLOGY ROUTES DIAGNOSTICS

When the user shows up with a problem, the routing goes:

1. What stage is the firm? (1 / 2 / 3 / 4)
2. Which of the six focus areas is the binding constraint?
3. Which owner role (E / M / T) is over- or under-staffed?
4. Is the firm client-centered or lawyer-centered on the dimension
   we're discussing?
5. Which of the five traits is missing in the owner's current behavior?
6. What is the stage-appropriate next move?

This routing is the entire skill. Everything else — pricing model
choice, intake script design, scorecard format, hire timing — is
downstream of getting these five questions right.
