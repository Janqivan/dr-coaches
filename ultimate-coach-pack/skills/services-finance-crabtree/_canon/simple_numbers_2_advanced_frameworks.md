## Framework 1 — Productivity Per Labor Dollar at Scale (SN2, late chapters)

**What it is:** the extension of LER for firms past $5M. At scale, "GP / total labor" hides too much — Crabtree moves to GP per direct labor dollar AND profit per total labor dollar, watched together.

**Why a second ratio:** at $1M revenue, watching one LER is enough. At $20M with three divisions, two service lines, and a leadership layer, one number averages out so many seats that it stops driving action. The second ratio — pretax profit per total labor dollar — catches the firms that have a strong GP/labor ratio but spend all of it on management overhead and produce no profit per labor dollar.

**Mechanism:**
1. GP per direct labor dollar — measures delivery productivity. Target 3:1+ at scale.
2. Pretax profit per total labor dollar — measures how much of the labor leverage actually converts to profit. Target $0.15-$0.25 per labor dollar at scale.
3. Watch both. If GP/direct labor is strong but profit/total labor is weak, the management + sales layer is eating the productivity.

**Operator move:** when a $10M+ services firm hits a profit plateau, this is the diagnostic that splits "delivery problem" from "overhead problem" — they look identical on the blended LER.

**Failure mode:** scaling a firm with strong delivery productivity into a management layer that doubles every two years. The blended LER drifts down by 0.1 per year and nobody notices until pretax is at 4%.

## Framework 2 — Talent Leverage and the Management Stair-Step (SN2, mid chapters)

**What it is:** the rule that says management hires don't get added one at a time as you "feel" you need them — they get added in stair-step jumps tied to specific GP thresholds.

**The pattern Crabtree observes:**
- $0-$1M: owner = everything. No formal management layer.
- $1M-$3M: first ops or delivery lead. One management hire. Pulled from inside.
- $3M-$5M: department leads start to appear. Sales, ops, delivery. This is the most dangerous step — most firms over-hire here.
- $5M-$10M: full leadership team forms. CFO/controller, head of ops, head of sales, head of delivery.
- $10M-$25M: division GMs if multi-service or multi-geography.
- $25M+: COO emerges. Layer below leadership starts to formalize.

**The rule:** each management hire must be tied to a GP threshold the firm has already crossed, not one it hopes to cross. "We're hiring a head of ops because we just hit $5M GP" is correct. "We're hiring a head of ops to help us get to $5M GP" is the move that breaks Management LER and starts the next Black Hole.

**Mechanism:**
1. Set the GP threshold the role unlocks (the GP level at which the role pays for itself at 2:1 Management LER).
2. Do not hire until trailing-twelve GP is at or above that threshold.
3. Hire only after the threshold is hit and the new GP is stable for two quarters.

**Failure mode:** hiring management on hope. The management cost shows up on the P&L day one. The GP shows up — if at all — six to twelve months later. The firm pays for the gap.

## Framework 3 — The Five-Bucket Cash Hierarchy (SN2, extension of Four Forces)

**What it is:** SN1's Core Capital + SN2's Four Forces of Cash Flow combined into the actual sequencing rule for what gets funded first as a scaling firm produces cash.

**The sequence (top of waterfall to bottom):**
1. **Operating cash** — payroll + opex for the next 30 days. Non-negotiable. Always full.
2. **Tax reserve** — quarterly estimates + a buffer for the annual surprise. Crabtree treats this as not-your-money the day it's earned.
3. **Core Capital** — the 2-months-of-opex fortress. Until this is full, no distributions, no aggressive reinvestment.
4. **Strategic reinvestment** — funded acquisition reserve, new-service-line investment, headcount-ahead-of-revenue if the GP math justifies it.
5. **Owner distributions** — the bucket that takes whatever is left after the four above are full.

**Why this order:** an owner who pulls distributions before tax reserve is full is borrowing from the government. An owner who pulls distributions before Core Capital is full is gambling on next quarter. Both end the same way — emergency LOC draw, panic decisions, mis-hires under pressure.

**Operator move:** the sequence is a discipline, not a budget. Each bucket gets funded in order every month. Distributions are the residual, not the line item.

**Failure mode:** treating distributions as a fixed monthly draw and squeezing the other four buckets to fund it. Every services firm that died in a downturn died from this.

## Framework 4 — ROIC as the Master Diagnostic (SN2, late chapters + keynote material)

**What it is:** Return on Invested Capital — pretax profit divided by invested capital (equity + interest-bearing debt) — is Crabtree's master diagnostic at scale.

**Why ROIC and not just LER:**
- LER measures labor productivity. ROIC measures business productivity.
- LER stays meaningful through scale. ROIC stays meaningful across capital structures — a firm running on owner equity and a firm running on bank debt are comparable on ROIC, not on raw profit.
- ROIC is the number a buyer or investor uses. Building the firm to a target ROIC builds enterprise value, not just current-year profit.

**Targets Crabtree references in his keynote work:**
- Below 20% ROIC: the business is under-earning on the capital tied up in it.
- 20-40%: healthy for a services firm.
- 40%+: world-class. Capital-light services firms running at world-class LER hit this.

**Mechanism:**
1. Calculate invested capital = equity contributed + retained earnings still in the business + interest-bearing debt.
2. Calculate pretax profit on a rolling-twelve basis.
3. Divide. Watch the slope.

**Failure mode:** running a services firm on pretax-profit-dollar targets without ROIC discipline. The owner builds a $30M revenue firm with $5M of trapped working capital and a 12% ROIC — financially they would have been better off staying at $10M revenue with $1M of working capital and a 30% ROIC.

## Framework 5 — Distributions Sequencing and the Owner's Personal Balance Sheet (SN2, mid-late chapters)

**What it is:** the rule that says owner distributions aren't a question of "how much can the business pay me" — they're a question of "what does the owner's personal balance sheet need next, given the business is funded."

**The hierarchy on the owner side:**
1. Market-based salary, paid through payroll every pay period. (Set in SN1.)
2. Tax obligations on K-1 / business income, paid from a tax-reserve bucket inside the business.
3. Owner emergency fund (6 months of personal expenses) outside the business, in liquid cash.
4. Owner retirement contributions to max legal limits per year — these compound, missing a year is expensive.
5. Diversified outside investments — owner net worth shouldn't be 95% trapped in the business.
6. Lifestyle distributions — the last bucket, funded only after 1-5 are healthy.

**The matching rule:** the business funds these in the order the personal balance sheet needs them, not in the order the owner feels like spending. A $2M distribution that goes to a vacation house while the owner has no liquid emergency fund and is under-funding retirement is bad capital allocation, even though the business can afford it.

**Why Crabtree pushes this:** owners who build $20M businesses with $50K of personal liquid net worth outside the business have one bad year and lose everything. The business funds the owner's full balance sheet, not just the fun part.

**Failure mode:** treating distributions as a reward for revenue growth. They're a function of business cash discipline AND owner net-worth diversification.

## Framework 6 — Tax Planning as a Profit Lever (SN2, late chapters + CRI tax planning content)

**What it is:** Crabtree's position — coming out of his CPA practice — that tax planning is a year-round profit lever, not an April surprise. Inside CRI Simple Numbers it's structured as quarterly tax planning, not annual tax filing.

**The principles (US default, applies in form to other jurisdictions):**
1. **Entity choice matches the math.** S-corp, C-corp, LLC, partnership — each produces different tax outcomes per dollar of profit at different revenue levels. The right entity at $1M may be wrong at $10M.
2. **Quarterly review beats annual scramble.** Tax is modeled every quarter against rolling-twelve P&L. Surprises in April mean the planning failed in Q1-Q3.
3. **Reasonable comp + distributions split (S-corp specific).** Owner takes market-based salary on payroll, the rest as distribution. The split must survive IRS scrutiny — too low a salary is a flag.
4. **Retirement vehicles as tax shelters.** Defined-benefit plans, solo 401(k)s, profit-sharing — each shelters profit at different scale. Most $5M+ firms under-use these.
5. **Capital investment timing.** Section 179, bonus depreciation, and equipment timing inside a tax year matter — but only after the operational decision is correct. Never let the tax tail wag the operational dog.

**Operator move:** the tax-strategy conversation happens in Q3 of every year, not in March. By Q3 you can still move things; by March you can only report.

**Failure mode:** treating tax as a once-a-year compliance event. The firm leaves five to seven figures on the table over a decade because nothing was modeled until the return was due.

## Framework 7 — Capacity Modeling at $5M+ (SN2, mid chapters + Functional Finance for Entrepreneurs content)

**What it is:** at scale, "do we have capacity for more work?" is no longer a gut call — it's a model. Crabtree's capacity framework ties seats to billable / productive hours, GP per hour, and the LER constraint.

**The model:**
1. Per Direct seat: define expected productive hours per year (typically 1,400-1,800 in services).
2. Per Direct seat: define GP-per-productive-hour target (set by LER 2.5:1 floor and the seat's fully-loaded labor cost).
3. Total Direct capacity = (number of Direct seats) x (productive hours per seat) x (GP per hour).
4. Compare total Direct capacity to current run-rate Direct GP.
5. Utilization = current Direct GP / total Direct capacity.

**The decision rules:**
- Utilization above 90%: hire ahead. You're about to run out of capacity and lose deals or burn the team.
- Utilization 70-90%: healthy band. Don't hire.
- Utilization 50-70%: don't hire. Sell more or right-size.
- Utilization below 50%: cut Direct seats now. The math will not improve by waiting.

**Why this beats gut:** services firms over-hire because the leader of each delivery team always feels short-staffed. The math gives the owner an answer that doesn't depend on whose team is loudest.

**Failure mode:** running capacity by team complaint. Whoever shouts loudest gets the next hire, regardless of whether their team is at 60% utilization.

## Framework 8 — The Pricing Discipline at Scale (SN2, late chapters + Pricing Strategy keynote)

**What it is:** Crabtree's pricing rule for services at $5M+ — every price point must clear three tests, not one.

**The three tests:**
1. **Cost-plus test.** Price covers fully-loaded labor cost + opex allocation + target GP at 2.5:1+ LER. This is the floor — below this you can't operate.
2. **Value-relative test.** Price reflects the value the client gets, not what it costs you to deliver. A 60-hour engagement that saves the client $2M should not be priced at 60 x hourly rate.
3. **Market-comparison test.** Price reflects where the market actually transacts for this service in this category — not where you wish it transacted.

**The discipline:** every renewal, every new proposal, every retainer review runs all three tests. If a deal clears the cost-plus test but fails the value-relative test, the firm is leaving money on the table. If it clears value-relative but fails cost-plus, the firm is funding the client.

**The inflation overlay:** in inflationary environments, the cost-plus floor moves every six months. Firms that hold prices constant during inflation watch LER collapse without anything else changing — labor cost is up 8% YoY, prices are flat, GP/labor ratio drops 0.2 in eighteen months.

**Operator move:** every six months, re-test the top 10 active engagements against all three. The bottom three on the value-relative test are your immediate price-increase candidates.

**Failure mode:** treating pricing as an annual conversation. Cost moves quarterly. Prices have to move at least annually, or the firm bleeds.

## Framework 9 — Acquisition Math for Services Firms (SN2, late chapters + speaker content)

**What it is:** for services firms looking to grow via acquisition (a roll-up, a tuck-in, a bolt-on practice), the rule set Crabtree applies before any deal closes.

**The pre-close tests:**
1. **LER comparison.** The target's Direct LER on a like-for-like cost basis (i.e., re-run with market-based owner comp). If target LER is below your floor, the deal is buying a problem.
2. **Customer concentration.** Top customer >25% of GP = acquisition risk goes vertical. Walk or discount hard.
3. **Owner dependency.** If the selling owner is the rainmaker AND the senior delivery talent, the GP walks out with them.
4. **Integration cost honesty.** Add 6-12 months of full duplicate overhead to the model. Most acquirers under-model this and produce post-close P&Ls that lie.
5. **Working capital absorption.** The acquired firm comes with AR, WIP, and payables. Net working-capital draw is real cash — model it explicitly.

**The post-close playbook:**
1. Re-cast the target's P&L on the SN chart of accounts (labor out of COGS, true GP, three LER buckets).
2. Set Core Capital Target for the combined entity within 90 days.
3. Hold market-based owner comp on every retained leader.
4. Run rolling-twelve on the combined entity from day one.

**Why services acquisitions fail:** the acquirer pays a multiple based on EBITDA that was calculated with the seller under-paid and labor mis-classified. The "real" EBITDA on the SN chart is half of what the deal was priced on.

**Failure mode:** trusting the seller's P&L without re-casting it. Buy a number, end up with a different number.

## Framework 10 — The 100-Company Economic Dashboard (SN2 + Crabtree's keynote series)

**What it is:** Crabtree's CRI Simple Numbers practice maintains an aggregated economic model across the firm's ~100 private-company clients, representing roughly $1B in revenue. Used as a benchmark and as a leading indicator.

**What's in the dashboard:**
1. LER averages by industry sub-segment (services, distribution, light manufacturing, construction).
2. GP-per-labor-dollar quartiles within each segment.
3. Wage growth trends — the leading indicator on LER pressure.
4. Pricing power trends by industry.
5. Working capital draw patterns through expansion vs. contraction.

**How operators use it (via the keynote series):**
- Benchmark your LER, GP/labor, owner comp ratio against your industry segment quartile.
- Spot wage growth trends 6-9 months before they show up in your numbers.
- Anticipate pricing power shifts industry-wide, not just inside your firm.

**Why this matters:** an owner running on their own numbers in a vacuum has no idea whether 2.4:1 LER in their segment this year is great or terrible. The benchmark cohort makes the number actionable.

**Public reference:** the keynote "Simple Numbers Private Company Economic Update from our 100 Company Model" — gregcrabtree.net/keynote-speaking/simple-numbers-private-company-economic-update-from-our-100-company-model/

**Failure mode:** running your firm without a benchmark cohort. You optimize against last quarter's version of yourself, not against where the segment is moving.
