## Framework 1 — The 80/20 Power Curve (80/20 S&M, ch 1-3)

**What it is:** Pareto distribution as a fractal law. 80/20 is recursive — apply it to the top 20% and you get another Pareto distribution. Apply it again and again.

**Distinction Marshall draws:**
- Bell-curve thinking → averages, central tendency, "typical" customer.
- Power-curve thinking → extremes drive everything, average is meaningless, top 0.8% can outweigh bottom 99.2%.

Most marketers were trained on bell-curve math. Marshall's argument: marketing distributions are almost never bell curves. They're power curves. Reasoning from the average is reasoning from a number nobody actually represents.

**The math (memorize):**
- 80/20¹: top 20% = 80% of output
- 80/20² (apply 80/20 to the top 20%): top 4% = 64% of output
- 80/20³ (apply again): top 0.8% = 51% of output
- 80/20⁴: top 0.16% = 41% of output

**Operator's move:** don't stop at the first cut. Most agencies "ran 80/20" once and never again. The leverage is in 80/20² and 80/20³.

**Failure mode:** doing 80/20¹ and then "optimizing the bottom 80%". The bottom 80% can't be optimized into the top 20% — that's not how power curves work. Cut it, reallocate the budget and attention, and run another 80/20.

## Framework 2 — DRT: Desperate, Reachable, Targeted (80/20 S&M, ch 4-5)

**What it is:** A three-filter prospect qualifier. Before you write a single ad, the prospect must pass all three.

**The filters:**
1. **Desperate** — active, urgent pain. Searching for a solution NOW. Not "interested in" — actively bleeding.
2. **Reachable** — there's a cost-recoverable channel to get to them. Google search, Meta interest, LinkedIn title, trade publication, conference, industry list.
3. **Targeted** — describable with precision. The job title, the firm size, the deal type, the geography. "EB-5 immigration attorneys at firms doing $2M+ in legal fees, US-based, English-speaking" is targeted. "Lawyers" is not.

**Two extension filters Marshall added in *Memos from the Head Office* and his Marketing Letter — the Five Power Disqualifiers:**
4. **Able to pay** — budget already allocated. Pain without budget is a charity case.
5. **Empowered to act** — the person in the conversation can decide. Three layers down can't.

**Why it works:** filters out the markets that look attractive but aren't economic. Most agency churn comes from prospects who pass 1 or 2 of the filters but not all 5.

**Failure mode:** chasing "interested" prospects who aren't desperate. Interest doesn't pay invoices. Pain does.

## Framework 3 — Bleeding Neck Test (80/20 S&M, ch 5)

**What it is:** The market-entry filter. Marshall's claim: only enter a market where the prospect's neck is actively bleeding.

**The diagnostic question:**
> If your prospect doesn't buy a solution in the next 30 days, what happens?

- Nothing → not bleeding-neck. Walk away.
- Mild inconvenience → not bleeding-neck. Walk away.
- Lose a deal / case / client / contract → bleeding-neck. Enter.
- Go out of business / get sued / lose license → severely bleeding-neck. Enter aggressively.

**Three signatures of a bleeding-neck market:**
1. Existing competitors charging real money (proof someone is already paying for relief)
2. Active Google search volume on problem-aware keywords (the prospect is searching)
3. A timeline the prospect cannot push (statute, deadline, contract renewal, fiscal cycle)

**Failure mode:** trying to educate a market into desperation. If you have to explain why they should care, the neck isn't bleeding. Find a market where the problem is already named and acknowledged.

## Framework 4 — The Star Principle (80/20 S&M, ch 14, after Richard Koch)

**What it is:** Adapted from Richard Koch's *The Star Principle*. The single best predictor of long-run business value: are you #1 in a clearly-defined growing category?

**Four-quadrant model:**

|  | Growing market | Flat / declining market |
|---|---|---|
| #1 in category | **STAR** — scale aggressively, reinvest profits | Cash cow — milk, do not grow |
| Not #1 | Question mark — fix to #1 or sell | **Dog** — exit, redeploy capital |

**Two key disciplines:**
1. **Define the category narrowly enough that #1 is possible.** "Marketing agency" is too broad — you'll never be #1. "Compliant-Meta-for-immigration-law" is narrow enough that #1 is achievable in 24-36 months.
2. **Verify the market is growing.** Search volume trends. Ad spend trends. New-entrant counts. If the underlying market is shrinking, even #1 is a slow death.

**Why this matters more than people think:** the structural economics of #1 in any category are fundamentally different from #2-5. #1 gets the inbound, the press, the talent, the partnerships, the pricing power. #2 has to buy growth. #3-5 are subsidizing #1's expansion.

**Failure mode:** pouring growth capital into a Dog because it's "profitable today". Profitable Dogs are extinct on a 10-year horizon. The capital should be redeployed into a Star category or exit.

## Framework 5 — Marketing DNA (80/20 S&M, ch 11)

**What it is:** A three-axis personality model for the operator (not the business). Each axis is binary. Eight possible profiles.

**The three axes:**
1. **Specialist vs Generalist**
   - Specialist: inch wide, mile deep. One niche, expert-level.
   - Generalist: mile wide, inch deep. Many domains, integrative-level.
2. **Sayer vs Doer**
   - Sayer: writes, speaks, teaches, persuades. Output is words.
   - Doer: builds, ships, operates, executes. Output is systems.
3. **Caretaker vs Hunter**
   - Caretaker: grows existing accounts, deepens relationships, retains.
   - Hunter: opens new accounts, breaks into cold markets, prospects.

**The operating rule:** the business structure should match the founder's DNA. Mismatch produces friction and burnout. Marshall's claim: most founder unhappiness is structural mismatch, not execution failure.

**For Ivan/DR:** the founder's DNA shapes which roles are hired first, which functions are outsourced, and which kinds of clients the business naturally serves well.

**Failure mode:** running a business that requires a different DNA than the founder has, then blaming the founder for not "stepping up". Fix the structure, not the founder.

## Framework 6 — The Renaissance Club / Network-Density Model (Marshall newsletter, Renaissance Club material)

**What it is:** Output ceiling is capped by network quality. The premise — your output is approximately the average of the five operators you spend the most time with. Upgrade the room, upgrade the output.

**Operating questions:**
- Who are the five operators DR benchmarks against?
- Are they ahead, level, or behind on the dimensions DR cares about (revenue, margin, niche depth, client quality)?
- If behind on average — the network is the bottleneck, not the strategy.

**Why higher-leverage than skill acquisition:** a 10% upgrade to the average peer set produces a larger output gain than a 10% skill improvement, because peers compound (introductions, deal flow, talent, pricing intelligence, mistake-avoidance).

**Failure mode:** investing in courses and tools while the peer set stays flat. The bottleneck is the room, not the toolkit.

## Framework 7 — Google Ads Relevance Triangle (Ultimate Guide to Google Ads, recurring across editions)

**What it is:** The structural model for any high-Quality-Score Google Ads account. Three vertices must align:
1. **Keyword** — what the prospect typed
2. **Ad copy** — what they see in the SERP
3. **Landing page** — what they hit on click

**The mechanism:** Google's Quality Score is, at its core, a measure of how well these three points reinforce each other. High alignment = lower CPC, higher impression share, better Ad Rank. Low alignment = bid prison (paying 3-10× what a well-aligned competitor pays for the same click).

**Operator's discipline:**
- One keyword theme per ad group (don't dump 50 keywords in one group)
- Ad headline contains the exact match keyword variant
- Landing page H1 contains the exact match keyword variant
- Negative keyword list aggressively pruned (kill the irrelevant queries)

**80/20 inside the account:**
- 80/20¹: ~20% of keywords drive 80% of conversions. Cut the rest.
- 80/20²: ~4% drive 64%. Build dedicated ad groups + landing pages for these.
- 80/20³: ~0.8% drive 51%. These are your business — protect them, defend them, never let them go down.

## Framework 8 — Meta Audience-Stack Model (Ultimate Guide to Facebook Advertising, 4th ed.)

**What it is:** Meta's algorithm finds audiences faster than humans can target them, but only if the signal is clean. The operator's job is to define the stack of audience-quality signals the algorithm can optimize against.

**The stack (from highest signal to lowest):**
1. **Customer list lookalikes** — past buyers, fed in via Conversions API
2. **High-intent on-site events** — checkout-started, demo-booked, application-submitted
3. **Engaged audiences** — video-watched-75%, page-engaged, lead-form-opened
4. **Cold interest stacks** — combined interest + behavior targeting
5. **Broad** — letting the algorithm find them with creative as the only steering signal

**80/20 inside a Meta account:**
- 80/20¹: ~20% of ad sets drive 80% of conversions
- 80/20²: ~4% drive 64%. Scale these specifically.
- 80/20³: ~0.8% drive 51%. Often a single creative concept × a single audience stack.

**Special-ad-category caveat (immigration law specifically):** Meta restricts targeting on housing, employment, credit, AND special-protected demographics. Immigration ads fall into the credit/employment/housing special category in many markets. Lookalikes and interest targeting are restricted. The 80/20 still applies — but the available targeting stack is shorter.

## Framework 9 — Customer-Level Testing (recurring theme, Marketing Letter + 80/20 S&M ch 9)

**What it is:** Marshall's argument that the highest-leverage A/B test is not at the ad level, the headline level, or even the offer level — it's at the **customer-profile** level.

**The leverage hierarchy (memorize):**

| Test layer | Typical lift | Effort |
|---|---|---|
| Customer profile (who you sell to) | 5-30× | High |
| Offer (what you sell them) | 2-3× | Medium |
| Channel (where you reach them) | 1.5-2× | Medium |
| Ad creative (how you reach them) | 1.1-1.3× | Low |

**Most agencies run this hierarchy upside down.** They test creative first because it's easy, then they wonder why the campaign plateaus at 1.2× the original. The plateau is structural — you've optimized the bottom of the leverage ladder.

**The right order:**
1. Run 80/20 on the existing customer base. Identify the top 4% by LTV, retention, and referral.
2. Build a customer-profile description of that top 4%.
3. Target THAT profile in the next campaign. Even with worse creative, you'll outperform.
4. Only AFTER customer-profile is locked, start testing offer, channel, creative.

**Failure mode:** spending 6 months optimizing creative on a campaign that's targeting the wrong customer profile. The ceiling is the profile, not the creative.
