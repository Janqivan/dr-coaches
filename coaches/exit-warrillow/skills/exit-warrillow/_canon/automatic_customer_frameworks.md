# The Automatic Customer — Framework Map

**Author:** John Warrillow
**Book:** *The Automatic Customer: Creating a Subscription Business in Any Industry* — Portfolio / Penguin, 2015

## Why subscription matters (the operator's frame)

Warrillow's core thesis: subscription businesses sell for higher multiples than transactional businesses because buyers can underwrite future cash flow with much higher confidence. A traditional services firm gets valued on a multiple of trailing EBITDA. A subscription business gets valued on a multiple of annual recurring revenue (ARR) — which, in healthy SaaS bands, can land at 3-10x ARR versus 4-8x EBITDA for the services version of the same business. The same dollar of profit is worth more when it arrives on a credit card every month than when it has to be re-sold every quarter.

The book is structured in three parts:
1. **Why subscription** — the multiple lift, the cash-flow lift, the customer-lifetime-value math.
2. **The nine models** — pick the one that fits your customer's recurring job.
3. **How to convert** — pricing, churn, customer-acquisition-cost math, and the transition path from transactional to recurring.

## Framework 1 — The Subscription Premium (why buyers pay more)

**What it is:** A valuation differential model. The same business — same product, same customers, same revenue — is worth meaningfully more if the revenue arrives on subscription than if it arrives via one-off transaction.

**Mechanism:** buyers underwrite future cash flow. Predictability lowers the discount rate they apply. A dollar of subscription revenue carries lower default risk than a dollar of project revenue, so the present value of the subscription dollar is higher. The multiple compounds: predictable cash → higher confidence forecast → lower required return → higher multiple.

**Execution rule:** before any growth tactic, ask whether moving from transactional to subscription will lift the multiple more than growing the top line will. Often the answer is yes — restructuring revenue beats adding revenue.

**Failure mode:** founders chase top-line growth in a transactional model and ignore the structural arbitrage available by converting that same revenue to recurring. Growing a project business 30% adds 30% to the headline; converting 60% of that revenue to subscription can double the multiple.

## Framework 2 — The 9 Subscription Models (model taxonomy)

**What it is:** A complete map of the nine distinct subscription business models. Subscription isn't one thing — it's nine, and the wrong choice produces churn even with a good product.

**The nine, with the recurring job each one serves:**

1. **Membership Website** — gated content for monthly fee. Job: stay current. Surface: paid newsletters, courses with ongoing updates.
2. **All-You-Can-Eat Library** — flat fee, unlimited consumption. Job: convenience beats ownership. Surface: streaming, e-book libraries, stock-image plans.
3. **Private Club** — scarce, status-laden access. Job: belonging + status. Surface: high-end masterminds, EO, members-only clubs.
4. **Front-of-Line** — pay to skip the queue. Job: certainty under pressure. Surface: priority support tiers, expedited service.
5. **Consumables** — automatic replenishment. Job: don't think about reordering. Surface: razors, coffee, contact lenses, supplements.
6. **Surprise Box** — curated periodic delivery. Job: discovery + delight. Surface: beauty boxes, hobby boxes, wine clubs.
7. **Simplifier** — pay someone to handle a recurring task. Job: time recovery. Surface: lawn care, bookkeeping, payroll, IT managed services, agency retainers.
8. **Network** — value rises as more people use it. Job: network access. Surface: dating apps, professional networks, marketplaces.
9. **Peace-of-Mind** — pay against an event you hope never happens. Job: risk transfer. Surface: insurance, alarm monitoring, emergency-response retainers.

**Mechanism:** customers don't subscribe to a category — they subscribe to a job. Each of the nine maps to a different recurring job. Pick the wrong model and the customer churns because the job isn't actually recurring for them.

**Execution rule:** start from the customer's recurring job-to-be-done, not from your product. If the job isn't recurring, no model works. If it is, the job tells you which of the nine models fits. For productized services, the most accessible models are Simplifier and Peace-of-Mind.

**Failure mode:** picking a model because it's trendy ("everyone has a membership site"). Wrong model = high churn = the math never works regardless of how good the product is.

## Framework 3 — Negative Cash Flow Cycle Reversal

**What it is:** A diagnostic on which way cash flows between you and the customer over the life of the contract.

**Mechanism:** in a traditional services business, you deliver work first and get paid 30-60 days later. Cash is negative at the front of every project. In a healthy subscription business, the customer pays first — annual upfront, monthly auto-billed — and you deliver against the pre-paid commitment. Cash is positive at the front of every cycle.

**The math implication:** a subscription business with annual upfront billing can self-finance growth. Each new customer's first cheque funds the next customer's acquisition. Traditional services with net-30 billing require external capital to scale.

**Execution rule:** wherever possible, get paid before you deliver. Offer a 10-20% discount for annual upfront. Default to credit-card-on-file with monthly auto-charge. Use deposits and prepays. The lift is twofold: working capital improves AND multiple expands (per the Valuation Teeter-Totter).

**Failure mode:** founders accept their industry's billing convention as immutable. Billing convention is a valuation decision, not a customer-service decision.

## Framework 4 — Customer-Lifetime-Value vs Customer-Acquisition-Cost Math

**What it is:** The unit-economics test that determines whether a subscription business is healthy or just looks healthy.

**The two numbers:**
- **CAC** — fully-loaded cost of acquiring one paying subscriber (ad spend, sales labour, sales tools, onboarding).
- **LTV** — gross margin per subscriber multiplied by average lifetime in months (or 1 / monthly churn rate).

**The targets:**
- **LTV / CAC > 3** — healthy. You're earning at least $3 of gross profit per $1 of acquisition cost.
- **CAC payback < 12 months** — fundable. You recoup the acquisition cost inside a year.

**Mechanism:** subscription businesses front-load CAC (you spend to acquire) and earn LTV slowly (over months/years). If the ratio breaks, you're paying to acquire customers you'll lose before they become profitable. Scale makes the hole bigger, not smaller.

**Execution rule:** measure LTV / CAC monthly. If the ratio drops below 3 — stop acquisition. Fix retention or pricing before adding more customers. Growth funded by broken unit economics is destruction at scale.

**Failure mode:** founders measure top-line MRR growth without measuring the unit economics underneath it. MRR can grow while LTV / CAC silently collapses, which means the business is consuming cash faster than it's compounding value.

## Framework 5 — Churn as the Master Variable

**What it is:** A diagnostic that elevates churn above all other subscription metrics. Churn is the single number that determines whether a subscription business compounds or leaks.

**Mechanism:** monthly churn of 1% means an average customer lifetime of ~100 months. Monthly churn of 5% means an average lifetime of ~20 months. The same MRR with 5x the lifetime is worth roughly 5x as much. Churn is leverage on every other metric.

**Two churn types to track separately:**
- **Customer churn** — % of accounts cancelling.
- **Revenue churn** — % of MRR lost (net of expansion).

Net negative churn — where expansion revenue from existing customers exceeds revenue lost to cancellations — is the holy grail. It means the business grows even with zero new acquisition.

**Execution rule:** track churn weekly. Diagnose every cancellation. Implement a save-flow on cancel. Engineer expansion paths (upsell to a higher tier, add seats, add modules). Reducing monthly churn from 5% to 3% can lift business value more than doubling marketing spend.

**Failure mode:** treating churn as a customer-success problem. Churn is a product, pricing, and onboarding problem first. Customer-success teams patch the symptom, not the cause.

## Framework 6 — The Transition Path (transactional to recurring)

**What it is:** Staged migration framework for converting an existing transactional business into subscription without killing it.

**The four stages:**
1. **Identify the recurring job** — find the customer task or outcome that genuinely repeats. Don't manufacture recurrence; discover it.
2. **Package the recurring job as a tier** — fixed-scope, fixed-price monthly offer alongside the transactional offering. Don't kill the transactional business yet.
3. **Convert at the next purchase decision** — at renewal/repurchase, present subscription as default and transactional as alternative. Price so monthly × 10-12 = annual transactional cost.
4. **Sunset the transactional offer** — once subscription is >50% of revenue, retire the transactional offer for new customers. Grandfather existing ones.

**Mechanism:** founders try to "switch" overnight and crater cash flow. The staged transition lets existing revenue fund the recurring book build.

**Execution rule:** map your top three repeat customers. The thing they keep coming back for is the recurring job. Build subscription around that.

**Failure mode:** subscribe-ifying the wrong product — usually the easiest one to package — instead of the one the customer has a recurring job for. Low conversion, high churn.

## Framework 7 — Pricing Anchors in Subscription Businesses

**What it is:** A pricing-architecture framework specific to subscription. Three pricing decisions that compound: tier structure, annual-vs-monthly anchor, and price elasticity discovery.

**The three decisions:**
- **Tier structure** — three tiers (good / better / best). The middle tier should be the target. Tier 1 anchors low, Tier 3 anchors high, Tier 2 wins by contrast.
- **Annual vs monthly anchor** — display annual price prominently with a "billed monthly" sub-line. Annual pricing lifts retention (customers who paid annually don't churn mid-year) and lifts cash flow (you collect 12 months upfront).
- **Price elasticity discovery** — most subscription businesses underprice by 30-50%. Run a price test on new sign-ups every 6 months. If conversion doesn't drop, you were underpriced.

**Mechanism:** subscription customers anchor on the first price they see and rarely reconsider. The initial price decision compounds for the customer's full lifetime.

**Execution rule:** test pricing on new cohorts; never change pricing on existing customers without explicit grandfathering. Existing-customer price hikes drive churn spikes that often exceed the revenue gain.

**Failure mode:** picking a "round number" price and never testing it. Round numbers like $99 or $199 are cultural defaults, not optimised price points.

## Framework 8 — Subscription Fit Test (when NOT to subscribe-ify)

**What it is:** A four-question filter for whether subscription is the right model for a given business. Not every business should be a subscription.

**The four questions:**
1. **Does the customer have a recurring job?** If the job is one-off (buying a wedding ring, selling a house), subscription is forced.
2. **Is the cost to deliver each cycle low enough that the LTV/CAC math works?** Subscription is a margin business. If marginal delivery cost is high (custom builds, bespoke consulting), each cycle has to be re-priced.
3. **Can you predict and control churn?** If churn is driven by uncontrollable external factors (regulation, customer industry collapse), the cash flow predictability that justifies the multiple disappears.
4. **Will buyers underwrite your subscription revenue?** Subscription with high churn or weak contracts gets discounted toward services multiples. The multiple lift requires the predictability to actually exist, not just the billing label.

**Mechanism:** subscription as a business model is a load-bearing structure. The load is the customer's recurring job. Without that load, the structure collapses.

**Execution rule:** answer all four questions before the build. Three "yes" answers and one "no" is a redesign signal — fix the no before going further.

**Failure mode:** subscribing a business because subscription is fashionable. The business model has to match the customer's recurring reality.

## Framework 9 — Forecastability and the Multiple Bridge

**What it is:** The bridge between operational metrics (MRR, churn, LTV) and the multiple a buyer pays at exit.

**The forecastability ladder:**
- Month-to-month, no contracts → 1-2x ARR or services-multiple of EBITDA.
- Annual contracts with auto-renewal → 2-4x ARR.
- Multi-year contracts, locked terms → 4-7x ARR.
- Multi-year + net negative churn + 40%+ EBITDA margin → 7-10x+ ARR (Rule-of-40 zone).

**Mechanism:** each rung lowers buyer discount rate. Same MRR in different contract structures = different multiples.

**Execution rule:** for 24 months pre-exit, every contract renewal is a deliberate ladder move — month-to-month → annual → multi-year → multi-year-with-auto-renew. Each move compounds at exit.

**Failure mode:** running the business optimised for current MRR while ignoring the contract structures that drive the exit multiple. Two businesses with identical MRR can sell for 3x different prices based purely on contract structure.
