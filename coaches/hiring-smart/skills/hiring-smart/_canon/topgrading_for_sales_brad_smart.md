## Framework 1 — The Cost of a Sales Mis-Hire (Ch 1)

Brad's general *Topgrading* research pegs mis-hire cost at 5-27x salary depending on level. For SALES specifically, Brad puts the floor at **roughly 5x salary** — and that's the conservative read.

**What the 5x captures:**
- Base + variable comp paid during the failed tenure
- Onboarding, training, ramp investment
- Lost deals — opportunities the rep was assigned but didn't close
- Pipeline cleanup — accounts the rep damaged on the way out
- Manager time spent coaching, then managing out
- Recruiting cost for the replacement
- Re-ramp time for the next rep (typically 3-9 months in B2B)

**The hidden multiplier — territory damage:**
A failed rep doesn't just fail to close. They poison accounts. Buyers ghost the brand. Champions move on. The next rep inherits a colder territory. Brad's frame: a sales mis-hire in a $1M ARR territory routinely costs $500K+ in trailing damage you can't put on a spreadsheet.

**Operator implication:** the math on a "hire fast, fire fast" sales-hiring approach almost never works. The "fast" half collects a 5x bill before the "fire" half kicks in. Topgrading's slower, more expensive interview process pays for itself the first time it stops one mis-hire.

## Framework 2 — The Sales A-Player Profile (Ch 2)

Brad's A-player definition from the parent *Topgrading* book is "top 10%." Sales requires a sharper operationalization because the bottom and middle of the curve LOOK identical in interviews — every sales candidate is articulate, charming, and tells a good story.

**The Sales A-Player profile — 6 markers Brad uses:**

1. **Quota history** — hit or beat quota in 4+ of the last 5 years, in roles roughly comparable to the seat you're hiring for
2. **Promotion velocity** — moved up or moved laterally to bigger territories every 18-36 months
3. **President's Club / top-rank cadence** — recognized in the top 10-20% of their org repeatedly, not once
4. **Reference-able numbers** — can name specific deals, specific dollar amounts, specific close dates without rehearsal (and bosses confirm them)
5. **Customer relationships that outlast tenure** — buyers who follow them between companies
6. **Self-coaching loop** — actively studies their own losses, can articulate what they changed after a loss

**Failure pattern:** the candidate sounds great but only one of the six markers is real. Sales B-players are world-class storytellers about their A-player past. The Topgrading interview's chronological discipline is what separates the story from the record.

## Framework 3 — The Sales-Specific Scorecard (Ch 3)

Same Scorecard structure Geoff uses in *Who* (Mission / Outcomes / Competencies) but with sales-specific calibration on each axis.

**Mission — must specify the SALES MOTION:**
- Bad: "Sell our software to enterprise accounts."
- Good: "Close 8 net-new logos per year at $50K+ ACV in the immigration-law vertical via outbound + referral motion, with sales cycles under 90 days."

**Outcomes — must use sales math, not adjectives:**
- "Hit $X in net-new ARR per quarter by Q3"
- "Maintain pipeline coverage of 4x quota at all times"
- "Win rate of Y% on qualified opportunities"
- "Average sales cycle under Z days"

**Competencies — sales-specific behaviors:**
- Prospecting discipline (do they hunt cold, or only farm warm?)
- Deal qualification (do they walk away from bad-fit deals, or chase everything?)
- Negotiation posture (do they hold price, or discount to close?)
- Forecast accuracy (is their commit number believable?)
- Coachability (do they apply manager feedback within 1-2 calls?)

**Test:** if the Scorecard could describe ten different sales jobs, it's not done. A Scorecard is good when it describes ONE seat at ONE company at ONE moment.

## Framework 4 — The Topgrading Interview Adapted for Sales (Ch 4)

The 3-4 hour chronological interview from Brad's parent *Topgrading* book, recalibrated with sales-specific probes at each career stop.

**The sales-specific probes at each job in the career walk:**

1. **What was your quota?** (number + currency + period)
2. **What did you actually close?** (number, not "exceeded quota")
3. **What was your rank on the team?** (out of how many?)
4. **What was the largest deal you closed at this role?** (amount + sales cycle length + decision-maker title)
5. **What was the biggest deal you LOST?** (and why — the loss tells more than the win)
6. **What was your pipeline coverage ratio?** (do they even know this number?)
7. **How was your variable comp structured?** (and did you hit accelerators?)
8. **Why did you leave?** (cross-checked against the boss reference later)

**The diagnostic move:** ask for the same numbers in two different ways, 30 minutes apart. A-players' numbers match. B-players' numbers drift. C-players' numbers contradict.

**Pattern detection across the career walk:**
- A-player pattern: numbers tighten, deal sizes grow, quotas grow, rank stays top quartile
- B-player pattern: numbers vague, deals plateau, lateral moves with no rank improvement
- C-player pattern: gaps in quota history, "I exceeded" without a number, blame shifts to product/market/manager at every role

## Framework 5 — The Sales Reference Call (Ch 5)

Brad's TORC (Threat of Reference Check, from his parent book) is most lethal in sales because sales bosses have HARD numbers. They know the quota, the attainment, the rank.

**The sales-specific reference questions:**

1. "What was [candidate's] quota that year, and what percentage of it did they hit?"
2. "Where did they rank on the team?"
3. "What was their largest closed deal — amount, customer name, sales cycle?"
4. "Did you ever put them on a Performance Improvement Plan? Why?"
5. "Would you hire them back? At what level?"
6. "On a scale of 1-10, where 10 means top performer on your team — where did they fall?"
7. "What kind of selling environment do they thrive in? Where do they struggle?"

**The killer move:** ask the candidate to set up the reference call. The candidate's calibration of their own performance against what the reference actually says is the highest-signal moment in the entire process.

**Sales-specific red flag:** the candidate says "I exceeded quota every year." The reference says "they hit quota 2 out of 5 years." That's not a memory issue. That's an integrity issue. Pass.

## Framework 6 — The Quota-Hit-Rate History (Ch 6)

Brad's strongest single predictive variable for sales hires: **the candidate's quota attainment over the last 5 years**, role by role.

**The math:**
- 4-5 of last 5 years at 100%+ quota → strong A-player signal
- 3 of last 5 years at 100%+ → B-player territory; dig deeper
- 2 or fewer of last 5 years at 100%+ → C-player territory; do not hire as a closer

**Adjustments Brad applies:**
- Was the candidate in territories with reasonable quotas, or did they get gifted easy numbers?
- Did they hit quota by closing large unfair deals (inheritance) or by building a real book?
- Did they hit quota in DIFFERENT companies, or only one (where the brand carried them)?

**The transferability test:** an A-player ships at multiple companies, in multiple roles, against multiple managers. A B-player ships in one specific environment that fit them. The Scorecard must specify whether the role is the same environment or a different one — because B-players don't transfer.

## Framework 7 — The Sales Manager Interview Overlay (Ch 7)

When the hire is a SALES MANAGER (not a rep), Brad layers additional probes on top of the standard chronological interview.

**The sales manager-specific probes:**

1. **Team build history** — how many sales reps have you hired? How many of them hit quota in their first year? In their second?
2. **Mis-hire rate** — how many of your hires have you had to manage out within 12 months?
3. **Coaching cadence** — what's your standard 1-on-1 structure? Pipeline review cadence?
4. **Pipeline forecast accuracy** — when you told your boss "I'll close $X this quarter," how often were you within 10%?
5. **Top performer retention** — of your team's top 20% in any given year, how many were still on the team 24 months later?
6. **What do you do when a rep is at 60% of quota at mid-year?**

**The cascade insight:** a sales manager who can't articulate their own hiring methodology will repeat your hiring pain at scale. They'll hire B-players because they ARE a B-player. Brad's frame from the parent *Topgrading* book — "A's hire A's, B's hire C's" — is operative here: a B-player sales manager is a C-player problem 18 months out.

## Framework 8 — Sales Coaching as A-Player Retention (Ch 8)

Hiring the A-player is half the job. Keeping them is the other half. Brad's argument: A-player sales reps stay where they're CHALLENGED, not where they're comfortable.

**The retention mechanism Brad calibrates:**

1. **Stretch targets** — A-players want quotas they can hit at 110%, not 200%. Set the bar where they have to grow but can win.
2. **Coaching depth** — A-players want a manager who can teach them something. A non-selling manager loses A-players first.
3. **Comp ceiling** — uncapped variable comp matters more to A-players than base. A capped plan signals "we don't want top performers."
4. **Career path visibility** — A-players want to know what the next seat looks like, and when. Vague answers lose them.
5. **Peer quality** — A-players will not stay on a team of B's and C's. The fastest A-player retention strategy is to upgrade the rest of the team.

**The 18-month retention check:** for any A-player closer, plan their next seat at month 12. If they don't see a path to bigger territory, more responsibility, or a manager seat, they're out by month 24.
