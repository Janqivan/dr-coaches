## Framework 1 — What Positioning IS (the working definition)

**Dunford's working definition (from her blog):**

> "Positioning defines what market you intend to win and why you deserve to win it."
> — https://www.aprildunford.com/post/an-introduction-to-positioning

**In own words:** positioning is two strategic decisions made deliberately — (1) which market context you compete inside, (2) what makes you the obvious choice within that context. Everything downstream — copy, demos, pricing, homepage — is execution on those two.

**The analogy she uses publicly:**

> "Positioning is like context-setting for products."
> — same source as above

**When to apply:** every time someone says "we need new messaging" or "we need a rebrand," check upstream first. Messaging is downstream of positioning. If positioning is broken, no amount of new copy fixes it.

## Framework 2 — What Positioning IS NOT (the five common confusions)

Dunford spends meaningful airtime in every interview clarifying what positioning isn't. The five most-repeated confusions:

1. **Positioning ≠ messaging.** Messaging is the words on the page. Positioning is the strategic choice the words flow from.
2. **Positioning ≠ tagline.** A tagline is a single line. Positioning is a multi-dimensional strategic artifact.
3. **Positioning ≠ brand story / vision / mission.** Those describe who you are. Positioning describes how the market should evaluate you.
4. **Positioning ≠ branding.** Branding is the visual identity and personality. Positioning is the competitive frame.
5. **Positioning ≠ a marketing department deliverable.** Positioning is a leadership-team artifact that requires CEO, sales, product, and marketing all in the room.

**Public source for #5:**
> "If you think positioning is a marketing exercise, you're doing it wrong."
> — https://www.aprildunford.com/post/if-you-think-positioning-is-a-marketing-exercise-youre-doing-it-wrong

**When to apply:** when someone in the room equates positioning with copywriting, stop and clarify. The conversation is happening at the wrong layer of the stack.

## Framework 3 — The 10-Step Positioning Process (as taught publicly)

Dunford teaches the 5-Step Process in her book and a 10-Step version in workshops and public talks. The 10-Step is the operational expansion.

**Operator's view of the 10 steps (synthesized from her public talks):**
1. Identify best-fit customers (close fast, stay, refer)
2. Form a positioning team — CEO, sales, product, marketing
3. List true competitive alternatives, including do-nothing and in-house build
4. Isolate unique attributes only you have
5. Map each attribute to a buyer-side value
6. Cluster values into 2-4 value themes
7. Define characteristics of customers who care most about each value theme
8. Pick the market category that frames your attributes as obvious
9. Layer in a relevant trend (optional)
10. Capture in a one-page canvas; brief product, sales, marketing, CS off the same canvas

**Public anchor:** the process is referenced across most podcast appearances; Lenny's June 2022 episode walks it most thoroughly — https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/april-dunford-on-product-positioning-segmentation-and-optimizing-your-sales-process

**When to apply:** any time the operator says "I want to redo our positioning." Don't let them start at step 8 (category) or step 10 (canvas). The early steps do all the work.

## Framework 4 — Alternatives-Driven Framing

**Concept:** the buyer's competitive set is NOT your competitor set. The buyer compares you to whatever they would do if you didn't exist — which is usually NOT another vendor in your category.

**Public source:**
> "The competitive alternatives... are often things that don't look like direct competitors."
> — https://www.aprildunford.com/post/an-introduction-to-positioning

**Three alternative archetypes Dunford repeats publicly:**
1. **Do nothing** — the manual workaround, the spreadsheet, "we just live with it"
2. **Build it ourselves** — internal dev / ops team takes it on
3. **A product in a different category** — the buyer hacks together something that wasn't built for the job

**Operator implication:** if your positioning treats "the leading competitor" as the alternative, but the buyer's actual alternative is "keep using Excel," your differentiated value statements are aimed at the wrong target. You'll out-feature a competitor the buyer wasn't comparing you to.

**When to apply:** during win/loss interviews and discovery calls — always ask "what else were you considering?" before "why us?" The "what else" answer reveals the real alternative.

## Framework 5 — Customer's Worldview as the Frame

**Concept:** the sales pitch's first job is to align the customer to YOUR worldview of the market — your point of view about how to evaluate solutions, what matters, what doesn't. You don't sell the product until the customer agrees with the worldview.

**Public anchor:** the *Sales-First Storytelling* Substack post (https://aprildunford.substack.com/p/sales-first-storytelling) and her *Market Insight* Substack post (https://aprildunford.substack.com/p/market-insight-and-communicating).

**Mechanism:**
- Step 1 — share an insight that reframes how to think about the market
- Step 2 — walk the alternatives honestly
- Step 3 — agree on what an ideal solution would look like
- Only THEN — introduce your product as the obvious instance of that ideal

**When to apply:** any sales pitch that opens with "let me tell you about our company" or "let me give you a demo of our product." You're skipping the worldview alignment and the buyer will revert to the incumbent's frame.

## Framework 6 — Best-Fit Customers (the segmentation move)

**Concept:** segmentation does not start with TAM, demographics, or firmographics. It starts with the customers you already have who close fast, stay, refer, and pay full price. Reverse-engineer their characteristics.

**Reverse-engineering walk (synthesized from her public interviews):**
1. List the customers who closed fastest
2. Cross-reference: stayed longest, paid full price, referred others
3. Strip out firmographic and behavioral commonalities
4. Strip out the trigger event that brought them to you
5. That intersection is your best-fit definition

**Public anchor:** Lenny's 2022 episode (most thorough segmentation treatment); also https://www.howtoweb.co/5-questions-with-april-dunford-author-and-product-positioning-expert-part-1/

**Tightness rule (paraphrased from public talks):** if you can't name 3-5 real companies that fit the best-fit profile, the profile is too vague.

**When to apply:** any time the operator says "our target market is small-to-mid B2B SaaS." That's not a segment. Best-fit should be narrower — to the point of feeling uncomfortable.

## Framework 7 — The Market Category Choice (not the starting point)

**Concept:** the market category you compete in is one of the most consequential strategic choices a company makes — but it is the LAST output of the positioning process, not the first input.

**Public anchor:**
> "Everyone understands that your choice of market category is an essential strategic decision."
> — Dunford LinkedIn post, https://www.linkedin.com/posts/aprildunford_everyone-understands-that-your-choice-of-activity-7019006135944830976-YRI5

**The three deliberate choices Dunford articulates publicly:**
1. Position into an existing category
2. Position against the dominant approach inside an existing category
3. Create a new category

**Why this comes LAST in the process:** the category choice depends on (a) which unique attributes you actually have, (b) what value those produce, (c) for whom that value is most urgent. You can't pick the category until you know those three. Founders who start with "we're a new category" usually back-fill the rest and produce default positioning.

**See also:** the *Understanding the Job of a Market Category* Substack post (https://aprildunford.substack.com/p/understanding-the-job-of-a-market) — Dunford's clearest public treatment of why category is the FRAME, not the THING.

**When to apply:** when a founder leads with "we're inventing a new category." Push back. Walk steps 1-7 first. If the category claim still holds after that, it's a real claim.

## Framework 8 — Common Positioning Mistakes (failure modes she names publicly)

The 5-6 failure modes Dunford returns to across her public interviews:

1. **Default positioning** — never made a deliberate choice; category inherited from whatever the first customer called the product
2. **Wrong competitive alternatives** — listed the competitors they wished they had, not what the buyer actually considered
3. **Feature-stuffing the value section** — listing what the product does instead of what the buyer gets
4. **Best-fit too broad** — segment so wide no sales script or marketing copy can be sharpened against it
5. **Picking category first** — fell in love with a category narrative and back-filled the rest
6. **Positioning lives only in marketing** — sales, product, CS never bought in; the positioning shows up only on the homepage

**Anchors:**
- Marketing-only failure: https://www.aprildunford.com/post/if-you-think-positioning-is-a-marketing-exercise-youre-doing-it-wrong
- Alternatives failure: https://www.aprildunford.com/post/positioning-and-competition

**When to apply:** any positioning diagnosis. Run against these 6 failure modes first — the real problem is usually one of these, not what the operator thinks.

## Framework 9 — Differentiated Value Is The Spine

**Concept:** differentiated value — what your product does for the customer that no listed alternative can do — is the strategic spine that connects positioning to sales pitch to marketing copy to product roadmap.

**Public source:**
> "Differentiated value is what we can do for a customer that no other alternative solution can."
> — paraphrased across Dunford's BoS 2023 talk
> https://businessofsoftware.org/talks/your-differentiated-value/

**Why "the spine":** every downstream artifact needs to ladder back to the differentiated value. The sales pitch is built around it (steps 5-6 of the storyboard). The homepage hero is the compressed version of it. The product roadmap exists to extend it.

**Operator failure mode:** differentiated value gets articulated once during a workshop, written on a slide, and then forgotten. Six months later marketing, sales, and product are each emphasizing different value claims and the buyer experiences incoherence.

**When to apply:** test every piece of customer-facing collateral against the question "does this ladder back to our differentiated value themes?" If a piece can't be traced back, it's drift.

## Framework 10 — Repositioning as a Fundamental Business Change

**Concept:** changing positioning is not a marketing project. It is a fundamental change to the business — it shifts who you compete with, what buyers expect from you, how you price, and what sales channels work.

**Public anchor (paraphrased from her positioning consultancy page):**
- A change in positioning changes the buyers' competitive comparisons
- Pricing expectations shift
- Sales channels and sales motion may need to shift
- Product roadmap needs to be rechecked against the new positioning

**Source:** https://www.aprildunford.com/consultant

**Operator implication:** before greenlighting a repositioning project, the leadership team should be ready for the downstream consequences. Otherwise the new positioning will be relabeled and the business will keep operating off the old one.

**When to apply:** any time someone says "we just need to reposition." Audit downstream readiness first — pricing, sales motion, roadmap, channel.

## Framework 11 — Positioning Across Multiple Products / Segments / Personas

**Concept:** when a company grows beyond one product or one segment, positioning multiplies. Each product needs its own positioning, but they all need to roll up to a coherent corporate-level frame.

**Public anchor:** https://aprildunford.substack.com/p/a-guide-to-positioning-with-multiple

**Two failure modes Dunford calls out:**
1. **Corporate-level mush** — generic "platform" story that says nothing about any product
2. **Disconnected product-level stories** — each product positioned in isolation, no coherent worldview

**The fix:** each product has its own positioning canvas AND a corporate-level frame ties them together. The connective tissue is usually the worldview from step 1 of the sales pitch.

**When to apply:** any multi-product B2B company, any platform play, any agency with multiple service lines.

## Framework 12 — The Investor Pitch Trap

**Concept:** founders raise capital with a specific pitch shape — TAM, problem, solution, traction, team, ask. That shape is wrong for customers. Founders who default to the investor pitch when selling to customers lose.

**Public anchor:** https://www.aprildunford.com/post/why-your-investor-pitch-is-ruining-your-sales-pitch

**Why the investor pitch fails customers:**
- Investors evaluate the COMPANY; customers evaluate the SOLUTION
- TAM is irrelevant to a single customer
- "Why now" is macro for investors; for customers it has to be specific to them
- The investor pitch glorifies the founder/team; customers don't care

**The fix:** keep both pitches. Investor pitch for capital, sales pitch (8-step storyboard) for customers. Don't conflate them.

**When to apply:** any founder-led sales motion where the founder is still using their VC deck on customer calls.
