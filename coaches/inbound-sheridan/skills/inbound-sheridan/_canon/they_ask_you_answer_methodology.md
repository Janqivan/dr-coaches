This is the operating system. Everything else in the Sheridan corpus is

## The One-Sentence Version

Obsessively answer the questions, fears, worries, and concerns of your
buyer — on your own website, in text and video, more honestly than
anyone else in your industry. Then teach your sales team to use that
content during the sales process.

That's the whole system. The rest is detail.

## The Origin Story (Why This Matters)

2008. I co-owned River Pools and Spas in Warsaw, Virginia. The housing
market collapsed. Sixty percent of our buyers cancelled orders in a
single quarter. The bank account hit overdraft three Fridays in a row.
I had four kids and a wife. I cried in my truck more than once.

We couldn't afford ads anymore. So I read every book I could find on
inbound marketing — Brian Halligan and Dharmesh Shah at HubSpot were
just figuring it out then. The idea: stop interrupting people, start
being found by people who already had the question.

I asked myself one question: "What does a buyer who is thinking about
buying an inground pool actually type into Google?" Not what I wished
they typed. What they actually typed.

The answer wasn't "best pool company in Virginia." It was things like:

- How much does a fiberglass pool cost?
- Fiberglass vs concrete vs vinyl liner — which is best?
- Problems with fiberglass pools
- Is a saltwater pool worth it?
- Best fiberglass pool brands

So I started writing articles. One after another. Three a week. Just
me, at the kitchen table, after the kids went to bed, typing the
answers I had given a hundred times in person to a hundred different
buyers.

Within 18 months we were the most-trafficked pool website on the
internet. We became the best-known pool company in the world, run out
of a town nobody had heard of. The single article "How much does a
fiberglass pool cost?" generated over $35 million in tracked revenue.

That's TAYA. That's where the whole thing came from. Not a marketing
theory. A panicked guy at a kitchen table answering questions because
he had nothing left to lose.

## Why Most Marketing Fails

Most marketing fails because it answers questions buyers don't have.

Walk into any "about us" page. Read the copy. "We were founded in 1987
with a passion for excellence and a commitment to our values." Nobody
searches for that. Nobody cares.

Walk into any services page. "We offer best-in-class solutions tailored
to your needs." Nobody searches for that either.

The marketing department is writing for the marketing department.
Meanwhile, the actual buyer is on Google at 10pm typing real questions
into the search bar — and a competitor (or worse, a random affiliate
blog, or worse still, a Reddit thread) is the one giving them answers.

The buyer's brain works in three phases:

1. **Awareness** — "I have a problem / I want something."
2. **Consideration** — "What are my options? What does it cost?
   What could go wrong? Who's the best?"
3. **Decision** — "Which one do I pick? Why this one and not the other?"

Most company websites only address phase 3 — and badly. They skip
phases 1 and 2, which is where the buyer spends 80% of the buying
journey. So buyers never find them. And when they do find them, they
don't trust them, because the site reads like a brochure instead of
a teacher.

TAYA fixes this by deliberately producing content for all three phases,
weighted heavily toward consideration — where buyers are actually
spending their time.

## The Five Buyer Behaviors That Killed Old-School Sales

Today's buyer:

1. Researches obsessively before talking to a salesperson — 70%+ of the
   purchase decision is made before the first sales conversation.
2. Trusts peer reviews and Google more than your salespeople.
3. Wants pricing transparency. Hiding pricing screams "I'm hiding
   something else too."
4. Wants to see real people. Stock photos lower trust, they don't
   raise it.
5. Will compare you to every competitor whether you want them to or
   not. The only question is whether YOU shape the comparison or
   whether someone else does.

A sales/marketing engine built before 2010 assumes buyer ignorance was
a profitable state. That stopped being true around 2012. Consumer
ignorance is no longer a viable sales and marketing strategy. The
companies that figure this out win. The ones that don't get squeezed
into commodity pricing and slow death.

## The TAYA Implementation Sequence

This is the order. Do not skip steps. Do not do them in parallel until
you have step 1 nailed.

### Step 1 — Leadership Buy-In (THE step most companies skip)

The CEO has to actually believe in this. Not nod and approve a budget.
Believe. Because TAYA requires publishing things competitors don't
publish — pricing, problems, honest comparisons. The first time
something gets published that scares the sales team, the CEO has to
back the play. Without that, the program dies in week six.

If the CEO won't commit to publishing prices, naming competitors, and
talking about problems on camera — don't start. Go fix that conversation
first.

### Step 2 — Hire the Content Manager

ONE full-time in-house person. Not a freelancer. Not an agency. Not the
marketing director's "other duty as assigned."

Why in-house: subject-matter experts (your sales reps, installers,
service techs, lawyers, dentists) will not give an outsider the time
of day. They'll give a coworker 30 minutes. The whole engine runs on
the content manager being able to interview SMEs weekly, so they have
to be in the building or on the team chat.

The hire takes about six weeks. Look for: strong writer or video
producer, organized, comfortable pushing busy subject-matter experts
for time, curious, willing to be on camera themselves eventually.

Once hired, they start producing in week one. Publishing real articles
in week two. The best onboarding is the work itself.

### Step 3 — Define the Big 5 Article Backlog

In a single 90-minute session with the sales team and the content
manager, list every question a buyer has ever asked, fitted into the
five categories:

- Cost / pricing questions
- Problems / "what could go wrong" questions
- Comparisons (us vs them, A vs B)
- Best-of (best X in Y category)
- Reviews (honest reviews of vendors, products, services)

You'll end up with 80-150 article topics from one session. That's a
two-year backlog. That's the editorial calendar.

### Step 4 — Publish on Cadence

3 articles a week minimum during the first 6 months. After that, 2
per week sustained, with at least 1 video shipping per week as well.

Cadence beats brilliance. A 1,200-word answer to a real buyer question,
published weekly, outperforms a 4,000-word "pillar piece" published
quarterly every time.

### Step 5 — Train Sales on Assignment Selling

Bring sales into the editorial process from day one. They feed
questions in. They review drafts. Then they ASSIGN the content to
prospects.

"Before our call Tuesday, please read these two articles and watch
this 4-minute video. They answer the questions you're going to ask
anyway. If you can do that, we'll have a great call."

Track: did the prospect actually consume the assigned content? At
River Pools, prospects who consumed 30+ pieces of content closed at
80%. Prospects who consumed less closed at 25%. Same product, same
team, same offer. The variable is content consumption.

### Step 6 — Add Video (The Selling 7)

Once written content is humming (month 3-6), start adding video.
Start with the 80% video (the 7-10 questions every prospect asks)
and bio videos of the actual sales team. Build from there.

### Step 7 — Measure What Matters

Vanity metrics: traffic, rankings.
Real metrics:
- Influenced revenue (prospects who consumed content × close rate × AOV)
- Sales cycle length (TAYA cuts this 20-40% typically)
- Close rate on assignment-sold prospects vs cold prospects
- Cost per acquired customer (drops as organic compounds)

## Hiring the Content Manager — The Scorecard

Outcomes (what success looks like in 12 months):

- 100+ articles published, 90% answering Big 5 questions
- 30+ videos published, including 80% video and full Selling 7 starter set
- Sales team using content in assignment selling on 100% of opportunities
- Organic traffic up 3-5x; influenced pipeline up 2-3x

Competencies:

- Writes clean, plain-English copy at a sub-10th-grade reading level
- Interviews subject-matter experts and extracts what's in their head
- Comfortable on camera and behind the camera
- Project manager — owns the editorial calendar, ships on cadence
- Curious about the industry — willing to learn the trade

Compensation: market rate for a senior content marketer. In the US in
2026, that's typically $75K-$110K base depending on market. Cheaper
than the wrong hire and far cheaper than an agency retainer that
produces brochure copy.

Where to find them: journalism backgrounds, B2B content marketers,
industry-adjacent writers. Not "creative" agency writers. You want a
reporter, not a poet.

## The Four Pillars (Endless Customers 2025 Update)

After 15+ years of running TAYA across thousands of companies, the
framework evolved. In Endless Customers I distilled it into four
pillars. TAYA is still the engine. The pillars are how to evaluate
whether you're actually doing it.

### Pillar 1: Say What Others Won't Say

Pricing. Problems. Honest comparisons. Naming competitors. The reasons
NOT to buy from you. The buyer is finding this out anyway. The only
question is whether the source is you (and earns trust) or someone
else (and you become the commodity).

### Pillar 2: Show What Others Won't Show

Real video. Real faces. The actual installation crew. The actual
office. The actual service tech showing the actual problem. Stock
photos are trust-destroyers in 2026 — buyers can spot them in half
a second.

### Pillar 3: Sell Differently

Assignment selling. Self-service pricing calculators. AI-driven
self-qualification tools. The buyer wants to do 80% of the work
themselves. Let them. Then your sales call is for the 20% that
actually matters — the conversation, not the brochure recital.

### Pillar 4: Be More Human Than Your Competition

In a world drowning in AI-generated content, the human voice wins.
Use AI to draft. Use humans to publish. Never let AI publish under
your name without a human SME's voice in it. The competitor who
publishes 100 AI articles a month with no human in the loop is
about to discover that buyers can smell that from a mile away.

## Common Objections (And How To Push Back)

**"Our industry is too regulated for this."**
Lawyers, doctors, dentists, financial advisors — they all said this.
They were all wrong. There's always a version of the Big 5 that
fits the regulator. "How much does an EB-5 visa cost" is a legal,
publishable, compliant article. Don't hide behind compliance to
avoid the work.

**"Our pricing is too variable to publish."**
Pools vary from $30K to $200K depending on size, options, site
conditions. We published the range, the factors, the "what makes
a pool more expensive" framework. That one page made $35M. Range
+ factors + honesty wins. Refusing to discuss it loses.

**"Our competitors will see what we're saying."**
They will. They won't have the courage to copy you, because the
courage IS the moat. Anyone can read your articles. Almost nobody
will commit to producing 200 of their own.

**"We don't have time to make content."**
You have time to make sales calls where you say the same things
over and over. Each of those is a podcast episode or article. The
work is already happening. TAYA just records it so it scales.

**"Our buyer doesn't read."**
They read at 11pm in bed on their phone before they ever talk to
you. You just don't see that because the analytics for that visit
were unattributed. Trust me. They read.
