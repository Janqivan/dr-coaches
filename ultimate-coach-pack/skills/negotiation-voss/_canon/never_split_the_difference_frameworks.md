## Framework 1 — Tactical Empathy (Ch 3, master frame)

**What it is:** Understanding the other side's emotional state and demonstrating that understanding out loud — without agreeing with their position. Empathy as intelligence-gathering, not as concession.

**Distinction Voss draws:**
- Sympathy = "I feel your pain" (concession)
- Empathy = "I see your pain" (acknowledgment)
- Tactical empathy = "I see your pain, I name it, and I use that to find what's actually driving you" (operational)

**Execution surface:** mirrors and labels (Ch 2 and Ch 3). Tactical empathy is the philosophy; mirrors and labels are the verbal tools that execute it.

**Failure mode:** trying to LOGIC someone out of an emotional position. Voss's hard rule — once emotion is in the room, you must process it before you can transact. Skipping this step is why most negotiations stall.

## Framework 2 — Mirroring (Ch 2)

**What it is:** Repeat the last 1-3 words the other party said, as a question, with downward inflection.

**Mechanism:**
1. Forces them to elaborate without you asking a question.
2. Buys 7-10 seconds of think-time without dead air.
3. Signals you're listening without agreeing.
4. Disarms aggression — it's hard to stay angry at someone repeating your own words back softly.

**Voice rule:** late-night FM DJ. Lower your voice. Slow it down. Down-inflection at the end (not up — up sounds confrontational).

**When to use:**
- Someone makes a hostile or extreme statement → mirror it
- You need information they didn't volunteer → mirror their last claim
- You need time → mirror anything, think while they elaborate

**Failure mode:** mirroring with rising inflection (sounds sarcastic). Mirroring more than 3 words (sounds like parroting).

## Framework 3 — Labeling (Ch 3)

**What it is:** A verbal acknowledgment of the OTHER party's emotion or position, named explicitly.

**Sentence stems (use only these three — never "I feel" or "I understand"):**
- "It seems like..."
- "It sounds like..."
- "It looks like..."

**Mechanism:**
- Naming a negative emotion drains some of its charge (Voss cites the neuroscience: amygdala activity drops when an emotion is named).
- Naming a positive emotion reinforces it.
- Demonstrating you can name their state without judgment makes you safe to talk to.

**Critical execution detail:** after the label, **shut up**. Silence does the work. The other party will rush to clarify, correct, or expand. That clarification is the data you needed.

**Failure mode:** labeling and then immediately following with a pitch ("It seems like you're frustrated, BUT let me show you..."). This kills the label. Label + silence. Always.

## Framework 4 — The Accusation Audit (Ch 3)

**What it is:** Pre-emptively listing every negative thing the other party could be thinking about you, and saying those things out loud — first.

**Mechanism:**
- Hidden objections control the negotiation invisibly. Visible objections can be addressed.
- Saying the worst thing first neutralizes it. The other party either nods (now you can move on) or denies (now you know it wasn't the real objection).
- Steals their thunder — they can't accuse you of something you already admitted.

**Format:**
> "You're probably going to think this is way more expensive than the last agency you used. You're going to wonder if we're going to disappear after the kickoff like the others did. You might think we're full of shit because every agency promised the same thing before us."

Then silence. Then a calibrated question.

**Where DR uses it:**
- Sales calls with previously-burned law firm managing partners
- Pricing conversations on the Case Flow installation offer
- Any first call where prior agencies have failed the prospect

## Framework 5 — No as Safety (Ch 4)

**Voss's break from Harvard:** "Getting to Yes" treats yes as the destination. Voss treats yes as a trap, and no as the start of safety.

**Three kinds of yes:**
1. **Counterfeit yes** — said to end the conversation. Will reverse.
2. **Confirmation yes** — neutral acknowledgment ("yes I heard you").
3. **Commitment yes** — the real one. Rare. You earn it.

**Why no is safer:**
- "No" gives the other party a sense of control. With control, they relax. Relaxed people negotiate better.
- "No" is reversible — they can change their mind. "Yes" feels like a trap they can't escape.

**Engineering for no:**
- Ask "no-oriented" questions: "Is now a bad time to talk?" beats "Do you have a minute?"
- "Have you given up on getting this resolved?" — gets a fast "no" that re-engages them.
- "Is it ridiculous to think...?" — invites a "no" that means "yes, your idea has merit".

## Framework 6 — "That's Right" vs "You're Right" (Ch 5)

**The win condition of any negotiation:** you summarize the other party's view so accurately that they say "that's right." Not "you're right."

**The distinction:**
- **"You're right"** — said to make you go away. Disengagement. Polite surrender to end the conversation. You've lost.
- **"That's right"** — said because you've nailed their internal model. Engagement. They now trust you understand them. Real negotiation can begin.

**How to get there:**
1. Use mirrors and labels to gather everything they've said and felt.
2. Summarize back in 1-2 sentences using their own words and emotional frame.
3. End with: "...does that capture it?"
4. Wait for "that's right." If you get "you're right" — you missed something. Go back to labels.

## Framework 7 — Bend Their Reality (Ch 6)

**Voss's loss-aversion frame:** people are 2x more motivated to avoid loss than to achieve equivalent gain. Most negotiators pitch gain ("you'll get X"). Voss pitches loss ("you'll lose X if you don't").

**Tactics inside this chapter:**
- **Anchor the emotion before the price.** Make them imagine the loss vividly before any number is on the table.
- **Let them go first when you have no information.** First number is the anchor.
- **Set a range, not a number, when you're forced to go first.** And anchor high.
- **Use precise numbers** — $37,893 is more credible than $38,000 because it sounds researched.
- **Throw in a non-monetary item at the close.** A surprise concession that costs you little but signals goodwill, used to land the final number.

## Framework 8 — Calibrated Questions (Ch 7)

**What they are:** open-ended questions starting with "how" or "what" that put the burden of the next move on the other party.

**Hard rules:**
- Never start with "why" — it's accusatory in every language.
- Never use "should", "could", or "would" inside — they invite lies and hedges.
- Never use yes/no questions — they end the conversation, not extend it.

**The master list (memorize these — Voss's actual go-tos):**
- "How am I supposed to do that?" — refuses an unreasonable demand without saying no.
- "What about this is important to you?" — surfaces their real driver.
- "How would you like me to proceed?" — passes responsibility for the next move.
- "What's the biggest challenge you face?" — opens Black Swan territory.
- "How does this affect things?" — checks downstream impact.
- "What needs to happen for this to work?" — invites them to define the deal.
- "How can we solve this problem?" — collaboration framing.

**Why it works:** illusion of control. The other party feels they're driving. They're actually walking into the frame you set with the question.

## Framework 9 — The 7-38-55 Rule + Rule of Three + Pinocchio Effect (Ch 8)

**7-38-55** (sourced from Mehrabian's research, used by Voss):
- 7% of communication = words
- 38% = tone of voice
- 55% = body language and facial expression

Implication for negotiation: when words and tone disagree, trust tone. When tone and body disagree, trust body. The literal content of what someone says is the least reliable signal.

**Rule of Three:** get the other party to agree to the same thing in three different ways before you believe them.
1. First yes — neutral.
2. Second yes (rephrased) — slight increase in commitment.
3. Third yes (in a question form, e.g. "so I can count on this Friday?") — only then is the commitment real.

**Pinocchio Effect:** liars use more words and more third-person pronouns than truth-tellers. When the other party gets verbose and starts referring to "we", "the team", "they" — flag it. They may be hiding a Black Swan.

## Framework 10 — The Ackerman Model (Ch 9, bargaining math)

**Setup:** You have a target price. Call it $100K.

**Six-step protocol:**
1. **Set your target.** Be honest with yourself about the real number.
2. **Open at 65% of target.** ($65K). This anchors low without being insulting.
3. **Plan three escalations: 85%, 95%, 100%.** ($85K, $95K, $100K). Each escalation gets smaller — signals "I'm reaching my limit" without saying so.
4. **Between each move, use calibrated questions and empathy.** Don't just lob numbers. Force them to justify their counter using "how" questions.
5. **Use non-round numbers at the final.** $99,847, not $100,000. Non-round signals research and difficulty — the number feels like a hard floor.
6. **Throw in a non-monetary item at close.** A small surprise concession to land the deal. Costs you little, but signals you're stretched.

**Why these specific percentages:** they create the optical illusion of generous concessions while keeping you in control of the path to your real target.

## Framework 11 — Finding the Black Swans (Ch 10)

**The three Black Swans:** Voss claims every negotiation has three unknown unknowns — facts that, if you knew them, would change everything.

**Categories of Black Swans:**
1. **Why they're really negotiating now** (the deadline behind the deadline)
2. **Who else has to say yes** (the decision-maker not in the room)
3. **What they CAN'T do** even if they want to (the constraint they can't disclose)

**How to hunt them:**
- Face-to-face when possible. 55% of communication is non-verbal.
- Calibrated questions specifically targeted at "their world" — "what's the biggest challenge you face in this deal?"
- Listen for what's NOT said. Topics they avoid. Subjects they change.
- Watch for inconsistencies between two meetings, two emails, two statements.
- Pay attention to the receptionist, the assistant, the in-between conversations. Side characters often have the Black Swan.

**Voss's bar:** if you don't think you know all three Black Swans, you don't. Keep hunting before you close.
