## Research backdrop (the operator's view)

The JOLT Effect is built on a conversational AI analysis of **2.5 million recorded B2B sales calls** (analyzed in partnership with Tethr). The single largest data set on sales conversation behavior in print. Two findings drove the book:

1. **40 to 60% of "lost" qualified deals were not lost to a competitor.** They were lost to **no decision** — the customer chose status quo. This is a different loss type than competitive loss, with a different cause and a different fix.

2. **The driver of "no decision" is not skepticism — it's indecision.** Specifically, **Fear of Messing Up (FOMU)** — the customer is worried they'll personally pick the wrong vendor, the wrong configuration, the wrong moment. They're not unsure of the rep. They're unsure of themselves.

The JOLT Method is the four-step playbook the high performers in the 2.5M-call data set used to convert indecisive customers. Win rates lifted from a baseline 14% on indecisive deals to 36% when JOLT was applied — more than 2.5x.

## Diagnostic — Skepticism vs Indecision

Before the JOLT method works, the rep has to diagnose what they're actually facing. The book's central operational claim: **most reps treat indecision as if it were skepticism, and the standard "build more value, send more proof" response makes it worse.**

| Signal | Skepticism | Indecision |
|--------|-----------|------------|
| What they doubt | YOU (your firm, your product) | THEMSELVES (their ability to choose well) |
| What they ask for | Proof, references, case studies, ROI math | More time, more options, more research |
| Body language in conversation | Engaged, pointed questions, push back on specifics | Polite, agreeable, vague, asks for "next steps" without committing |
| What "build value" does | Reduces it | Amplifies it (more information = more options = more freeze) |
| What FOMO ("you'll lose ___") does | Often closes the deal | Backfires 87% of the time — amplifies FOMU |
| What "take risk off" does | Doesn't change much | Closes the deal |

**Hard rule from the book:** if the customer is asking for more case studies on call 4, that's not skepticism. That's indecision masquerading as due diligence. Stop sending case studies. Start JOLTing.

## The Three Sources of Indecision (JOLT Effect, Ch 1-3)

Indecision has three drivers. The book labels them and ties each to a specific JOLT move.

1. **Valuation problems** — the customer can't choose between options. Too many configurations, too many vendor candidates, too many feature combinations. **Cure:** Limit the exploration. Cut the options.

2. **Lack of information** — the customer believes they don't yet have what they need to decide. **Cure:** Offer the recommendation. Stop being a content vending machine. Say what to do.

3. **Outcome uncertainty** — the customer can't predict the consequences of the decision. They imagine the worst-case scenario. **Cure:** Take risk off the table. Build the safety net.

The high performer diagnoses which of the three is operative before prescribing the move. Most reps default to dumping more information on every customer — which solves only case 2, and makes case 1 actively worse.

## The JOLT Method — Four Moves (JOLT Effect, Ch 4-7)

### J — Judge the level of indecision

Before committing to JOLT, gauge how indecisive the customer actually is. The book identifies three levels:

- **Low indecision** — small wobble, normal late-stage anxiety. Standard close works.
- **Moderate indecision** — the customer is stuck between configurations or vendors, but still talking. JOLT works best here. The 60% conversion rate in the data lands in this tier.
- **High indecision** — the customer is paralyzed, going dark, missing meetings. Often unsalvageable inside the current cycle. Pull back and re-qualify whether they should be in pipeline.

The judge step also identifies which of the three sources (valuation / info / outcome) is dominant — diagnostic before prescription.

**Operator move:** ask the customer directly. "What's making this hard to decide?" — not "what would help you move forward?" The first surfaces the FOMU; the second invites another case-study request.

### O — Offer your recommendation

Indecisive customers don't want options. They want a guide. The high performer in the data set takes a stance: "Based on what you've told me, here's what I'd do — start with the X plan, not the Y plan, because of A, B, C."

The bad rep, by contrast, presents all the options neutrally and asks the customer to choose. This is the failure mode. Neutral option-presentation amplifies FOMU.

**Three rules for the recommendation:**
1. **Reasoning > preference.** Give the WHY. "I'd start with X because your team is new to this and X has the shallower learning curve."
2. **Authority earns the right.** The recommendation only works if the rep has earned authority through the diagnostic phase. Recommendations from a rep who hasn't done the discovery feel pushy.
3. **Take a clear position.** Hedged recommendations ("you could go either way, but maybe X") don't reduce FOMU. Pick a side.

### L — Limit the exploration

Cut the option space. Indecisive customers will fill any vacuum with more research. The high performer closes off the unproductive branches: "You don't need to see another competitor demo. Here's why X, Y, Z don't fit your situation. Let's focus on these two."

**The "pings and echoes" technique** the book identifies (also discussed on Lenny's podcast):
- A **ping** = the customer expressing a worry without naming it directly ("we have a lot going on this quarter…").
- An **echo** = the rep's chance to surface the underlying concern without making the customer say it out loud ("it sounds like the timing is the bigger question, not the product?").
- Catching pings and echoes early lets the rep limit the relevant exploration before the customer wanders into a side-research rabbit hole.

**At DR:** when the immigration MP says "let me look at one more agency just to compare", that's a ping. The Dixon-trained response is an echo + limit: "Sounds like the question is really whether ANY agency can move the needle on signed cases. If that's the question, here's the data on the three we've worked with — comparing us to another generic-marketing shop isn't going to answer it."

### T — Take risk off the table

The single highest-impact move in the data. The safety net. Specific mechanisms:

1. **Performance guarantees** — "if we don't hit X by Y, we refund / renegotiate / extend."
2. **Phased rollout / pilot** — start small, expand on proof. Reduces "what if we pick wrong" anxiety because the wrong pick is recoverable.
3. **Money-back / cancel-at-will clause** — used carefully, but very effective on the highest-indecision deals.
4. **Reset expectations downward.** Don't anchor the customer on the spectacular case study. Anchor them on the realistic outcome. Then over-deliver.

**The omission bias** — Dixon and McKenna explicitly cite this behavioral economics principle: people feel worse about bad outcomes caused by ACTION (commission) than by INACTION (omission). The customer's status-quo choice feels safer because if it fails, they didn't do it — circumstances did. JOLT's T-move is designed to make the action feel as safe as the inaction.

**At DR:** the Case Flow installation pitch should include a phased onramp ("first 60 days are install + audit, you can pause if the audit findings don't justify continuing") — that's a T-move. Without it, the indecisive MP defaults to "let me think about it" indefinitely.

## The FOMO trap (JOLT Effect, Ch 8)

The most-cited counterintuitive finding in the book: **dialing up FOMO backfires on indecisive customers 87% of the time.**

The mechanism: FOMO ("you'll miss this opportunity if you don't act") works on customers who are confident in their judgment but unsure of timing. They feel pressure to lock in. But indecisive customers aren't unsure of timing — they're unsure of THEMSELVES. Adding FOMO amplifies the underlying fear. The customer reads the pressure as more evidence that they shouldn't commit to a decision they don't feel ready to make.

The high performer in the data set does the opposite: dials DOWN the stakes. Reduces the pressure. Makes the decision feel reversible and contained. Counterintuitive, and the data is unambiguous.

**Hard rule at DR:** "if you don't sign by Friday, the pricing changes" pressure tactics work on confident skeptics. They DESTROY indecisive deals. If you can't tell which one you're facing, default to NOT applying pressure.
