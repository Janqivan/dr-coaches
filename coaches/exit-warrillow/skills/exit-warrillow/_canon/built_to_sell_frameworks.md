## Framework 1 — The 8 Drivers of Company Value (Value Builder System, cross-book)

**What it is:** Eight independent dimensions that compound into a saleability score. The composite score predicts the multiple of pre-tax profit a buyer will pay.

**The eight, in working order:**
1. **Financial Performance** — clean, audited books and consistent profit. Mechanism: buyers underwrite the numbers, not the story. Execution rule: get a review-grade financial statement before you go to market. Failure mode: messy books force the buyer to discount for risk; quality of earnings adjustments can wipe 1-2 turns of EBITDA.
2. **Growth Potential** — defensible runway the new owner can keep compounding. Mechanism: a buyer pays for the future they can capture, not the past you delivered. Execution rule: document the TAM, the next two adjacent markets, and the playbook for entering them. Failure mode: capped local-market businesses get a "lifestyle business" discount.
3. **Switzerland Structure** — concentration risk on customers, employees, and suppliers. Mechanism: any one party > 15% of revenue or capacity is a single point of failure the buyer underwrites against. Execution rule: cap your largest customer at 15% before you go to market, even if it slows growth. Failure mode: two customers at 30% each can halve a multiple.
4. **Valuation Teeter-Totter** — cash flow timing. Mechanism: businesses paid before they deliver are worth more than businesses paid after. Execution rule: shift to deposits, prepays, annual-paid-monthly billing. Failure mode: net-60 receivables consume the buyer's working capital and force a price haircut.
5. **Hierarchy of Recurring Revenue** — which tier of predictability your revenue sits on. Mechanism: harder-to-cancel revenue is worth a higher multiple. Execution rule: move every revenue stream one tier up (consumable → subscription → contract). Failure mode: project revenue, no matter how big, gets a service multiple, not a software multiple.
6. **Monopoly Control** — defensibility. Mechanism: a buyer wants to know what stops a competitor from copying you. Execution rule: document IP, methodology, brand authority, exclusive partnerships, switching costs. Failure mode: businesses competing on price get commodity multiples.
7. **Customer Satisfaction** — NPS and repeat-purchase rate. Mechanism: buyers underwrite the customer base, not the marketing. Execution rule: measure and track NPS quarterly; build a referral economy. Failure mode: churn that the founder explains away in narrative kills credibility in diligence.
8. **Hub & Spoke** — owner-dependence. Mechanism: the founder being the hub means the business is a job, not an asset. Execution rule: the 90-day-offline test. Failure mode: if Ivan can't leave for 90 days, the multiple stays in the "lifestyle business" band — typically 1-3x discretionary earnings instead of 4-8x EBITDA.

## Framework 2 — The TVR Filter (Built to Sell, productization core)

**What it is:** The three-question test for whether a service line can be productized — and therefore made saleable, scalable, and worth a multiple.

**The three questions:**
- **Teachable** — can someone other than the founder be trained to deliver this in 60-90 days?
- **Valuable** — does the customer pay enough that delivering it makes economic sense at the trained-employee labor rate (not the founder's rate)?
- **Repeatable** — does the customer come back for the same offer, same scope, same price? Or is every engagement bespoke?

**Mechanism:** a service that fails any one leg is not a product. It's the founder's job dressed up as a service. Buyers won't underwrite a job.

**Execution rule:** apply the TVR Filter to every service line in the business. Kill or restructure any line that fails two legs. Reprice and scope any line that fails one leg. The lines that pass all three become the productized core.

**Failure mode:** founders defend bespoke work by saying "but our clients love that we customize." Customization is the disease. It's incompatible with teachable and repeatable. Custom work is consulting; product work is product.

## Framework 3 — Hub & Spoke (Built to Sell)

**What it is:** The owner-dependence diagnostic. The founder is the hub; the business is a wheel of spokes radiating from them. Cut the hub out and the wheel collapses.

**Mechanism:** buyers won't pay a strategic multiple for a hub-and-spoke business because the asset they're buying — the founder — walks out the door at close. The only way around it is an extended earnout, which transfers the risk back to the seller.

**Execution rule:** the 90-day-offline test. Take 90 days completely off (or imagine doing so). Document everything that breaks. Each fracture is a decision-rule the founder is carrying in their head that has to be externalized into:
- A written SOP
- A trained number-two with explicit authority
- A system of record that isn't the founder's inbox

**Failure mode:** founders mistake delegation of *tasks* for elimination of *dependence*. The test isn't "do my people do the work?" — it's "do the decisions get made the same way without me?"

## Framework 4 — Switzerland Structure (Built to Sell + cross-book)

**What it is:** A neutrality test on three concentration vectors — customers, employees, suppliers/platforms.

**The 15% rule:**
- No single **customer** > 15% of revenue.
- No single **employee** > 15% of capability to deliver.
- No single **supplier or platform** > 15% of capacity (this includes ad platforms, software providers, distributors).

**Mechanism:** buyers value cash flow proportional to the risk of losing it. A customer at 40% of revenue isn't worth 40% of the cash flow — it's worth a discount applied to the entire business.

**Execution rule:** cap, diversify, or split before you go to market. Sometimes this means turning down growth. Two customers at 30% each is worse than no customer above 15%, even if revenue is lower.

**Failure mode:** anchor-client gratitude. Founders protect their biggest customer at the expense of their multiple. The biggest customer is the biggest risk.

## Framework 5 — The Valuation Teeter-Totter (Built to Sell + Art of Selling)

**What it is:** A cash-flow-timing diagnostic. Picture a seesaw with the seller on one end and the buyer on the other. Cash collected before delivery sits on your end; cash owed by you sits on the buyer's end.

**Mechanism:** the same revenue, billed differently, is worth a different multiple. Prepaid annual revenue is worth more than monthly billing. Monthly is worth more than net-30. Net-30 is worth more than milestone billing. Each step shifts working capital between buyer and seller.

**Execution rule:** restructure every customer contract toward the seller end of the teeter-totter. Offer a discount for annual prepay. Move from net-30 to net-7. Introduce setup deposits. Each conversion lifts the multiple.

**Failure mode:** founders assume billing terms are a customer-service decision. They are a valuation decision.

## Framework 6 — The 9 Subscription Models (The Automatic Customer)

**What it is:** A taxonomy of nine distinct recurring-revenue business models. Subscription isn't one thing — it's a category with nine subtypes, each fitting a different customer job-to-be-done.

**The nine:**
1. **Membership Website** — gated content for monthly fee. Job: stay current in a field.
2. **All-You-Can-Eat Library** — flat fee, unlimited consumption (Netflix). Job: convenience > ownership.
3. **Private Club** — scarce, high-status access (EO, Soho House). Job: belonging + status.
4. **Front-of-Line** — pay to skip the queue or get priority. Job: certainty under pressure.
5. **Consumables** — replenishment on schedule (razors, coffee). Job: don't think about reordering.
6. **Surprise Box** — curated periodic delivery (Birchbox). Job: discovery + delight.
7. **Simplifier** — pay to delegate a recurring task (lawn, bookkeeping). Job: time recovery.
8. **Network** — value increases with more users (LinkedIn). Job: access to a network.
9. **Peace-of-Mind** — pay against an event you hope never happens (insurance, monitoring, retainer-for-emergencies). Job: risk transfer.

**Mechanism:** customers don't subscribe to a category; they subscribe to a job. Picking the wrong model produces churn even with a good product.

**Execution rule:** start from the customer's recurring job-to-be-done and reverse-engineer to the model. For productized services, the two most accessible models are **Simplifier** and **Peace-of-Mind**.

**Failure mode:** picking a model based on what's trendy ("everyone has a membership site") rather than what's load-bearing for the customer.

## Framework 7 — Hierarchy of Recurring Revenue (The Automatic Customer)

**What it is:** A six-tier predictability hierarchy. Not all recurring revenue is created equal. The harder the revenue is to cancel, the higher the multiple it earns.

**The six tiers, weakest to strongest:**
1. **Consumables** — customer can stop reordering at will.
2. **Sunk-Money Consumables** — they bought a device that only takes your refills (printer ink).
3. **Renewable Subscriptions** — annual contract with default renewal; cancellation is a single decision.
4. **Sunk-Money Subscriptions** — integration costs make switching painful.
5. **Auto-Renewal Subscriptions** — credit card on file; opt-out is required action.
6. **Contract Subscriptions** — multi-year, locked, cancellation has explicit cost.

**Mechanism:** buyers underwrite cash flow proportional to its difficulty to lose. Each tier shift lifts the implied retention assumption and therefore the multiple.

**Execution rule:** move every revenue stream one tier up over the next 12 months. Convert month-to-month to annual. Convert annual to multi-year with a discount incentive. Add auto-renewal language. Build switching costs into the product.

**Failure mode:** confusing recurrence with predictability. A monthly service the customer can cancel any time is recurring but not predictable. The hierarchy measures predictability, not just billing cycle.

## Framework 8 — Monopoly Control (cross-book)

**What it is:** A defensibility test. The question every buyer asks: what stops a competitor from doing what you do for less?

**Accepted answers:**
- Proprietary methodology with documented IP.
- Brand authority and earned media (podcast, book, conference presence).
- Exclusive supplier, distribution, or platform partnerships.
- Network effects (more customers create more value per customer).
- Switching costs (integration, data, contracts).

**Mechanism:** without monopoly control, you're competing on price. Price competition compresses margin, and compressed margin destroys multiples. Defensibility is the difference between a 4x business and a 7x business.

**Execution rule:** name your monopoly source explicitly. If you can't, build one. The book itself, the podcast, the framework — these are monopoly-control assets. So is a proprietary system documented as IP (the way DR's Case Flow is structured).

**Failure mode:** confusing "we're better" with "we're defensible." Better is undefended. Defensible is structural.

## Framework 9 — Deal Structures and Buyer Types (The Art of Selling Your Business)

**What it is:** The taxonomy of who buys companies and how the deals are structured. Three buyer types, each with distinct economics.

**Three buyer types:**
1. **Strategic buyer** — wants your customers, your IP, your team, or your channel. Pays the highest multiple because they extract synergies. Slowest to close. Most likely to demand earnouts.
2. **Financial buyer** (private equity, family office) — wants cash flow. Pays a market multiple. Will keep the team, often the founder, on an earnout. Speed: medium. Diligence: heavy.
3. **Individual buyer** (search funds, owner-operators) — wants a job and a return. Pays the lowest multiple. Fastest to close on small deals. Highest emotional volatility in process.

**Four typical offer structures:**
- All cash at close (rare, lowest headline number).
- Cash + earnout (most common; the earnout transfers risk back to seller).
- Cash + seller financing (you become the lender; carries default risk).
- Cash + rollover equity (you keep a slice for the next exit).

**Mechanism:** the headline number is less important than the structure. A $10M deal with $4M cash and $6M earnout is not a $10M deal. It's a $4M deal with an option.

**Execution rule:** pick buyer type before you pick a banker. The type determines the process, the timeline, and the realistic multiple. Don't run a strategic-buyer process if your business isn't strategic to anyone.

**Failure mode:** chasing the highest headline number into a structure where most of the upside is contingent. Earnout disputes kill more deals than valuation gaps.
