## The operator's worldview (combined)

These two books are paired because they cover the **modern closing layer** in two dimensions:
- *Virtual Selling* — the **environmental** dimension. How to run a sales motion when every interaction is mediated through video, asynchronous messages, and a screen. The buyer is never in the room.
- *INKED* — the **negotiation** dimension. How to hold price, structure terms, and close without giving away margin. Blount's pushback on the old "Always Be Closing" school.

The unifying thesis: modern sales has compressed time, expanded buyer choice, and removed most of the physical cues a rep used to rely on. Therefore the rep needs (a) sharper deliberate presence in virtual environments and (b) much stronger negotiating discipline at the close — because the buyer is comparing more options, faster, with less rep influence than ever.

# Part 1 — Virtual Selling (2020)

---

## Framework 1 — Video Presence (Camera On, Always)

Blount's first rule for virtual selling: **camera on, both sides, every call.** Not optional. Not "if you feel like it." Camera off is the modern equivalent of taking a sales call from behind a one-way mirror.

**Mechanics:**
- Frame: head and shoulders, eyes slightly above center, camera at eye level (not below).
- Lighting: front-facing, soft, not from above. Window in front, not behind.
- Background: deliberate. Not necessarily branded — but not messy.
- Audio: external mic. The mic is the single biggest perceived-credibility upgrade per dollar.
- Eye contact: look at the camera lens, not the screen, when speaking. Look at the screen when listening.

**Mirror principle:** the rep with camera on creates social pressure for the buyer to turn theirs on. Camera-off prospects close at materially lower rates. The data point Blount surfaces consistently in his keynotes.

---

## Framework 2 — The Virtual Engagement Decay Curve

Blount's frame for attention in a remote call: virtual attention spans are roughly half of in-person spans. The buyer who could sit through a 30-minute pitch in a conference room will mentally check out at 15 minutes on Zoom.

**Mechanics:**
- Re-engage every 5-7 minutes with an interactive moment: a question, a screen-share toggle, a "stop me if this isn't landing," a deliberate pause.
- Asymmetry rule: in person, the rep can talk for 5 minutes before the buyer disengages. On video, that window is 90 seconds. After 90 seconds of monologue, the buyer is in another tab.
- Pre-built micro-engagement points: every slide should have a planned question or interaction, not a feature dump.

**The named failure mode:** the rep who treats a Zoom call like a webinar. Slides advance, the rep monologues, the buyer's screen fills with their email inbox. The rep doesn't notice because the buyer's nodding face is on autopilot.

---

## Framework 3 — Asynchronous Deal Momentum

Blount's frame for keeping deals moving between live calls. In a virtual sales motion, the spaces between meetings are longer, and deals die in those gaps unless the rep is deliberate.

**The four async tools:**
- **Personalized video** — 1-2 minute Loom or Vidyard recordings sent between meetings. Used to send a recap, surface an objection, or push a next step. Outperforms text email at roughly 3-5x the response rate in Blount's anecdotal data.
- **Calendar momentum** — the next meeting is booked before the current one ends. Open the calendar on screen, propose two specific times, get the slot locked while you have attention.
- **Document-as-conversation** — the proposal is a working document, not a final artifact. Edit it live with the buyer on the call. Sharing edit access pulls the buyer in.
- **Tight cadence** — the gap between virtual interactions should be 3-5 days, not 1-2 weeks. Long gaps in remote selling kill momentum.

**Operator rule:** in remote selling, the deal moves at the speed of the rep's next-step discipline. Buyers are juggling more, distracted more, and forgetting more. The rep is the only one keeping the deal alive between meetings.

---

## Framework 4 — Pre-Suasion in Virtual Environments

Blount's adaptation of Cialdini-style pre-suasion to the constraints of virtual selling. The setup happens *before* the call starts.

**The pre-call moves:**
- **Pre-meeting agenda** — sent 24 hours in advance, three bullets, ending in "anything else you want to add before we meet?" The act of confirming the agenda pre-commits the buyer.
- **Pre-meeting research signal** — one specific, observable detail about the buyer's company surfaced in the agenda email. Demonstrates effort. Tilts the buyer toward expecting a competent rep, not a generic pitch.
- **Pre-call regulation** — the rep's two-minute pre-call routine before joining: breathing, posture, mental review of the buyer's situation. Sets the rep's emotional state before the camera goes on (see *Sales EQ* file: emotional contagion).
- **First 30 seconds choreography** — pre-rehearsed opener, pre-built check-in, pre-set first question. Removes hesitation in the highest-stakes 30 seconds of the call.

**Why pre-suasion matters more in virtual:** the rep has fewer in-room cues to recover from a weak start. A flat opening on Zoom can't be saved by a strong handshake, a confident walk-in, or a shared coffee. The first impression is fully delivered through the camera.

---

## Framework 5 — The Virtual Psychological Close Distance

Blount's frame for the felt sense of distance between rep and buyer in a virtual call. Virtual feels far, even when the screen makes the buyer's face larger than life. That perceived distance affects close rates.

**Compensating moves:**
- **More direct asks.** In person, soft asks read as polite. On video, soft asks read as uncertain. Be sharper.
- **More named labels.** "It sounds like you're concerned about implementation timeline" — explicitly naming the buyer's state shortens the distance.
- **More deliberate silence.** Silence on video feels longer than in person. Use it anyway. Let the buyer fill the gap. This is where buyers reveal the real objection.
- **More physicality.** Lean in toward the camera at key moments. Slow down. Use the hands deliberately for emphasis. The camera reduces physical signal — the rep has to amplify it.

# Part 2 — INKED (2020) — Negotiation at the Close

---

## Framework 6 — Negotiation Begins Before the Close

Blount's foundational principle in *INKED*: by the time the rep is negotiating price, **80% of the leverage has already been set** by what happened earlier in the cycle. Most reps get crushed at the close because they treated the discovery and demo phases as separate from negotiation, when in fact those phases were already setting the price ceiling and floor.

**What "negotiating before the close" means:**
- Setting a fair-value anchor early in the cycle (mention real pricing ranges in discovery, don't dodge).
- Building consequence on inaction (so the buyer feels the cost of "no deal," not just the cost of "your price").
- Disqualifying buyers who can't afford the value before you've spent the cycle (saves margin downstream).
- Refusing to discount mid-cycle (each pre-close discount trains the buyer that the rep flinches).

**The named bug:** the rep who keeps everything friendly through discovery, then is shocked when the buyer asks for 30% off at the close. The rep set the buyer up to expect that ask by being too eager through the cycle.

---

## Framework 7 — The "Always Be Closing" Replacement (Discipline Over Pressure)

Blount's pushback on the old ABC school. The "always be closing" school taught reps to push, pressure, and trial-close incessantly until the buyer cracked. Blount argues this approach is **obsolete in the modern complex sale** because:
- Buyers have more information and more vendor options than ever.
- Buyers have low tolerance for pressure tactics — they will simply ghost.
- The relationship cost of pushing too hard now extends across LinkedIn, peer networks, and online reviews.

**Blount's replacement:** **disciplined negotiating** instead of pressured closing.

The disciplined-negotiating frame:
- Hold price unless you get something of equal value in exchange (concession-for-concession, never concession-only).
- Slow the pace at the close, not speed it up. Buyers under pressure look for an exit.
- Ask more diagnostic questions in the final round than you did in discovery. The buyer's real objection often only surfaces here.
- Be willing to walk. The willingness to walk is the single biggest price-protection tool the rep has.

---

## Framework 8 — Concession Strategy (Never Give Without Getting)

Blount's rule on concessions: **every concession must be paired with a get.** If the buyer asks for 10% off, the rep doesn't give 10% off. The rep gives 10% off *if* the buyer commits to a longer contract, an immediate signature, a logo reference, an annual prepay, or a faster implementation date.

**The mechanics:**
- Concessions in isolation train the buyer that the rep was lying about original price.
- Concessions paired with gets train the buyer that the rep has a structure — there are levers, but they cost something.
- The **first concession is the most expensive** — it sets the expectation for all subsequent concessions. If the rep folds easily on the first ask, the buyer will keep asking.
- Concessions should get smaller in size and slower in frequency as the negotiation progresses. The opposite signals desperation.

**Operator-level point:** the rep should have a pre-built menu of "things I can ask for in exchange for a discount" before walking into the negotiation. Items on that menu: contract length, prepay, logo rights, case study commit, referrals, expanded scope. Negotiating a discount without a get is malpractice.

---

## Framework 9 — Closing Signals Are Physical, Not Verbal

Blount's frame for reading buyer readiness at the close. Verbal signals at this stage are noisy — buyers say "let me think about it" when they're ready, and "this is great" when they're not. The reliable signals are physical.

**The physical signals to watch:**
- **Posture shift.** Buyer leans toward the camera or toward the document.
- **Pen pickup.** Buyer reaches for something to write with — unconscious signal of committing the moment to record.
- **Pace change.** Buyer's questions get shorter and more specific. They've stopped exploring and started preparing to act.
- **Looking at the contract.** Buyer's eyes track to where the signature goes, even before being asked.
- **Asking implementation questions.** "When could we start?" "What does onboarding look like?" — buyer is mentally already inside the deal.

**Operator move:** when two or more of these signals are present, **stop selling and ask for the close.** Continuing to pitch past a buying signal is the named failure mode — the rep talks the buyer back out of the deal they were already in.

---

## Framework 10 — Trial Closes Before the Real Close

Blount's tactical layer between the discovery phase and the close: a series of small **trial closes** that surface objections incrementally, before the rep gets to the price conversation.

**Trial-close construction:**
- "If we could solve [X concern], would this be a fit?"
- "Assuming budget works, would you be ready to move in the next 30 days?"
- "If you were going to move forward, who else would need to be in the room?"

**Why trial closes matter:**
- Each trial close either surfaces an objection (which the rep can now handle while there's still time) or removes one (which builds momentum toward the real close).
- A real close at the end of a series of clean trial closes is much higher probability — the rep has already validated every variable.
- A real close *without* trial closes is a guess. The rep is testing the variables for the first time at the moment of highest stakes.

---

## Framework 11 — Walking-Away Power

Blount's argument: the single most undervalued lever in sales negotiation is **the rep's genuine willingness to walk away from a bad deal.** Not a bluff. A real willingness, grounded in pipeline health.

**Why it works:**
- Buyers can read bluff-walk-away from real walk-away. Bluffing reads as desperation.
- Real walk-away requires that the rep's pipeline is healthy enough that this deal doesn't have to close. That's a function of *Fanatical Prospecting* discipline (see Blount file on prospecting).
- The willingness to walk shows up in micro-behaviors: longer silences, less rushed responses, no over-explaining, no urgency-faking on the rep's side.

**The compound effect:** reps with full pipelines negotiate better, hold price better, and close higher-quality deals. The cause is upstream — prospecting discipline → pipeline health → walking-away power → margin held at the close.

**Operator implication:** if the rep is consistently caving on price, the root cause is almost never poor negotiation skill. It's an empty pipe. Fix the pipe, the negotiation fixes itself.
