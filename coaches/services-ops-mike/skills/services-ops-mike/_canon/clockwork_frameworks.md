# Clockwork — Framework Map

**Source:** Michalowicz, Mike. *Clockwork: Design Your Business to Run Itself.* Portfolio, original 2018, revised expanded edition 2022.
**Treatment:** Framework structures described in own words for synthetic-coach use. Book text is NOT reproduced. Each framework is named, mapped to the source chapter or concept, and described mechanically. For Mike's parables — the bartender, the four-week vacation case studies, the QBR identification stories — buy the book.

---

## What Clockwork is for

Clockwork is the OWNER LAYER of Mike's Services Operating System. Profit First fixes the cash. Pumpkin Plan fixes the client base. Clockwork fixes the founder — specifically, the founder who has built a job, not a business, and cannot leave the building without revenue collapsing.

The terminal question Clockwork answers: **can the owner disappear for four weeks, with no contact, and the business still runs, sells, delivers, and grows?** If the answer is no, the business is owner-dependent — a design defect, not a workload issue. Clockwork is the redesign protocol.

The shift Mike pushes the operator through: stop optimizing your effort, redesign the system that requires the effort. More hustle compounds the trap. The exit is structural.

## Framework 1 — The 4D Mix (Doing / Deciding / Delegating / Designing)

**What it is:** the four categories every human work-block in a business falls into. Time-track a week, bucket every block into one of the four, get a brutal picture of where the owner actually lives.

**The four Ds:**
1. **Doing** — execution. Hands-on output. Writing the proposal, running the ad account, sending the invoice, delivering the workshop.
2. **Deciding** — judgment calls on someone else's work. Approving copy, picking the budget, signing off on a hire, calling a strategic direction.
3. **Delegating** — assigning work AND ownership of an OUTCOME to a specific person, with the brief, the measure, and the deadline.
4. **Designing** — building the system, the offer, the strategy, the playbook. The work that compounds because it removes the need for future Doing.

**The diagnostic rule:** trapped owners are 70-90% in Doing. Healthy founders trend toward Designing-dominant (Mike's target benchmark is 80% Designing for the visionary seat), with Deciding and Delegating filling most of the rest, and Doing close to zero.

**The team benchmark Mike proposes** (Clockwork revised, chapter on the 4D mix):
- Team-wide healthy mix is roughly 80% Doing / 2% Deciding / 8% Delegating / 10% Designing.
- The proportions shift by seat. Frontline producers live in Doing. Managers live in Deciding + Delegating. Founder lives in Designing.

**Operator use:**
1. Pick a normal week. Track every 15-minute block. Tag it D-1 (Doing), D-2 (Deciding), D-3 (Delegating), D-4 (Designing).
2. Total the percentages on Friday.
3. If you're >50% in Doing as the owner, you are the bottleneck. Begin migration: every Doing task gets a successor, a brief, and a date.

**Failure modes:**
- Self-deception. Owners code Designing work that's actually Doing. Test: would a competent hire be doing this in 90 days? If yes, it's Doing.
- "I'll move out of Doing once revenue is stable." Revenue never stabilizes for a Doing-dominant owner. The order is reversed: move out of Doing FIRST so revenue can stabilize.

## Framework 2 — Queen Bee Role (QBR)

**The biology metaphor:** a beehive has thousands of workers and one queen. The queen lays the eggs. Without the queen, the hive collapses inside weeks. The bees instinctively protect her — they feed her, clean her, surround her, redirect heat and resources toward her. The hive's number-one job is keeping the QBR functional.

**The business translation:** every business has ONE function that is disproportionately responsible for its success. The Queen Bee Role. If the QBR runs at world-class level, every other function gets easier. If the QBR stutters, every other function compounds the problem.

**How to find the QBR (the test):**
1. Ask: "What activity, if performed excellently and consistently, would make every other function in this business easier?"
2. Cross-check: "If this activity stopped for four weeks, would the business survive?" If the answer is "no, it'd implode," that's a candidate QBR.
3. Identify who currently performs it. Nine times out of ten, the owner. That's the trap.
4. Audit what % of that person's week is actually on QBR vs admin, firefighting, low-value meetings.

**Examples (archetypes):** media agency → media buying / creative testing. Law firm → case-work quality on signed matters. Restaurant → kitchen consistency.

**The operator move — Protect the Queen:**
- Strip every non-QBR activity off the QBR performer. Move it to the team, automate it, eliminate it, or accept it'll be done badly elsewhere.
- The QBR performer's calendar is sacred. Meetings, admin, slack, ops are second-class.
- Every other team member's job description gets rewritten with one line at the top: "Your job is to make the QBR easier and more effective."

**Failure mode:** designating a QBR the owner WISHES were the QBR vs the one that actually is. Wishful QBRs are usually strategy, vision, or "leadership." Real QBRs are usually narrower and more operational than the owner wants to admit.

## Framework 3 — Time Analysis Tracking (the Clockwork audit)

**What it is:** the diagnostic that precedes any redesign. Two-week time log, every block coded against three dimensions.

**The three dimensions Mike codes every block on:**
1. **4D bucket** — Doing / Deciding / Delegating / Designing.
2. **QBR-relevance** — does this block advance the Queen Bee Role? Yes / no.
3. **Person-bound vs role-bound** — could a different qualified person do this block? Yes / no.

**What the data reveals:**
- The % of the owner's week trapped in Doing.
- The % of the team's week on QBR vs adjacent.
- The blocks that are person-bound (because only the owner has the knowledge / relationship / authority) — those are the priority delegation targets.

**The reduction protocol after the audit:**
1. Cut: blocks that produce no measurable outcome. Eliminate.
2. Transfer: blocks a team member could do with a 1-page brief. Hand them off.
3. Trash and Train: blocks the owner is doing because no one else knows how. Document, train, transfer.
4. Treasure: blocks that are QBR-advancing and only the owner can do. Protect, expand.

**The math Mike points operators toward:** a clean Clockwork audit usually surfaces 15-30 hours of weekly owner time that can be migrated within 90 days. That's the redesign runway.

## Framework 4 — The Four-Week Vacation Test

**What it is:** the binary outcome test for whether Clockwork has worked. The owner leaves for four consecutive weeks, no calls, no email, no Slack, no "quick checks." On return, the business should be the same or stronger than when they left.

**Why four weeks specifically:** Mike's argument is that one or two weeks is survivable on momentum and stockpiled decisions. Four weeks forces the system to face real situations — a client complaint, a hire decision, a campaign that needs adjustment, a payment problem. If the system handles those without the owner, the system works.

**What the test actually measures:**
- Decisions: does the team have the authority and the criteria to decide without the owner?
- Sales: do new clients still come in and convert?
- Delivery: do existing clients still get the work without the owner?
- Cash: do invoices go out, payments come in, bills get paid?
- People: do team issues get resolved without the owner mediating?

**The staged approach:** week-long unplugged trip each quarter → two-week trip annually → four-week trip once the muscle is built.

**The failure mode:** owners take the trip, get called three times, and declare it "basically a pass." It's not. The standard is no contact. Three calls = three system gaps. Fix the gaps. Try again.

## Framework 5 — Capture / Organize / Streamline / Bolt-On (the COSB system redesign loop)

**What it is:** Mike's four-step protocol for converting any owner-held task into a team-runnable system. Used after the Time Analysis flags a block for transfer.

**The four steps:**
1. **Capture** — document the task in operator-grade detail. Screen-record yourself doing it. Narrate every decision. Output: a raw walkthrough.
2. **Organize** — clean the walkthrough into a sequence with named steps, decision points, and acceptance criteria. Output: an SOP.
3. **Streamline** — strip every step that doesn't produce outcome. Remove approvals, redundant checks, optional polish. Output: a lean SOP.
4. **Bolt-On** — attach the SOP to a specific seat in the org chart. Assign metrics. Assign the next person to run it. Owner exits the task.

**The discipline Mike enforces:** owners want to skip to step 4 ("just hand it to her"). Steps 1-3 are non-negotiable. Without Capture + Organize + Streamline, the handoff is doomed and the task boomerangs back.

## Framework 6 — The Big BANG (Big Aha + Next Step Goal)

**What it is:** the focus mechanism Clockwork uses to prevent the team from "designing" a hundred things at once.

**The structure:**
- **Big Aha:** the single, company-wide breakthrough goal for the next 12-18 months. One sentence. Quantified. Time-bound. Outcome-oriented, not activity-oriented.
- **Next Step Goal:** the ONE measurable milestone in the next quarter that, if hit, gets the company materially closer to the Big Aha.

**The operator constraint:** one Big BANG per business per cycle. Not three. Not "our top five priorities." One.

**Why one:** Mike's argument echoes the Pumpkin Plan logic — concentrated effort on one vine produces a colossal pumpkin. Distributed effort across ten produces ten mediocre pumpkins. The Big BANG forces the colossal-pumpkin discipline at the company-goal level.

## Framework 7 — The Right Person, Right Role allocation rule

**What it is:** Mike's heuristic for placing humans against the 4D mix as the business grows.

**The structure:**
- **Frontline doers (junior team)** — 95%+ Doing, with clear playbooks. They execute. Decisions are made above them.
- **Managers** — 40% Doing, 30% Deciding, 20% Delegating, 10% Designing. They run players and decide on disputes.
- **Leaders / Heads-of** — 10% Doing (only QBR-grade), 20% Deciding, 30% Delegating, 40% Designing. They build the system.
- **Owner / Visionary** — Designing-dominant (80%+), small Deciding and Delegating remainder, Doing approaching zero (or restricted entirely to QBR).

**The misfit symptoms:**
- Manager stuck in Doing → undertrained team or too few players. Hire down.
- Owner stuck in Doing → no manager layer exists yet, or the manager is undertrusted. Build the layer.
- Owner stuck in Deciding → bottleneck. The team has authority gaps. Push decision rights down with clearer criteria.

## Framework 8 — The Trim Tab metaphor (small system change, large output change)

**What it is:** Mike's framing for which Clockwork interventions to prioritize. Borrowed from naval engineering — the trim tab is the tiny rudder-on-the-rudder that turns the rudder, which turns the ship. Small lever, large effect.

**Operator application:**
- After the Time Analysis, you'll have a list of 30+ possible interventions. Don't run them all.
- Pick the 1-3 trim tabs — the small system changes that, if implemented, will move the largest downstream outcome.
- Common trim tabs in services businesses:
  - One new role on the org chart (e.g., a delivery lead) that absorbs 60% of owner Doing.
  - One new metric on a scorecard (e.g., QBR-hours per week) that re-anchors the team's attention.
  - One eliminated meeting (e.g., the daily standup) that returns 5 hours of Designing time.

**The discipline:** trim tab work is unglamorous. The owner wants the big redesign. The leverage is in the small structural shifts.

## Framework 9 — The Org Chart of the Future

**What it is:** the planning artifact Mike asks every Clockwork operator to draw before they hire next.

**The structure:**
1. Draw the org chart of the business as it should look in 3 years at the revenue and capacity you want.
2. Name every seat. Even the seats that don't yet exist.
3. Mark which seats currently exist (filled) and which are vacant.
4. Mark which seats the OWNER currently occupies (often 4-7 of them).
5. The hire sequence becomes obvious: hire to vacate the owner's hottest occupied seats first, in order of QBR-relevance.

**Why it works:** owners hire reactively (we need a junior, we need a coordinator). The Org Chart of the Future hires structurally (we need this specific role to exit the owner from this specific seat).

**The check Mike enforces:** if the owner is still in 3+ seats on the org chart 18 months from now, the chart isn't aggressive enough. Redraw with the owner in 1 seat — Visionary.

## Framework 10 — The Clockwork Standard for "running"

**What it is:** Mike's operating definition of "the business runs itself." Used as the bar against which any Clockwork effort gets measured.

**The four conditions, all of which must be true:**
1. **Sales runs without the owner.** Lead flow, conversion, and onboarding happen on a system.
2. **Delivery runs without the owner.** Client work ships at standard quality on the team's hand.
3. **Cash runs without the owner.** Invoices go out, AR is chased, AP is paid, allocations happen on cadence.
4. **People runs without the owner.** Hiring, firing, performance, and conflict resolution are handled by a designated layer (HoO / manager / leadership).

If even one of the four still routes through the owner, the business doesn't "run itself" — it runs the owner. Clockwork is incomplete until all four are true.

## How Clockwork interacts with the rest of the system

- **Pre-req:** Profit First should be in place. A Clockwork redesign on a cash-bleeding business fails because the owner can't fund the hires required to exit Doing.
- **Pre-req:** Pumpkin Plan should be in flight. Clockwork redesign on a bad-fit client base is impossible — bad clients consume disproportionate Doing capacity and prevent system design.
- **Diagnostic gate:** Fix This Next flags "Order" as the broken level → Clockwork is the open-the-book moment.
- **Downstream:** with Clockwork in place, the owner's Designing time finally exists. That's when Surge (anticipatory positioning) and Get Different (demand) become possible. Without Clockwork, the owner has no bandwidth to use them.
