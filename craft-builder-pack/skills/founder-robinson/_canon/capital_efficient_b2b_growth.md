# Capital-Efficient B2B Growth — the Adam Robinson playbook

This canon documents how Adam Robinson built two bootstrapped B2B SaaS
companies — Retention.com to ~$22M ARR with 6 people and RB2B from 0 to
$5M ARR in ~15 months with 5-6 people — and the operating principles
underneath. It is the counter-model to growth-at-all-costs venture SaaS:
freemium as distribution, person-level signal over company-level, ARR
per employee as the scoreboard, gross margin as the moat, and
distribution-before-monetization as the sequencing rule.

Use this when the question is: how do I price, how do I structure the
free tier, should I raise or bootstrap, what does "good" look like at
my stage, and how do I avoid the standard SaaS traps.

---

## 1. The frame — bootstrap as the default, not the exception

For most B2B SaaS and most B2B services (including a performance
marketing agency), the right capital posture is bootstrapped + capital-
efficient, not VC-tracked + growth-at-all-costs. The reasoning:

- VC dollars compress timelines but expand obligations. Once you take
  the money, you owe an exit at a multiple that requires growth that
  the underlying market often can't sustainably support.
- Most B2B niches are not winner-take-all. The winner-take-all framing
  is what VCs require to justify the model; it is not what the market
  actually rewards.
- Capital efficiency lets the founder keep equity, keep optionality,
  keep the right to refuse a bad deal, and reinvest free cash flow
  into the only channel that compounds without a CAC line item:
  founder-led media.

Robinson is on the record across multiple interviews (Founderpath,
Capitaly, the Collin Cadmus podcast, Sramana Mitra's series, Smooth
Operators Guide) as someone who specifically chose bootstrap after
exposure to the alternative. Retention.com hit $22M ARR with 87% gross
margins on a 6-person team. That ARR-per-employee math (~$3.6M) is
not achievable inside a VC growth model because the headcount line
expands faster than ARR can.

The default position: bootstrap. Only deviate if you can articulate a
specific winner-take-all dynamic that requires capital you cannot
generate from operations within the window the market gives you.

## 2. Capital efficiency — the actual numbers to watch

Robinson optimizes for, and publishes, a small number of metrics. They
are not the SaaS-standard metrics that VCs ask about (LTV/CAC, magic
number, Rule of 40 in isolation). They are:

- **ARR per employee.** Retention.com ~$3.6M/head. RB2B ~$800K-$1M/head
  in year 1, climbing. For comparison, the median VC-backed B2B SaaS
  is closer to $150-250K/head. The gap is not luck; it is structure.
- **Gross margin.** Retention.com runs at ~87% gross margin (versus
  typical SaaS 75-80%). High gross margin gives you the option to
  fund product, content, and team out of cash flow rather than dilution.
- **Free cash flow margin.** Profit you can actually deploy. This is
  what funds Robinson's content engine, R&D for the next product, and
  optionality.
- **Burn multiple, if any.** For bootstrapped operators, the answer
  should usually be: not applicable, because there is no burn.

For a services firm (Ivan / DR): the parallel metrics are revenue per
head, gross margin per client, and retained earnings per quarter. The
discipline is identical — refuse the headcount expansion that would
drop revenue-per-head below the bar; refuse the client mix that would
compress gross margin below the bar.

## 3. Person-level visitor identification — the RB2B product hypothesis

The strategic insight that became RB2B: every intent / ABM / visitor
tool in the market stops at the company level. They tell you Acme Co.
visited your site. They don't tell you Sarah Chen, VP of Demand Gen
at Acme Co., visited at 11:47pm on a Sunday, four times this week,
spending 6 minutes on the pricing page.

The bet is that company-level intent is mostly noise (large companies
have hundreds of employees, any of whom could be on the site for any
reason) and person-level intent is signal. If you can put the actual
person, with name + LinkedIn URL + email, in the seller's Slack in
real time, you've created a new category of B2B data.

The generalizable principle, beyond RB2B's product:

- In any B2B GTM motion, the unit of attention should be the named
  human, not the logo.
- Account-based marketing without person-resolution is mostly waste.
- The piece of infrastructure that converts logo-level signal into
  person-level signal is disproportionately valuable.

For DR specifically: when auditing a prospect or designing outreach,
the question is never "did this company engage?" It's "which named
person at this company engaged, with what specific behavior, on what
specific page, at what specific time." That is the entire game.

## 4. Freemium-as-distribution — not freemium-as-discount

Most "freemium" SaaS treats the free tier as a discount: a stripped-
down version of the product, designed to nudge users toward paid
through crippled limits. Robinson's model is the opposite: the free
tier is the distribution channel, and the free product must deliver
real, unambiguous value on its own.

RB2B's free tier: real-time Slack alerts for the first ~200 person-
level identifications per month, no credit card required. This is not
a demo. It is the working product. A user can set it up, get a Slack
alert that day, and run their pipeline off of it for months without
paying.

Paid unlocks: more volume, email enrichment, CRM integrations,
additional users. The buyer upgrades when the free tier's limits
become a real constraint on a workflow they've already adopted.

The design principles:

1. **Value before invoice.** The free product must solve a real
   problem the first day. No "talk to sales to see the data."
2. **No credit card upfront.** The friction of the credit card kills
   the flywheel.
3. **The free tier is the marketing.** Every free user is a node in
   distribution: they bring colleagues in, they post about it, they
   become case studies.
4. **Paid is unlocked, not gated.** The user already gets value;
   paid removes a ceiling, not a wall.
5. **Distribution before monetization.** Robinson explicitly cites the
   Mailchimp playbook — nail distribution first, monetize second.

For services (Ivan/DR): the parallel "free tier" is a productized
audit, public competitor teardown, or self-serve scorecard. Same
rules: real value the first day, no credit card, no sales call
required, paid engagement is unlocked when the prospect wants what
the free thing cannot give them.

## 5. Pricing experiments — public, fast, and honest

Robinson runs pricing changes in public. When RB2B changes pricing,
the change is announced on LinkedIn and in the newsletter, with the
reasoning, the alternatives considered, and (often) the numbers
behind the decision. The public RB2B newsletter posts include titles
like "Public RB2B Baremetrics Dashboard | Don't Measure ANYTHING |
New Pricing is OUT!!!"

Why this matters operationally:

- Pricing changes done in public force you to articulate the
  reasoning, which exposes weak reasoning before it ships.
- The audience becomes a calibration loop. Pricing pushback in the
  comments is faster and cheaper than running a quant test.
- Customers do not punish public pricing decisions the way founders
  fear they will. They punish surprise pricing changes hidden behind
  emails. The transparency itself is the deal.

The pricing operating cadence Robinson runs (inferred across his
public posts and the RB2B newsletter): a meaningful pricing
experiment every ~quarter, announced before it ships, instrumented
during, and reviewed publicly after.

## 6. The 0 → $5M ARR playbook (RB2B specific)

The condensed version of how RB2B got from launch (March 2024) to
$5M ARR in roughly 15 months, with 5-6 people:

**Pre-launch:**
- Robinson had spent months building his LinkedIn audience while
  running Retention.com, posting about that company's journey. By
  launch day, ~3,000 people were on the RB2B waitlist (cited in the
  Capitaly and SaaStock writeups).
- The product hypothesis (person-level vs company-level) had been
  refined publicly through LinkedIn posts soliciting feedback from
  the B2B SaaS audience.

**Launch (March 2024):**
- Free tier opens. Slack alerts, no credit card. Anyone can sign up
  and use the product in minutes.
- Single channel for awareness: Adam's LinkedIn. No paid ads, no
  outbound team, no SDRs.

**Weeks 1-16:**
- RB2B hits $1M ARR in ~16 weeks — Robinson's fastest of three
  companies to $1M (Founderpath, SaaS Club).
- Content cadence holds at daily. Posts mix waitlist wins, product
  updates, customer screenshots, P&L snapshots, and pricing
  questions.

**Months 4-6:**
- $0 → $200K MRR in ~6 months (StartupGTM Substack).
- Freemium signups continue compounding. Conversion from free to
  paid is the optimization focus, not new top of funnel.

**Months 6-15:**
- Crosses $5M ARR around 15 months in (multiple interview citations).
- Team headcount holds at 5-6. ARR per head climbs into the
  $800K-$1M range.
- Public Baremetrics-style dashboard goes live to keep the
  transparency discipline credible at scale.

**What did NOT happen:**
- No paid ads. No outbound SDR team. No partnership / channel motion.
  No PR push beyond podcast appearances Adam personally went on.

The single biggest takeaway is that the entire $5M ARR was built on
the compounding of three things: a free tier that delivered instant
value, a founder posting daily in a vulnerable specific voice, and a
product hypothesis (person-level) that was differentiated enough that
the content had something concrete to point at.

## 7. RB2B vs Retention.com — the lessons crossover

Retention.com (ecommerce identity resolution) and RB2B (B2B person-
level identity) share founder, methodology, and operating principles
but differ instructively. The lessons Robinson has surfaced across
interviews:

- **Retention.com taught the bootstrap math.** Hitting $22M ARR with
  6 people and 87% gross margin proved the model — and proved that
  the constraint on growth was not capital, it was the founder's
  attention.
- **Retention.com's growth flattened in 2024** while RB2B grew. This
  is Robinson's data point that even a profitable bootstrapped
  business needs a second product or a second wave; the same
  audience can be sold a new shape of the same problem.
- **RB2B's freemium was a bet Retention.com couldn't have made.**
  Ecommerce identity resolution requires too much per-customer
  setup to give away for free; B2B Slack alerts at low volume
  cost almost nothing. The product economics of B2B vs ecommerce
  shaped what was possible.
- **The founder-led content engine transferred cleanly.** The same
  LinkedIn audience Robinson built for Retention.com became the
  warm launch list for RB2B. The audience is the durable asset; the
  product is interchangeable.

The cross-product lesson for any founder: the moat is the audience
and the operating principles, not the specific product. If you build
the founder-led-content + capital-efficient + freemium-as-distribution
machine once, you can run it again on the next product without
starting over.

## 8. Application to DR / services firm context

The Robinson model translates to services more cleanly than founders
assume. The translations:

| RB2B/SaaS | DR/services |
|---|---|
| ARR per employee | Revenue per head, retained-earnings per head |
| 87% gross margin | Gross margin per client cohort |
| Freemium product | Productized free audit / competitor teardown / scorecard |
| Daily LinkedIn posting | Daily LinkedIn posting (no change) |
| Public Baremetrics dashboard | Public scorecard / quarterly anonymized client results |
| Person-level visitor ID | Named-prospect intent (which decision-maker on which page) |
| Bootstrap, no raise | Self-fund expansion from retained earnings |
| Pricing experiments in public | Pricing tiers and case mix published openly |

The hard things for a services founder, in this model, are (a)
committing to the founder-led-content cadence when the business has
been delivery-driven, (b) productizing a free front-door that's
useful on its own, and (c) holding the line on margin and revenue-
per-head as headcount grows from 20 to 100.

Robinson's specific advice would be: pick one of the three to
attack this quarter, don't try all three at once.

## 9. The traps to avoid

- **Raising because peers raised.** The peer-raise pressure is the
  single most common reason capital-efficient operators give up the
  thing that was working. Don't.
- **Hiring ahead of revenue.** In a services firm, every headcount
  added before the revenue is contracted compresses gross margin
  and slows the flywheel.
- **Free tier that requires sales.** If the free product can't be
  activated without a sales call, it's not freemium — it's a demo
  request with extra steps.
- **Pricing locked in stone.** The founders who never raise prices
  are leaving 20-40% margin on the table. Run a pricing experiment
  every 1-2 quarters.
- **Multi-channel before single-channel works.** Don't add paid
  before LinkedIn is saturated. Don't add SEO before paid is
  optimized. Don't add outbound before product-led conversion is
  measured. The constraint moves; chase the constraint.
- **Founder out of the channel.** The moment the founder steps off
  LinkedIn, distribution flattens. The cost of the founder being
  ON the channel is the cheapest CAC in the business.
