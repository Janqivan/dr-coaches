# The Win Without Pitching Manifesto — 12 Proclamations Map

Synthesised from publicly available summaries, Blair Enns' own articles
on winwithoutpitching.com, and his interview corpus (2Bobs, The Futur,
Ditching Hourly, Newfangled, Duct Tape Marketing, Build A Better Agency,
Society of Advice). This is a map of the stance — not a reproduction of
the book. Buy the book.

The 12 Proclamations are the operating system underneath every Enns
framework. Pricing Creativity, the Four Conversations, the seller-doctor
posture — all of it sits on top of these twelve commitments. Pricing
mechanics applied without the underlying stance produce short-lived wins
and long-term resentment. The stance is the moat.

How to read each entry:
- **What it says** — the proclamation in plain language.
- **Why it matters** — the underlying mechanic.
- **How to apply** — the move Ivan makes this week.
- **Failure mode** — what it looks like when this is being violated.

---

## 1. We Will Specialize

**What it says.** Pick a narrow position. Become more expert than the
field at one thing. Refuse to be a generalist who does many things
adequately.

**Why it matters.** Power in the buyer-seller relationship comes from
expertise scarcity. If a buyer can substitute you with three competitors
on a Google search, your pricing is capped by the lowest bidder. Enns
on the Manifesto's core position, paraphrased: if you are not seen as
more expert than the competition, you are one in a sea of many. The
sea is priced by procurement. Experts are priced by the value they
create.

**How to apply.** Pick a vertical narrow enough that you can credibly
be the most expert in it. Verticalise the case studies, the writing,
the offer, the website, the pricing. Refuse work outside the position.
For Ivan: this is already half-done — three verticals (immigration,
franchise, vocational). The work is to pick one and become known as
*the* shop for it, not three shops that do three things.

**Failure mode.** "We work with anyone who has a marketing budget."
Generalist case studies. Lead nurture that reads like every other
agency's. Procurement-led RFPs. Margin compression. Comparing yourself
to other agencies instead of being the obvious answer.

## 2. We Will Replace Presentations With Conversations

**What it says.** No more pitch decks. No more dog-and-pony shows.
No more Big Reveal. The sale of expertise happens in conversation —
two-way, diagnostic, mutual. The pitch is a vendor's act.

**Why it matters.** A presentation puts the seller in a performance
posture. A conversation puts the seller in a peer posture. You cannot
diagnose while performing. Buyers who buy expertise buy from the
expert who showed up calm, asked sharp questions, and disagreed when
needed. They do not buy from the slides.

**How to apply.** Replace the kickoff deck with a structured
conversation. Replace the proposal walkthrough with a written document
and a phone call. Replace the "creds deck" with a five-minute spoken
positioning statement. If a buyer demands a pitch, treat that as a
qualifying signal — they want a vendor, not an expert.

**Failure mode.** "Let me put together a deck and walk you through
our capabilities." Spec creative. Free strategy in the pitch room.
A 47-slide capabilities document on the first call.

## 3. We Will Do With Words What We Used To Do With Paper

**What it says.** Proposals are conversations first, documents second.
The work happens in dialogue. The document is a confirmation, not a
sales tool.

**Why it matters.** A long, polished proposal becomes the negotiation
surface — buyers attack it line by line. A short proposal that
follows a real Value Conversation is just paperwork. The selling has
already happened.

**How to apply.** Keep proposals short. Three options. One page of
context, three options, one page of acceptance. No appendices, no
methodology section, no "about us." If the conversation hasn't
happened yet, don't write the proposal — have the conversation first.

**Failure mode.** 40-page SOWs. Methodology slides as proposal pages.
"Let me send you a proposal and we'll discuss." Proposal-as-pitch.

## 4. We Will Seek To Better Understand The Client

**What it says.** Before solving, before pricing, before pitching —
understand. The buyer's stated problem is usually not the real problem.
Find the real one.

**Why it matters.** The expert is the one asking better questions, not
the one with faster answers. Buyers who feel deeply understood relinquish
control — they let the expert lead. Buyers who feel processed do not.

**How to apply.** In the Qualifying Conversation, ask why now, why us,
what happens if nothing changes, who else is at the table, what the
last firm got wrong. In the Value Conversation, ask what the desired
future looks like in their words — not yours. Listen four times as
much as you talk.

**Failure mode.** Talking over the buyer. Pre-loading the agenda.
"Here's how we work" before "here's what you need." Confusing rapport
with diagnosis.

## 5. We Will Build Expertise Rapidly

**What it says.** Specialisation is not a static position. The expert
keeps deepening. Skills, processes, IP, frameworks — built fast,
published faster.

**Why it matters.** Expertise that does not deepen erodes. Competitors
catch up. The position that won you the last engagement is the
position that loses you the next one. Compounding expertise is the
durable advantage.

**How to apply.** Treat every engagement as research. Publish what you
learn. Build proprietary frameworks (named diagnostics, named playbooks,
named methods). Hire for capability you don't yet have. For Ivan: each
immigration / franchise / vocational engagement should produce a
codified asset — a benchmark, a playbook, a teardown — that becomes
the next engagement's leverage.

**Failure mode.** Running on the same playbook for five years. No
proprietary IP. No published thinking. The team gets faster at the
same work, not better at harder work.

## 6. We Will Make Continuous Learning Mandatory

**What it says.** Everyone in the firm keeps learning. Not optional.
Not "when there's time." Built into the operating rhythm.

**Why it matters.** The expert positioning is a team property, not an
owner property. If only the founder is learning, the firm can never
sell more than one founder's worth of work. If the team is learning
continuously, expertise scales.

**How to apply.** Weekly reading. Quarterly skill assessments. Budget
for training, conferences, books. Tie learning to compensation or
promotion. Make it visible — share notes, post takeaways, codify
into team docs.

**Failure mode.** "We're too busy to train." Junior staff doing senior
work without senior reading. Founder is the only one with strategic
opinions. Knowledge concentrated in two heads.

---

## 7. We Will Be Selective

**What it says.** Saying no is the highest-leverage move in selling
expertise. Choose clients. Refuse work that doesn't fit position,
scale, or values.

**Why it matters.** Every yes to a wrong-fit client is a no to a
right-fit client you could have served instead. Wrong-fit clients
hurt margins, focus, team morale, and case study quality. Selectivity
is not a luxury — it is the discipline that makes everything else work.

**How to apply.** Have a written ICP. Have a written disqualification
list. In the Qualifying Conversation, lead with mutual-fit framing:
"this conversation is for both of us to decide if we should work
together." Walk away cleanly and early when fit is wrong.

**Failure mode.** Taking every dollar that walks in. Engagements that
don't match positioning. "We need the revenue" as a justification.
The team dreading Monday morning client calls.

## 8. We Will Not Solve Problems Before We Are Paid

**What it says.** Thinking is the product. You do not give thinking
away for free in pursuit of the sale. You learn enough to assess fit.
You do not diagnose, prescribe, or strategise in the sales process
without compensation.

**Why it matters.** Free thinking trains buyers to value the thinking
at its price — zero. It also destroys the option of paid diagnostics
later in the engagement. Once the buyer has free strategy, they will
not pay for it. Worse: the free strategy is often what they buy from
someone else.

**How to apply.** Draw a clean line. "Learning enough to assess fit"
is free. "Diagnosing the problem" is paid. If a prospect wants
strategic recommendations before contracting, sell a paid diagnostic
engagement — small, fixed scope, clear deliverable — as the on-ramp.
For Ivan: replace "free audit in the sales process" with "paid audit
as the first engagement."

**Failure mode.** Free audits. Free strategy decks. "Let me sketch out
how we'd approach it." Multiple discovery calls with the senior team.
The pitch becomes the project, except it's unpaid.

## 9. We Will Address Issues Of Money Early

**What it says.** Money in the first real meeting. Not the second.
Not the proposal. Early.

**Why it matters.** Avoiding money is the surest way to lose a deal
at the last moment. The buyer's budget is a pinch point. If you don't
find it early, you build a proposal aimed at a number you imagined,
which collides with the number they had, and the deal dies in week
six. Talk about money early and the pinch point surfaces while the
relationship still has room to navigate it.

**How to apply.** In the Qualifying Conversation, surface budget
range — not as a request, but as a calibration. "Engagements like
this typically range from X to Y depending on scope. Is that in the
right zone for you?" If the buyer can't or won't talk money in the
first meeting, that is a qualifying signal — usually disqualifying.

**Failure mode.** "We'll get to pricing later." Three meetings deep
with no budget discussion. The proposal arrives as a surprise. The
buyer ghosts.

## 10. We Will Refuse To Accept Work At A Loss

**What it says.** Every engagement is profitable. No exceptions. No
"loss leaders." No "we'll make it up on the next one."

**Why it matters.** Unprofitable work doesn't lead to profitable work.
It leads to more unprofitable work. The buyer who paid you below cost
expects to pay you below cost forever, and refers other buyers who
expect the same. Loss work is also corrosive to the team — the senior
people quietly subsidise the bad deal with their hours.

**How to apply.** Know your floor — the minimum fee at which an
engagement is profitable for the firm in your current cost structure.
Hold it. If a buyer can't meet it, walk. The floor is not embarrassing;
it is the expression of self-respect every other proclamation depends
on.

**Failure mode.** "We'll discount this one to win the logo." Heroic
hours subsidising a bad fee. The senior team working nights for free.
A pipeline full of below-cost engagements that block the team from
taking better work.

## 11. We Will Charge More

**What it says.** As expertise deepens and as the impact on the buyer's
business grows, the price grows with it. Charging more is not greed.
It is the honest reflection of value.

**Why it matters.** Underpriced experts are not generous — they are
under-resourced. They cannot afford to think for the client. They
take on more clients than they can serve properly. Charging more
creates room to do the work well. It also signals expertise to the
market in a way no portfolio can.

**How to apply.** Raise prices each year. Test the next tier with the
next prospect. Walk away from work that won't pay the new rate. Build
the proposal options around the new anchor, not the old floor. For
Ivan: the immigration vertical is high-value enough to support 3-5x
typical performance-agency fees if the engagement is priced to outcome
(signed retainer) rather than activity (campaigns managed).

**Failure mode.** Discount-by-default. Pricing to the cheapest
competitor. Apologising for the rate. Caving the second a buyer
flinches. The team works harder, the margin shrinks, the founder
burns out.

---

## 12. We Will Hold Our Heads High

**What it says.** Behave as professional practitioners — doctors,
lawyers, architects — not as suppliers. Seek respect first; money
follows. Refuse to grovel.

**Why it matters.** The seller-doctor stance is psychological before
it is tactical. If you behave as a vendor, you will be priced as a
vendor regardless of the quality of your work. If you behave as a
peer practitioner, you will be priced as one. The buyer cannot see
you any way other than how you are showing up.

**How to apply.** Watch the language. Watch the posture. Sit on the
same side of the table — figuratively and sometimes literally. Refuse
demeaning processes (spec work, mandatory pitches, free pilots).
Disagree when disagreement is warranted. Walk away calmly when the
process disrespects the work.

**Failure mode.** Apologising for the rate. Over-explaining the
methodology. Volunteering free work to "prove" capability. Accepting
last-minute meeting reshuffles, late payments, contemptuous
procurement. The accumulation of small concessions that say "we are
beneath you."

## Cross-Cutting Patterns

A few patterns repeat across the proclamations. Recognise them when
diagnosing.

**Pattern A — Power lives in expertise.** Proclamations 1, 5, 6, and
12 all point at the same thing: power in the seller-buyer relationship
flows from expertise scarcity, not from sales skill. Fix expertise
first; sales becomes easier.

**Pattern B — Money is a discipline, not a topic.** Proclamations 8,
9, 10, and 11 are the financial spine. Don't give thinking away. Talk
about money early. Hold the floor. Raise the ceiling. Each one supports
the others.

**Pattern C — Conversation beats performance.** Proclamations 2, 3,
and 4 are about replacing performance (pitches, decks, monologues)
with conversation (diagnosis, listening, mutual vetting). The expert
converses; the vendor performs.

**Pattern D — Selectivity is the multiplier.** Proclamation 7 is the
multiplier on every other proclamation. Without selectivity, all the
other commitments break under the weight of wrong-fit work.

## How To Diagnose A Stuck Engagement Against The Manifesto

When a deal is bleeding, run through the proclamations in this order
and ask which one is being violated. The first one to break is
usually the root cause.

1. Specialisation broken? → buyer can substitute you, so price is
   capped by competition.
2. Conversation skipped? → you're pitching instead of diagnosing,
   and the buyer is comparing decks.
3. Money avoided? → you don't know the pinch point, and the proposal
   is a guess.
4. Free thinking given? → buyer has the strategy, no longer needs you.
5. Loss work accepted? → you're already losing, before the kickoff.
6. Charging too little? → you can't afford to do the work well, the
   team burns, the case study suffers.
7. Vendor posture? → buyer leads, you respond, price is set by them.

The Manifesto is a checklist for self-respect. Most pricing problems
are positioning problems wearing pricing clothing.
