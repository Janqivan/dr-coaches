# Marketing Made Simple — Framework Map

**Authors:** Donald Miller + Dr. J.J. Peterson
**Primary source:** Marketing Made Simple: A Step-by-Step StoryBrand Guide for Any Business (HarperCollins Leadership, 2020)
**Source treatment:** Framework structures described in own words for synthetic-coach use. Book text is NOT reproduced. Each framework is named, mapped to its part of the book, and described mechanically so the /storybrand skill can apply it. To read Miller + Peterson's own prose — the templates, the swipe copy, the example funnels — get the book.

## Book map (the operator's view)

Building a StoryBrand answered "what do we SAY?" Marketing Made Simple answers "how do we DEPLOY what we say?" The book is a deployment manual. The output is a 5-part sales funnel that runs from first impression to closed sale.

| Part of the book | Asset it produces | Where it sits in the funnel |
|------------------|-------------------|------------------------------|
| One-liner | The compressed pitch | Used in every touchpoint |
| Wireframe | The long-form landing page | The site that captures interest |
| Lead generator | The PDF / quiz / checklist | Earns the email at low risk |
| Nurture sequence | 6+ weekly value emails | Builds authority over time |
| Sales sequence | 5-email closing campaign | Converts to purchase |

These are not optional pieces. Miller's claim — skip one and the funnel leaks. The book's own arc is a checklist for hire.

## Section 1 — The One-Liner (Part 1)

### Framework 1 — The One-Liner template

**What it is:** A single sentence that compresses an entire BrandScript into something a stranger can repeat at a dinner party. The atomic unit of the funnel. Used in email signatures, sales-call opens, networking events, above-the-fold subheads, LinkedIn bios, pitch decks.

**Three-part structure:**
1. **The problem the customer has** — stated in the customer's language
2. **The product / service you offer** — named plainly
3. **The result they'll experience** — concrete, future-state

**Template skeleton:**
> "You know how [problem]? Well, we [solution], so that [result]."

**Mechanism:** people don't buy what you sell; they buy what your product DOES for them. The one-liner anchors every conversation in the customer's outcome, not your process.

**Failure mode:** one-liners that read like a job description ("We're a full-service digital agency") instead of a transformation. If a stranger can't repeat it back after one hearing, it's broken.

## Section 2 — The Wireframe (Part 2)

### Framework 2 — The 9-Section StoryBrand Wireframe

**What it is:** The canonical landing-page section order for any StoryBrand-aligned long-form page. Not a design system — a section sequence. The order mirrors the customer's psychological flow from confusion to commitment.

**The nine sections, top to bottom:**
1. **Header** — one-liner + direct CTA + transitional CTA + a hero image where the customer is happy after using the product
2. **Stakes** — the cost of inaction, listed as 3 specific losses
3. **Value proposition** — 3 quick bullets naming what the customer gains
4. **Guide section** — empathy line + authority proof (testimonials, numbers, logos)
5. **Plan** — 3 customer-facing steps to buy / use / win
6. **Explanatory paragraph** — the long-form story arc of the brand-and-customer relationship
7. **Video** — optional, 90 seconds or less, mirrors the one-liner
8. **Price comparison / anchor** — frame the cost so the value is obvious
9. **Junk drawer** — FAQs, fine print, social proof, any leftover

**Mechanism:** every section answers a specific customer question in the order the customer is asking it. Skipping a section leaves a confusion gap that kills conversion.

**Failure mode:** putting the company history above the fold instead of the customer's problem-and-promise. The customer cares about themselves first; the brand is a guide, not a subject.

---

### Framework 3 — The Header Test

**What it is:** A specific 3-question gate for the above-the-fold section of the wireframe. A more granular cousin of the SB7 Grunt Test.

The header must answer:
1. **What do you offer?** (the product, named)
2. **How will my life be better if I buy it?** (the transformation, stated)
3. **What do I need to do to buy it?** (the direct CTA button)

**Where Miller is strict:** these answers must be visible without scrolling, on desktop AND mobile. The video doesn't count. The chat widget doesn't count. The "learn more" link doesn't count. Above the fold, in words.

**Failure mode:** headers that feature founder credentials or industry awards. Customer wants to know what's in it for them, not who you are.

## Section 3 — The Lead Generator (Part 3)

### Framework 4 — The PDF Lead Generator structure

**What it is:** The transitional asset that earns the email address. A short, high-value PDF (or quiz, or checklist) that solves a small piece of the customer's problem in exchange for contact info.

**The four qualifying tests:**
1. **Does it position the brand as a Guide on a specific problem?** (Not a generic "marketing tips PDF" — specific to one pain.)
2. **Is the title irresistible and curiosity-driven?** ("Five Things Every Immigration Attorney Should Track in Their Meta Ad Account" beats "Our Free Marketing Guide.")
3. **Is it short enough to consume in 10 minutes?** (Long PDFs go unread. 3-5 pages.)
4. **Does it open the door to the paid product?** (The lead magnet should naturally lead the reader to the next step, not be a dead end.)

**Formats Miller lists as proven:**
- PDF guide / checklist
- Quiz (scored, results-driven)
- Webinar replay
- Mini-video series
- Free-shipping or sample offer (for physical product)
- Discovery-call booking link

**Failure mode:** lead generators that solve no specific pain. "Sign up for our newsletter" is not a lead generator. It's a graveyard.

---

### Framework 5 — The Three Stages of Customer Relationship

**What it is:** Miller + Peterson's customer-journey model. The funnel exists because customers move through three psychological stages, and the asset chain must match where they are.

**The three stages:**
1. **Curiosity** — "Will this help me survive / thrive?" Answered by the one-liner and the header.
2. **Enlightenment** — "How does this work? Can I trust them?" Answered by the wireframe + lead generator + nurture emails.
3. **Commitment** — "I'm ready to pay. What's the ask?" Answered by the sales emails and the direct CTA.

**Mechanism:** asking for commitment during the curiosity stage gets the tab closed. Offering more curiosity content during commitment gets the buyer cold. Stage-match every asset.

**Failure mode:** sending the sales sequence before the nurture sequence. Or pitching on the lead-gen page. Customer must be enlightened before they'll commit.

## Section 4 — The Nurture Email Sequence (Part 4)

### Framework 6 — The Nurture Email rhythm

**What it is:** A 6+ email sequence (then ongoing weekly) sent to anyone who opted in via the lead generator. Pure value. No hard pitch. The job is to build the brand's authority and earn the right to sell later.

**The rotation Miller + Peterson teach:**
- **Tip emails** — one specific, useful tip per email. ~300 words.
- **Notification emails** — heads-up about new content, new resources, upcoming events.
- **Soft-pitch reminders** — occasional, light mentions of the paid product as a passing reference, not a hard sell.

**Send rhythm:** weekly. Same day, same time, ideally. Predictability builds the relationship.

**Mechanism:** the average buyer needs 5-12 touchpoints before purchase. The nurture sequence creates those touchpoints without burning the list with constant pitching.

**Failure mode:** writing nurture emails that pitch every time. The list unsubscribes. Or writing them like newsletters with five topics — pick one tip, one email.

---

### Framework 7 — The Email Subject Line Principle

**What it is:** Miller + Peterson's rule for email open rates. Subject lines should provoke curiosity, name a specific problem, or promise a clear payoff — and almost never be clever.

**Three subject-line types that work:**
1. **Curiosity gap** — "The one thing every immigration firm gets wrong on their homepage"
2. **Specific number / list** — "5 mistakes that kill your CPL"
3. **Direct promise** — "Here's how to fix your Meta ad waste in 30 minutes"

**Anti-pattern:** branded subject lines like "DR Weekly Roundup #47." Those are tab-closers.

**Failure mode:** treating subject lines as titles. Subject lines are hooks. If the open rate is below 25%, the subject line is the lever.

## Section 5 — The Sales Email Sequence (Part 5)

### Framework 8 — The 5-Email Sales Sequence

**What it is:** The closing campaign. A specific sequence of 5 emails sent to the warmed-up list when the brand is ready to drive purchases. Each email has a specific psychological job.

**The five emails in order:**
1. **Email 1 — Deliver the asset.** Send the lead magnet they signed up for. No pitch.
2. **Email 2 — The problem.** Name the external + internal problem the product solves. Resonate, don't pitch.
3. **Email 3 — The story / testimonial.** A customer's transformation, told as a mini-narrative. Social proof in story form.
4. **Email 4 — Overcome the objection.** Name the biggest reason people DON'T buy and dismantle it (price, time, risk, fit).
5. **Email 5 — The direct sales email + deadline.** Hard ask. Specific offer. Time limit. The only email in the sequence with an unambiguous "buy now."

**Mechanism:** the 5-email arc walks the prospect through the exact psychological sequence: notice the problem → see someone like me solve it → resolve my hesitation → take action under deadline pressure.

**Failure mode:** pitching in every email. The pitch only works in email 5 because the first four built the case. Skip the build, lose the close.

## Section 6 — The Marketing Rhythm (Part 6, the operating layer)

### Framework 9 — The Marketing Rhythm

**What it is:** The cadence that keeps the funnel alive after the initial build. The funnel is not a one-time project; it's a flywheel. Miller + Peterson lay out the recurring activity needed.

**The weekly marketing rhythm:**
- **Monday:** Write one new nurture email + queue for send.
- **Tuesday:** Post the same tip on LinkedIn / social, repurposed.
- **Wednesday:** Review funnel analytics — open rates, click rates, page conversion.
- **Thursday:** Make ONE specific optimization to the worst-performing asset.
- **Friday:** Plan next week's content theme.

**The quarterly marketing rhythm:**
- Run one new lead-generator campaign (new PDF, new quiz, new webinar).
- Refresh the wireframe based on the quarter's conversion data.
- Audit the sales sequence — update the testimonials and the offer.

**Mechanism:** a funnel without a rhythm rots within 90 days. The list goes cold, the wireframe goes stale, the testimonials age out. Rhythm prevents decay.

**Failure mode:** treating the funnel as "set and forget." The build is 30% of the work. The rhythm is 70%.

---

### Framework 10 — The Marketing Department Audit

**What it is:** A diagnostic Miller + Peterson use to identify which part of the funnel is the bottleneck. Run quarterly. Honest scoring against the 5-part chain.

**The 10-point audit (2 questions per funnel part):**

| Funnel part | Question 1 | Question 2 |
|-------------|------------|------------|
| One-liner | Can every team member recite it? | Does it appear in every touchpoint? |
| Wireframe | Does it pass the header test? | Is the section order correct? |
| Lead generator | Does it solve a specific pain? | Is the opt-in rate above 5%? |
| Nurture sequence | Are emails going out weekly? | Are open rates above 25%? |
| Sales sequence | Is there a written 5-email sequence? | Has it been used in the last 90 days? |

**Mechanism:** find the weakest link. Fix it. Re-audit in 90 days. Funnel performance compounds when the weakest piece is upgraded, not when the strongest piece is polished.

**Where DR uses it:** every client onboarding includes a marketing-rhythm audit before any new ad spend. No funnel = paid ads pour water through a sieve.

## How to use this file inside the /storybrand skill

1. Diagnose whether the messaging problem is the SAY layer (use storybrand_sb7_frameworks.md) or the DEPLOY layer (use this file).
2. If the customer already has clean SB7 messaging but isn't converting — almost always a deployment problem. Run the Marketing Department Audit (Framework 10) to find the weakest funnel piece.
3. If the customer is building a funnel from scratch — work the funnel in order: one-liner → wireframe → lead gen → nurture → sales. Skipping ahead breaks the chain.
4. When recommending specific copy formats (PDF lead gen, nurture email rhythm, sales 5-email arc), pull the exact mechanism from the matching framework here.
5. NEVER substitute this file for the book — these are operator-grade summaries. For Miller + Peterson's voice, swipe copy templates, and worked examples, point at the book and the Marketing Made Simple Podcast.
