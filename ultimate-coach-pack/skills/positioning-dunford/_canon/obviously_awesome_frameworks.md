The book is structured as 10 chapters. The first three reframe what positioning is (and isn't). Chapters 4-8 install the 5-Step Process one step per chapter. ## Framework 1 — Default Positioning Failure (Ch 1)

**What it is:** the silent failure mode that produces most bad positioning. The founder never made a deliberate market category choice. The product's category is whatever the first customer happened to call it, or whatever the dominant competitor labels itself. The unique attributes get buried because the inherited category frames buyers' expectations around the wrong set of features.

**Diagnostic signals:**
- Sales reps describe the product five different ways
- The homepage and the sales deck disagree about what the product is
- Win/loss interviews show prospects "got confused" or "thought it was something else"
- Pricing keeps slipping because there's no clean category anchor

**Dunford's hard rule:** default positioning is the worst kind of positioning. Any deliberate choice — even a flawed one — outperforms drift.

## Framework 2 — Why Geoffrey Moore's Template Fails (Ch 2)

**The Mad Libs:** *"For [target customer] who [statement of need], our [product] is a [category] that [key benefit]. Unlike [competition], our product [unique differentiator]."*

**Why Dunford rejects it:**
- It assumes you already know the right category — but choosing the category IS the work
- It collapses target + need + category + benefit + differentiator into a single sentence, which forces compression too early
- It produces lookalike output across every company that uses it
- It treats positioning as a copy artifact, not as a strategy artifact

**The replacement:** the 10-Component canvas. Each component gets its own field. Compression happens later, on the marketing team's side, when copy gets written.

## Framework 3 — The 5-Step Positioning Process (Ch 3-8, master frame)

The whole book is a long argument that positioning is a strategic process, not a writing task. Five steps, executed in order:

1. **Competitive alternatives** — what customers would actually do if you didn't exist
2. **Unique attributes** — features, capabilities, relationships, expertise only you have
3. **Value** — the buyer-side outcome those attributes produce
4. **Best-fit customers** — the narrow slice for whom that value is urgent
5. **Market category** — the frame of reference that makes the unique attributes feel inevitable

**Execution rule:** you can't skip a step. You can't start at step 5 ("let's invent a category") and back-fill the rest. The category is an OUTPUT of steps 1-4, not an input.

**Failure mode:** founders typically try to start at step 5 (because the category feels glamorous) or at step 2 (because they're emotionally attached to their features). Both produce default positioning.

## Framework 4 — Step 1: Competitive Alternatives (Ch 4)

**What it is:** the brutally honest list of what your best-fit customer would do if you didn't exist.

**Three buckets:**
1. **Do-nothing / status quo** — the manual workaround, the spreadsheet, the intern doing it by hand, the "we just live with this problem." Almost always the real competition.
2. **Internal builds** — the customer's team building it themselves.
3. **Other categories of solution** — products in a different category that solve a related problem in a worse way.

**Execution rule:** ask actual customers (especially recent buyers and recent losses) what they evaluated alongside you. Don't trust your founder-instinct list — it's almost always wishful.

**Failure mode:** listing the competitors you wish you had instead of the alternatives the buyer actually considered. This produces a positioning that defends against ghosts and ignores the real fight.

---

## Framework 5 — Step 2: Unique Attributes (Ch 5)

**What it is:** features, capabilities, relationships, data, expertise, or partnerships that you have and the listed alternatives do not.

**Five categories of attribute to scan for:**
1. Product features and capabilities
2. Data assets or proprietary data sources
3. Relationships (channel, partnership, integration)
4. Expertise (the team's track record, credentials)
5. Business-model differences (pricing model, delivery model)

**Execution rule:** an attribute is only "unique" if it's demonstrably true AND not held by at least one of the listed alternatives. "We care about customer success" is not an attribute — every alternative claims it.

**Failure mode:** padding the list with platitudes. If a competitor's marketing site could plausibly claim the same attribute, strike it.

---

## Framework 6 — Step 3: Value (Ch 6)

**What it is:** the translation of each unique attribute into a buyer-side outcome that matters.

**Mechanism:**
- Attribute = thing you have
- Value = thing the buyer gets because you have it
- Value theme = grouping of related values into a single buyer-meaningful headline

**Execution rule:** each value must answer "so what?" from the buyer's perspective. If the answer to "so what?" is another feature, you haven't translated yet.

**Failure mode:** stopping at the attribute. "We have proprietary data" is not value. "You can target audiences your competitors can't see" is value.

---

## Framework 7 — Step 4: Best-Fit Customers (Ch 7)

**What it is:** the narrow slice of the market for whom your value themes hit hardest. The customers who buy fast, stay long, pay full price, and refer.

**Five axes to define them:**
1. **Firmographic** — industry, size, geography, business model
2. **Trigger event** — what just happened that made them start looking
3. **Emotional driver** — what they're afraid of, what they're trying to prove, what status they're protecting
4. **Buying-committee map** — who pushes, who blocks, who signs
5. **Negative best-fit** — lookalike customers who LOOK like best-fit but actually churn, complain, or never close

**Execution rule:** if you can't name 3-5 real companies that match the best-fit definition, the definition isn't real yet. Name them.

**Failure mode:** widening the best-fit definition to capture more TAM. The best-fit is supposed to be narrow. Widening it dilutes every downstream message.

---

## Framework 8 — Step 5: Market Category (Ch 8)

**What it is:** the frame of reference inside which your unique attributes look obviously valuable.

**Three deliberate choices:**
1. **Position into an existing category** — low risk, high constraint. You inherit the buyer's expectations for that category. Works when your unique attributes line up with what category buyers already want more of.
2. **Position against a category** — claim you're a better instance of an existing category, while calling out what the dominant players get wrong. Higher risk, higher payoff. Works when there's a real flaw in the dominant approach.
3. **Create a new category** — highest risk, highest reward. Only viable when (a) the market is genuinely missing a frame, (b) the unique attributes can't fit any existing category honestly, and (c) you can fund the buyer education needed for a new category to take hold.

**Execution rule:** the deliberate market category choice has downstream consequences for pricing, sales motion, buying committee, and feature roadmap. Make it explicit. Write it down. Get the leadership team to agree on a single sentence.

**Failure mode:** drifting into a category by default and only realizing it months later when the sales pitch keeps mutating.

---

## Framework 9 — Step (+1): Relevant Trends (Ch 9)

**What it is:** the optional trend you attach to your positioning to make the category feel urgent.

**Mechanism:** trends create buyer urgency. AI, privacy regulation, remote work, supply-chain reshoring — these macro forces give buyers a reason to act now. Riding the right trend makes a slow category feel hot.

**Hard rule:** only ride a trend that's GENUINELY tied to your unique attributes. If the trend is bolted on, buyers smell it. "AI-powered" plastered on a category that doesn't need AI is the most common version of this failure.

**Failure mode:** chasing every trend cycle (AI this quarter, sustainability next quarter). Pick one, only if it makes your unique attributes feel more relevant, and only if you can sustain the claim.

---

## Framework 10 — The 10-Component Positioning Canvas (Ch 10)

**What it is:** the one-page artifact that captures the output of the 5-Step Process plus the operational components a go-to-market team needs.

**The 10 components:**
1. Competitive alternatives
2. Unique attributes
3. Value
4. Best-fit customer characteristics
5. Market category
6. Relevant trends (optional)
7. Trigger event
8. Buying committee
9. Differentiated value themes (3-4)
10. Sales narrative (the story arc)

**Execution rule:** the canvas is a leadership-alignment artifact, not a marketing brief. Every leadership-team member should be able to recite it. Sales should be able to repeat it on a call.

**Failure mode:** treating the canvas as a marketing-team document. If the CEO, head of product, and head of sales can't agree on the 10 components in one room, you don't have positioning yet — you have wishful thinking.

---

## Framework 11 — Rollout: Getting Positioning to Stick (Ch 10)

**The rollout sequence:**
1. Lock the 10-Component canvas with leadership
2. Brief product (so the roadmap reflects the unique attributes)
3. Brief sales (so the sales pitch storyboard rebuilds around the new frame)
4. Brief marketing (so messaging and copy rewrite from the canvas, not from the old homepage)
5. Brief customer success (so onboarding and account expansion match the best-fit definition)

**Hard rule:** if positioning lives only on a slide deck, it dies. It has to show up in the product roadmap, the sales pitch, the homepage, the onboarding flow, and the renewal conversation. Same canvas, same vocabulary, every surface.

**Failure mode:** marketing writes new copy on top of the new positioning, but product, sales, and CS keep working off the old default. Buyers experience a fractured story. Positioning collapses back to default within two quarters.
