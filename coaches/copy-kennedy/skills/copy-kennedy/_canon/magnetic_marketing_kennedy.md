## Kennedy's worldview on attraction marketing

Most marketing is push. The operator chases the prospect — cold calls, mass ads, billboards, polished brand campaigns aimed at "everyone". Kennedy's contempt: this is the worst possible economics. The attracted customer is worth a multiple of the pursued customer. The right system flips the polarity — prospects raise their hand, qualify themselves, and step forward asking to be sold. The Magnetic Marketing canon is the architecture for that flip.

Three operator implications run through every chapter:

1. **Mass marketing is for fools and Fortune 500s.** Small and mid-market operators who imitate Coca-Cola die slowly. Direct response is the only marketing the small operator can afford and the only marketing the small operator can measure.
2. **Generic offers attract generic customers — or none at all.** Specificity is the entire game. A specific message to a specific avatar through a specific medium with a specific offer outperforms broad reach every time.
3. **Customers are not all equal.** The best 20% of a list will out-buy the bottom 80% by an order of magnitude. Treat the list as a stratified asset, not a flat one.

## Framework 1 — The 4-Step Magnetic Marketing System

**What it is:** Kennedy's master sequence for engineering attraction. Four steps, each gating the next. Skip a step and the system breaks.

**The four steps:**

**Step 1 — Right MESSAGE.**
The message is the offer + the promise + the language. The bar:
- Is it specific to one avatar, not "everyone"?
- Does it lead with a benefit the avatar already cares about (not the operator's product features)?
- Does it use the avatar's own language — words they would use to describe the problem to a friend?
- Does it have a reason-why that justifies the promise?
- Is there a clear, irresistible call to respond — not just "learn more"?

A message that tests OK on all five points is "magnetic" — it pulls the right reader in and repels the wrong one. A message that tests weak on any one point becomes background noise.

**Step 2 — Right MARKET (Audience).**
The audience is who receives the message. The bar:
- Is the avatar defined to the level where you could describe one specific person — their job, income, pains, prior attempts to solve the problem?
- Do you have access to a list / channel / audience that contains a high concentration of that avatar?
- Have you ranked the audience by likelihood-to-buy (recency, frequency, monetary value, prior engagement)?
- Have you removed people who LOOK like the avatar but don't behave like them?

A correctly defined market lets a mediocre message succeed. A wrongly defined market makes the best message in the world fail.

**Step 3 — Right TIME (Media / Moment).**
The right time is BOTH (a) the medium that reaches the avatar where they're already paying attention, and (b) the moment in the avatar's day or buying cycle when they're receptive. The bar:
- Are you reaching them in the medium they consume most — direct mail, email, social, podcast, YouTube, in-person event, search?
- Are you reaching them at a moment of relevance — a trigger event, a seasonal cycle, a life event, a buying window?
- Are you running enough frequency to break through, or are you a one-shot?

Most operators botch this by reaching the right avatar through the wrong medium, or by reaching them in the right medium at the wrong moment in their cycle.

**Step 4 — Right OFFER.**
The offer is what the avatar gets in exchange for action. The bar:
- Is it irresistible to the chosen avatar — would they feel stupid saying no?
- Does it stack value (core + bonuses + guarantees) so the perceived value far exceeds the price?
- Does it have a deadline, scarcity, or other reason to act now?
- Does it remove the buyer's risk (guarantee, trial, refund)?
- Is the next action one click / one call / one decision — no friction?

Kennedy's rule: a strong offer can save a weak message; a weak offer cannot be saved by a strong message.

**Operator use:**
Run all four steps in order before any campaign launch. A failure in step 1 or 2 means kill the campaign. A failure in step 3 or 4 means redesign and re-launch. Never proceed with one of the four steps marked "good enough".

## Framework 2 — The Magnetic Marketing Triangle (deep)

**What it is:** The three-sided alignment check that compresses the 4-Step system into a faster diagnostic. Use the Triangle when you don't have time to walk every step.

**The three sides:**

- **Right MESSAGE.** Compresses step 1. The offer + promise + language must fit the avatar exactly.
- **Right MARKET.** Compresses step 2. The audience must contain the avatar at high concentration.
- **Right MEDIA.** Compresses steps 3 and 4 (channel + moment + delivery format).

**Kennedy's diagnostic question:** when a campaign fails, which side of the Triangle broke?
- If the message is great but response is low → market problem (you're shouting the right thing at the wrong people).
- If the market is right but response is low → message or media problem (right people, wrong words OR right people, wrong channel).
- If the message and market both test well → the media or offer is broken (right people, right words, wrong delivery or wrong incentive).

**Operator note:** the Triangle is a deeper version of the Message/Market/Media frame summarized in the No B.S. Direct Marketing canon. Use the 4-Step system for design. Use the Triangle for diagnosis. They are the same idea at different resolutions.

## Framework 3 — The Mass-Marketing Failure Diagnosis

**What it is:** Kennedy's argument for why most marketing fails. Useful when an operator brings you a "we tried marketing and it didn't work" story.

**The failure modes:**

1. **The message is generic.** It tries to appeal to "everyone" and therefore repels no one and attracts no one. Magnetic Marketing requires polarity — the right reader leans in, the wrong reader walks away. Beige copy gets ignored.
2. **There's no offer.** It's a brochure, not an ad. Information without invitation produces nothing.
3. **There's no deadline.** "Call us when you're ready" is the marketer's version of giving up. No deadline = no response.
4. **There's no measurable response mechanism.** No tracked phone number, no UTM, no dedicated landing page, no source code. The operator can't tell what worked and what didn't.
5. **It's a one-shot.** One ad, one email, one mailer. The operator hits send and waits. Magnetic Marketing requires sequence — multi-touch over weeks.
6. **It looks pretty.** Designed by an agency for an awards portfolio, not for response. Kennedy's lean: ugly direct response usually outpulls beautiful brand work.
7. **It costs more than it produces.** The operator never measured cost-per-lead, cost-per-sale, or lifetime value. They have no idea whether the campaign was profitable.

**Operator use:** when a prospect says "marketing doesn't work for us", run this list. It's almost always one of the seven, not a market problem.

## Framework 4 — Customer Attraction Principles

**What it is:** The underlying principles that make the 4-Step system work. The frame for *why* magnetic marketing attracts when push marketing repels.

**The principles:**

- **Polarity over politeness.** Take a position. Have an enemy. Pick the side of the conversation that earns you fans and detractors. Beige operators get ignored; polarized operators get hired.
- **Specificity over breadth.** A narrow promise to a narrow avatar beats a wide promise to a wide audience. The narrower the niche, the easier it is to be magnetic.
- **Self-qualification.** A great magnetic ad does work the sales team would otherwise do — it filters out tire-kickers, time-wasters, and price-shoppers BEFORE they ever talk to a human.
- **Reciprocity through value-first.** Give a piece of valuable IP up front (a guide, a video, a case study, a tool). The avatar receives value before they're asked to buy. Reciprocity does the rest.
- **Authority signaling.** Books, podcasts, articles, video, awards, named clients. The avatar must perceive the operator as the expert *before* the buying conversation, not during it.
- **Owned audience compounding.** Every magnetic campaign captures the prospect into a list owned by the operator. The list is the asset. The platform is rented land.

**Operator use:** when a campaign is technically correct (Triangle aligned, 4 steps run) but still underperforming, audit these principles. Usually the operator is too polite, too broad, or too unwilling to take a position.

## Framework 5 — The Lead Magnet & Conversion Sequence

**What it is:** The mechanism by which an attracted prospect becomes a buyer. Kennedy's standard architecture.

**The sequence:**

1. **Lead Magnet.** A specific, valuable, problem-solving asset — a guide, checklist, video training, free chapter, scoring tool, swipe file — offered free in exchange for the prospect's contact information. The magnet must be valuable enough that the prospect would have paid for it.
2. **Capture page.** Single purpose: get the prospect onto the list. No navigation, no distractions, no "learn more about us". Headline + benefit + form + button.
3. **Immediate delivery + bridge.** Deliver the magnet instantly. Inside the delivery, bridge to the next step — a video, a sales letter, a call invitation. The prospect is at peak attention; don't waste the window.
4. **Indoctrination sequence.** A multi-email (or multi-touch) sequence over 5-14 days that does three jobs: (a) gives the prospect a quick win from the magnet, (b) establishes the operator's authority and worldview, (c) introduces the offer.
5. **Offer presentation.** A dedicated long-form asset — sales letter, VSL, webinar, application page — that presents the paid offer with full Kennedy Sales Letter Architecture.
6. **Follow-up sequence.** Multi-week, multi-channel sequence for prospects who didn't buy on the first offer. Most sales come after touch 7+, not touch 1.
7. **Re-engagement sequence.** For the dormant portion of the list — quarterly campaigns to reactivate cold subscribers with a new hook, new mechanism, or new offer.

**Kennedy's rule:** the operator who runs the full sequence will out-earn the operator who runs only steps 1-3 by a wide margin. Most operators stop at step 3 and wonder why their lists don't convert.
