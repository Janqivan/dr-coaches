# Peep Laja's CRO and B2B Differentiation Principles

The twelve principles below are the operating system behind Laja's
writing on cxl.com, his How to Win podcast, the peeplaja.com
newsletter, Wynter research drops, and his LinkedIn essays. They
explain why some pages convert and others don't, and why almost
every B2B SaaS company looks identical to its competitors.

These are not best practices. Peep refuses the phrase. A best
practice is what wins on average across all categories — by
definition mediocre for any specific one. These are principles
that hold across categories, even when the tactics differ.

---

## 1. RESEARCH BEATS OPINION

The defining stance. Every CRO decision should be traceable to
data — analytics, recordings, qualitative research, user testing,
message tests against real ICP buyers. Decisions made in
conference rooms by people staring at the page are guesses, and
guesses lose at scale.

The corollary: when a senior person says "let's test X," the
correct response is "what does the research say?" If the answer
is nothing, the test is a guess wearing a lab coat. Slow down,
do the research, then test.

This is why Peep built CXL (to teach research-first marketing),
Speero (to do research-first CRO for clients), and Wynter (to
make B2B message research cheap and fast). The three companies
are the same idea applied at education, services, and product
layers.

---

## 2. THE VALUE PROPOSITION FORMULA

Three components, all required:

- **Relevance** — Does this clearly state what you do and who it's
  for, in the customer's language? Not your branded internal
  language. Their language, pulled from sales calls, reviews, and
  qualitative research.
- **Quantified end-benefit** — What specific outcome does the
  customer get? Not "powerful automation" — "cut your onboarding
  time from 14 days to 2 days." Specificity is the price of being
  believed.
- **Unique differentiation** — Why you and not the obvious
  alternative? If the visitor could swap your name for a competitor's
  on the page and the copy still makes sense, you have failed this
  test.

The hero of every page must answer these three. If it answers two
of three, conversion suffers. If it answers one of three, you have
a "leading platform for [generic noun]" page and you are doing
sameness with extra steps.

---

## 3. THE CONVERSION SEQUENCE — MOTIVATION × VALUE × INCENTIVE − FRICTION − ANXIETY

Adapted from MECLABS. The probability of conversion is driven by:

- **Motivation** — Did they arrive wanting this badly? (Largely set
  by source, search query, ad, referrer.)
- **Value proposition clarity** — Does the page communicate the
  value match?
- **Incentive** — Is there a reason to act now? (Free trial,
  guarantee, time-limited offer.)
- **Friction** — Is the path to conversion clean? (Form length,
  page speed, CTA clarity, layout.)
- **Anxiety** — What worries them about signing up? (Price hidden,
  security unknown, social proof missing.)

The page can't lift motivation much — motivation is set upstream by
the traffic source. But the page can hammer clarity, hammer incentive,
strip friction, and address anxiety. Most of the wins are on the
friction-and-anxiety side, but only after motivation-and-clarity are
in place.

Common misdiagnosis: junior CROs strip friction (shorten the form)
when the real problem is motivation (the page doesn't make anyone
want it). Strip friction on a non-motivating page and conversion
stays flat.

---

## 4. THE LIMITED CONSIDERATION SET

In a mature B2B category, buyers shortlist 3–5 vendors before they
ever fill in a demo form. Wynter's B2B Buyer Journey research showed
~78% of buyers shortlist three vendors max. If you are not in the
three when the buyer decides to evaluate, you don't get the chance
to convert them — no matter how good your landing page is.

This reframes "marketing" in B2B. The job is not lead gen. The job
is **mental availability** — being the vendor the buyer thinks of
first when the buying situation arises. Mental availability is
built upstream of the demo form: content, podcast presence, founder
visibility, customer noise, peer recommendations, brand campaigns.

A great landing page on a brand nobody has heard of converts a small
percentage of warm traffic and gets crushed by a mediocre page on a
brand the buyer already trusts.

---

## 5. THREE WAYS TO STAND OUT — INNOVATION, POSITIONING, PENETRATION

There are only three real ways to differentiate. Everything else
is decoration.

- **Innovation** — Build a genuinely better product. In B2B SaaS,
  the window is short — features get copied in months. Innovation
  works when you are first AND fast AND keep moving.
- **Positioning** — Be the obvious choice for a specific segment,
  use case, or job-to-be-done. Narrow until you are the only
  serious answer. Cheapest, most defensible, most overlooked.
- **Penetration** — Outspend competitors on share of voice. Requires
  capital. Works when you have it. Doesn't when you don't.

If a B2B company can't articulate which of the three they are doing,
they are doing none — they are doing sameness with extra steps.

---

## 6. BRAND IS YOUR MOAT

In a category where features get copied, the only durable advantage
is brand. Brand here means: the set of associations a buyer has when
they hear your name. Strong brand = high mental availability + clear
positioning + emotional pull.

Brand is built through:

- A founder or operator with a public voice
- Original research and content nobody else publishes
- A point of view about the category that competitors won't say
- An identifiable visual and verbal style
- Customer noise (case studies, reviews, social mentions)
- Consistent presence over years

Brand is also the thing every short-termist scoffs at because it
doesn't show up in this quarter's pipeline report. Peep's stance:
build the brand, or get crushed by someone who did.

---

## 7. SAMENESS IS THE DEFAULT — AND IT'S FATAL

The default state of any mature B2B category is that every player
looks, sounds, and pitches the same. Hero copy says "the platform
for [job]." Screenshot shows the same dashboard. Logo wall in the
same layout. Testimonial in the same format. Feature list ticks
the same boxes. "Trusted by, easy, fast, powerful." Everyone.

Sameness happens because:

- Teams study competitors and inch toward them
- Best practices produce the same page everywhere
- Founders are afraid to take a stance
- Marketing is run by committee, and committees average

The result: buyers can't tell vendors apart, default to the loudest
brand or cheapest price, and the rest get scraps. If you look the
same, you compete on price. Competing on price kills B2B economics.

The fix: pick a stance, name an enemy (the wrong way of doing
things), specialize, develop a strategic narrative, write copy in
the customer's voice not the branded voice, and test the message
against real ICP buyers before shipping.

---

## 8. THE STRATEGIC NARRATIVE

Bigger than positioning. A strategic narrative answers: what
changed in the world that makes our category necessary now, what
is the old wrong way of doing things, and why is the new way (us)
inevitable?

Peep on strategic narrative: positioning sits inside a larger
story about why now and why not the old way. Without the narrative,
positioning is just a tagline. With the narrative, positioning
becomes a worldview the buyer adopts before they buy.

The strategic narrative is the most copy-able and least copied B2B
asset. Most companies don't write one because it requires the
founder to have a real opinion about the world. Most founders prefer
to play it safe. Most companies look the same as a result.

---

## 9. THE T-SHAPED MARKETER

CRO and growth are not a single skill. The horizontal bar of the T
includes literacy in: analytics, copywriting, UX, behavioral
psychology, statistics, qualitative research, brand strategy,
demand generation, and experimentation discipline. The vertical
spike is deep expertise in one or two of those.

Implications for hiring: when you hire a CRO person, ask what
their spike is and where their blind spots are. Then ask whether
the rest of the team covers those blind spots. A team that is all
analytics-deep and zero copy-deep will run clean tests on broken
messages. A team that is all copy-deep and zero stats-deep will
declare wins on noise.

The horizontal literacy is the harder part to develop. CXL was
built to teach it.

## 10. WHY BEST PRACTICES DON'T WORK

A best practice is the average of what works across all sites in
all categories with all audiences. By definition it is the
middle of the distribution. The wins live on the edges.

If you do best practices, you produce a page that is identical to
every competitor's best-practice page. That page might convert at
the category average — but in a competitive market the average
loses to the differentiated. You can't win by being average.

Best practices are useful as a baseline (do these unless you have
a reason not to), not as a goal. The goal is to find what works
for *your* page, *your* audience, *your* offer — which is what
ResearchXL is for.

The strongest signal a marketer doesn't know what they are doing
is the phrase "best practices say." The next strongest is "what's
the industry benchmark." Industry benchmarks describe the average.
You want to be above the average. The benchmark doesn't tell you
how.

## 11. SPECIFICITY OVER GENERIC CLAIMS

Generic claims are invisible. Specific claims are believed.

Generic: "We help companies grow." Specific: "We help B2B SaaS
companies between $5M and $50M ARR add a second growth channel
in 90 days."

Generic: "Powerful automation." Specific: "Cut your onboarding
from 14 days to 2 days by automating the seven steps your team
does manually."

Generic: "Trusted by leading brands." Specific: "Used by 600+
sales teams including [three named recognizable logos]."

Specificity does three things at once:
- Disqualifies non-fit visitors (a feature, not a bug)
- Signals you actually know your customer
- Beats the sameness test — competitors can't match the specifics
  without obviously copying

The trade: specificity narrows the market and turns away non-fit
buyers. That is what makes it work. Trying to be relevant to
everyone makes you relevant to nobody.

---

## 12. TEST THE MESSAGE BEFORE YOU SHIP THE PAGE

The most expensive mistake in B2B marketing: build a page, ship
it, run paid traffic to it for three months, then discover the
message didn't land. Three months of media budget gone, three
months of sales pipeline missed.

The fix: test the message against real ICP buyers before the page
goes live. Wynter exists for this — recruit five to fifty buyers
in the target role, show them the copy, ask whether they understand
what it is, whether they'd consider it, what's missing, what
confused them. Iterate the copy until the comprehension and
appeal scores hit a defensible bar. Then ship.

Alternatives if Wynter is not used:
- 5-second tests on UserTesting.com with target-role recruits
- Cold outreach with copy variants in subject lines and openers,
  measuring reply rate
- Sales call mining — read 50 demo recordings, extract the
  language buyers actually use, write the page in that language
- Customer interviews — 5–10 best-fit customers, deep questions
  about how they describe the problem and the solution

There is no excuse for shipping unmessage-tested copy in 2026.
The cost of testing is now in the low hundreds of dollars and the
upside is the rest of the campaign's success.

## HOW THESE PRINCIPLES CONNECT

The principles fit together as a system:

1. Don't guess — research (P1, P11)
2. Get the value proposition right (P2, P3)
3. Make sure you can even be considered (P4, P5)
4. Build a durable brand and narrative (P6, P9)
5. Refuse the path to sameness (P7, P10, P12)
6. Hire and build for the breadth the work requires (P8)
7. Test everything important against real buyers (P12)

The teams that win in B2B are the ones who run all seven layers
at once. The teams that lose either skip research (1), skip
differentiation (5), or hire one-trick CRO people and call it a
program (6).

Peep's bottom-line frame: "If you're just like everyone else,
it's a hard life." (Public quote, How to Win podcast positioning
episode — https://howtowin.transistor.fm/episodes/differentiation-how-to-stand-out-keep-it-that-way)
