# Copyhackers — The Standing Principles

Source canon: 15 years of Copyhackers blog posts (2011-present), the
Tutorial Tuesdays YouTube library, Wiebe's CTAConf / MozCon / INBOUND /
Business of Software / CXL Live talks, the Copy Hackers book series, and
the operator vocabulary Wiebe has codified through Copy School.

These are the principles Wiebe returns to. They're not formulas. They're
the operating beliefs that decide how she approaches a page, an email, an
audit, or a critique.

---

## PRINCIPLE 1 — KILL THE PIXIE DUST

"Pixie dust" is Wiebe's term for fluffy marketing-speak that sounds nice
and means nothing.

Examples:
- "We're passionate about helping you succeed."
- "Cutting-edge solutions for forward-thinking businesses."
- "Empowering your team to achieve more."
- "Best-in-class, world-class, enterprise-grade."

Pixie dust is the dialect of the brand voice agency. It's adjective-heavy,
evidence-light, and could be lifted onto any competitor's page without
changing the meaning. That last part is the test: if the copy could
belong to anyone, it belongs to no one.

The cure is specificity. "Helps you achieve more" → "Cuts your monthly
close from 14 days to 4." "Cutting-edge" → "Built on the only stack that
processes 50K events per second on a single instance." Numbers, names,
dates, processes. Specifics.

If you can't be specific, you're not ready to write. Go back to research.

---

## PRINCIPLE 2 — WRITE LIKE A HUMAN, CONVERT LIKE A BADASS

The Copyhackers motto. The two halves matter equally.

"Write like a human" — sound like a person talking, not a corporate
press release. Use contractions. Use asides. Use the exact phrases your
reader uses. Read your draft out loud. If you stumble, the copy is too
stiff.

"Convert like a badass" — but never at the expense of the conversion
goal. Plain talk doesn't mean lazy talk. Plain talk means you're not
hiding behind jargon. The page still has to do the job: move the reader
to yes.

The mistake: assuming you have to choose. Conversion copywriting refuses
the choice. Plain + specific + evidence-rich = both.

## PRINCIPLE 3 — THE PAGE IS QUALIFYING, NOT JUST CONVERTING

A landing page that converts everyone converts the wrong people too. A
good page filters — it pulls the right reader closer and pushes the
wrong reader away.

Practical implication: specificity in the headline qualifies. "For solo
founders making under $10K/month" qualifies. "For ambitious
entrepreneurs" qualifies nobody.

A page should:
- **Convert** the right reader (the One Reader)
- **Qualify out** the wrong reader (the not-One-Reader)

Conversion rate goes up when you're brave enough to push the wrong
reader away. Counterintuitive but true.

## PRINCIPLE 4 — THE HERO DOES 80% OF THE CONVERSION WORK

Most visitors decide within the hero section whether to keep scrolling.
Most A/B test wins come from hero changes. Most rewrite ROI comes from
the hero.

The hero has to answer four questions, in this order, within 6 seconds:

1. **What is this?** (clarity — the product / offer category)
2. **What does it do for me?** (value — the transformation)
3. **Why should I believe it?** (proof — social, data, or logo anchor)
4. **What do I do next?** (CTA — the button copy)

Most heroes nail one or two of the four. Excellent heroes nail all four.

If you have 8 hours to spend on a page, spend 6 of them on the hero.

## PRINCIPLE 5 — EVIDENCE BEATS ADJECTIVES

Every claim earns its place by carrying evidence. Without evidence, a
claim is a brag. With evidence, a claim is a fact.

Adjective copy: "Trusted by thousands of teams."
Evidence copy: "5,432 marketing teams. 4.8 stars on G2. Backed by Y
Combinator."

Adjective copy: "Lightning-fast performance."
Evidence copy: "Loads in 0.4 seconds — 18x faster than the industry
average (CrUX, 2025)."

Adjective copy: "Save time and stress less."
Evidence copy: "The average customer cuts their workweek by 4.2 hours
within 60 days."

The "prove it" sweep (see Principle 11) catches adjective copy and
demands evidence. If you can't supply evidence, you can't make the
claim.

## PRINCIPLE 6 — THE RULE OF ONE

Lock these four before you write a word:
- **One Reader** — a specific person, not a segment
- **One Offer** — a specific thing, not a portfolio
- **One Promise** — a specific transformation, not "value"
- **One Big Idea** — a specific hook, not a category

If any of the four is vague, the page will be vague. If all four are
specific, the page almost writes itself.

The Rule of One is the single most violated principle in B2B SaaS
copywriting. Most homepages have three Readers, two Offers, four
Promises, and zero Big Ideas. That's why they read like a Wikipedia
entry instead of a sales letter.

## PRINCIPLE 7 — DON'T WRITE COPY. FIND IT.

The best copywriters listen first, write second. The most-converting
headline is almost always a verbatim quote from a real customer.

You don't sit down at a blank page and brainstorm. You sit down at a VoC
spreadsheet and look for the line that keeps repeating, the phrase that
keeps surfacing, the metaphor that keeps appearing. That's your headline.

This is the single biggest difference between conversion copywriting and
brand-voice copywriting. Brand voice tries to invent. Conversion
copywriting reports.

## PRINCIPLE 8 — MATCH THE MESSAGE TO THE STAGE OF AWARENESS

Eugene Schwartz's 5 Stages of Awareness (1966 — pre-dates Wiebe, she
uses heavily):

1. **Unaware** — doesn't know they have the problem
2. **Problem-aware** — feels the pain, doesn't know solutions exist
3. **Solution-aware** — knows solutions exist, doesn't know which is best
4. **Product-aware** — knows your product, hasn't decided to buy
5. **Most-aware** — already convinced, just needs the close

Page length and content shift dramatically by stage:
- Unaware → long lead-up, story-first, no product mentioned for half
  the page
- Problem-aware → name the pain in their words, agitate, introduce
  category
- Solution-aware → compare options, position yours
- Product-aware → proof, objections, offer, close
- Most-aware → short, direct, ask for the order

The most common mistake in B2B SaaS: writing product-aware copy for
problem-aware traffic, or problem-aware copy for product-aware traffic.
Diagnose the stage of the traffic source BEFORE deciding the page
structure.

## PRINCIPLE 9 — CLICK TRIGGERS EARN THE CLICK

Click triggers are the small bits of copy around the button that resolve
the last objection before the click.

Examples:
- Under the button: "No credit card required."
- Under the button: "Cancel anytime in one click."
- Under the button: "Used by 5,432 teams at companies like Stripe and
  Notion."
- Under the button: "14-day free trial. We never charge until day 15."

The click trigger does the work of risk reversal at the point of
maximum hesitation. Most pages forget the click trigger entirely and
leave the visitor staring at a naked button.

Rule: every primary CTA gets a click trigger. Every time.

## PRINCIPLE 10 — DON'T POLISH. SWEEP. (THE 7 SWEEPS DEEP DIVE)

After the draft is done, every conversion copywriter has to edit. The
question is HOW.

Polishing — the agency-copywriter habit — strips specificity, kills
voice, generalizes the page until it sounds like every other landing
page. Polishing is what happens when an editor reads the page once and
"cleans it up." Polishing is enemy.

Sweeping — Wiebe's method — runs seven separate passes, each focused on
one job. Sweeping preserves the soul and tightens the prose.

### The 7 Sweeps

**Sweep 1 — Clarity sweep**
Read the page like you've never seen it. Mark every phrase that could
be ambiguous, every jargon term that needs a definition, every sentence
that requires two reads. Rewrite for first-read clarity.

**Sweep 2 — Voice & tone sweep**
Does this sound like a real human, on-brand? Or does it sound like the
generic SaaS landing-page voice? Read it out loud. Where you stumble or
sound robotic, rewrite. Cadence matters.

**Sweep 3 — "So what?" sweep**
Read every line and ask "so what?" If you can't answer, the line is
filler. Cut. Wiebe's rule: every line earns its place or dies. Most
landing pages can lose 30-40% of their word count to this sweep alone.

**Sweep 4 — "Prove it" sweep**
Every claim needs evidence. Walk every claim through the page. Is the
proof there — a testimonial, a stat, a logo, a number, a demo? If not,
either add the proof or kill the claim. Naked claims read as brags and
hurt conversion.

**Sweep 5 — Specificity sweep**
Replace generic words with specific ones. "Many" → "8,000+." "Save
time" → "save 4 hours a week." "Customers love it" → "4.8 stars on G2
from 1,200 reviews." Specifics build credibility; generics destroy it.

**Sweep 6 — Friction sweep**
Cut friction words. Overlong sentences. Hedges ("might," "perhaps,"
"could possibly"). Passive voice. Run-on sentences. Anything that
slows the reader's eye. Read each paragraph and ask: can this be 30%
shorter?

**Sweep 7 — Heart sweep**
The first six sweeps make the page tight and credible. They also can
strip the soul. The final sweep restores it — read the page as a human
reader would, feel where it falls flat, and put back the human moment
(the unexpected word, the parenthetical, the laugh, the empathy line).

Edit in sweeps. Each pass does one job. Don't try to do all seven at
once — you'll do none of them well.

## PRINCIPLE 11 — THE TUTORIAL TUESDAYS VOICE

Wiebe's public teaching voice — Copyhackers blog, Tutorial Tuesdays,
podcast appearances — is a specific dialect worth modeling:

- **Warm, not corporate.** Uses contractions. Uses "yeah," "ok,"
  "let's," "alright."
- **Specific, not vague.** Names brands. Names exact pages. Names
  exact words she changed and the conversion lift.
- **Self-deprecating without false modesty.** "I screwed this up the
  first time…" "I used to think X, then I tested it…"
- **Confidently contrarian.** Calls out bad practice by name. Pushes
  back on agency convention. Doesn't hedge.
- **Reader-first.** Frames every principle in terms of what the
  reader (or the customer of the reader) gets out of it. Never in
  terms of what the copywriter gets out of it.
- **Generous with the playbook.** Gives away the technique. The Amazon
  review mining technique is in a free blog post from 2014, freely
  available.

If you're channeling Wiebe and the voice sounds like a corporate slide
deck or a LinkedIn motivational post, you're doing it wrong. Read more
Copyhackers blog posts and watch more Tutorial Tuesdays until the
cadence sticks.

## PRINCIPLE 12 — HEADLINES THAT GET CLICKED (Wiebe's headline approach)

Headlines are the single highest-leverage piece of copy on the page or
the email. Wiebe's standing rules for headlines:

1. **Start from VoC.** The headline is almost always a verbatim or
   near-verbatim from a repeated customer quote.
2. **Lead with the value, not the brand.** "Cut your monthly close from
   14 days to 4" beats "Acme Software for Finance Teams."
3. **Stress-test with the 4 U's** (Masterson — useful / urgent /
   unique / ultra-specific). Aim for 3 of 4 minimum.
4. **Write 20 variations.** The first 5 are obvious. The next 10 are
   throwaway. Variations 16-20 are usually the keepers.
5. **Read it as the One Reader would.** Would they stop scrolling? If
   not, rewrite.
6. **Test the top 2-3 against control.** Don't pick by gut. Let the
   conversion rate pick.

The cardinal sin: clever-but-vague headlines. ("Marketing, simplified.")
Clever doesn't convert. Specific converts.

## SUMMARY — THE STANDING DOCTRINE

If you only remember 10 lines from this canon:

1. Research is the work. Writing is the visible part.
2. Don't write copy. Find it.
3. Kill the pixie dust.
4. Write like a human, convert like a badass.
5. The page qualifies, not just converts.
6. The hero does 80% of the conversion work.
7. Evidence beats adjectives.
8. Match the message to the awareness stage.
9. Don't polish. Sweep.
10. The customer already wrote your headline.
