**Authors:** A.G. Lafley and Roger L. Martin
**Publisher:** Harvard Business Review Press, 2013

**Secondary source:** A New Way to Think — Your Guide to Superior
Management Effectiveness
**Author:** Roger L. Martin
**Publisher:** Harvard Business Review Press, 2022

## Playing to Win — Chapter map (the operator's view)

The book is organised around **the five choices** of the Strategic
Choice Cascade. Each chapter takes one question of the cascade,
explains the underlying logic, and illustrates with P&G cases (Olay,
Tide, Pampers, Bounty, the Gillette acquisition).

| Ch | Title (short) | Cascade layer introduced |
|----|---------------|--------------------------|
| 1 | Strategy is choice | The cascade as a whole; choice vs planning |
| 2 | What is winning? | Winning Aspiration |
| 3 | Where will you play? | Where to Play (the arena choice) |
| 4 | How will you win? | How to Win (the value proposition choice) |
| 5 | Play to your strengths | Capabilities (the activity system) |
| 6 | Manage what matters | Management Systems |
| 7 | Think it through | Strategy logic flow + reverse-engineering |
| 8 | Shorten your odds | Possibility-based problem-solving |

## Framework 1 — The Strategic Choice Cascade (Ch 1, master frame)

**What it is:** five interlocking choices that together constitute a
strategy. Each choice constrains the next. None can be skipped. Partial
cascades create the illusion of strategy without the substance.

```
Winning Aspiration       (Q1 — what does winning look like?)
        ↓
Where to Play            (Q2 — which arenas?)
        ↓
How to Win               (Q3 — what's the unique value prop?)
        ↓
Capabilities             (Q4 — what must be uniquely true of us?)
        ↓
Management Systems       (Q5 — what reinforces the choices?)
```

**Integrity rules:**
1. Each layer must be a **choice among real alternatives**, not a
   description.
2. Each layer must **constrain** the layer below — i.e. the answer to
   Q2 must rule out Q3 options.
3. Each layer must be **consistent** with the layer above.
4. Two-way arrows in practice — sometimes you discover the Capabilities
   answer that forces you to refine Where to Play.

**Failure mode:** filling in the cascade with descriptions ("we play in
North America" — not a choice, that's where you happen to be) rather
than choices ("we choose North America and we explicitly exclude EMEA
for the next 3 years because…").

## Framework 2 — Winning Aspiration (Ch 2)

**What it is:** the answer to "what does winning mean for us, for this
business, against this competitive set?"

**Distinction Martin draws:**
- A **vision statement** = how the company sees itself in the future.
  Often vague. Not testable.
- A **mission statement** = why the company exists. Often unchanging.
- A **winning aspiration** = a specific, customer-facing definition of
  what victory looks like. Testable. Time-bound enough to know if
  you've won.

**Test it must pass:** can a stranger read this and tell who you serve,
what need you meet better than anyone, and what victory looks like?

**P&G's example (in own words):** to improve the everyday lives of more
consumers in more parts of the world more completely. Each phrase is a
choice — more consumers (scale), more parts of the world (geographic
expansion), more completely (deeper relationship per consumer, not
just transactional).

**Failure modes:**
- "Be the best" — best at what, for whom, measured how?
- "Be the leader" — leader by which metric?
- "Customer-first" — every company says this; it is therefore not a
  choice.

## Framework 3 — Where to Play (Ch 3)

**What it is:** the arena choice. The set of competitive fields you
will play in, and — equally important — the ones you won't.

**Five dimensions of the arena:**
1. **Geography** — countries, regions, cities
2. **Customer segment** — which buyers explicitly, named
3. **Product/service category** — which offerings
4. **Channel** — how you reach the buyer (direct, distributor, retail,
   online, partner)
5. **Vertical stage** — where in the value chain you operate (raw,
   manufacturing, brand, distribution, retail, service)

**The most-under-practised discipline: declaring what's out.** A
Where-to-Play statement that doesn't exclude is not a Where-to-Play
statement. It's a description.

**Integrity check:** are there customer segments you would politely
turn down today? If no, you haven't chosen.

**Common failure modes:**
- "Everyone who needs X" — not a choice
- "All of North America" — geography without segment is half a choice
- "B2B" — too broad to constrain anything downstream

## Framework 4 — How to Win (Ch 4)

**What it is:** the unique value proposition inside your chosen arena.
The reason your chosen customer chooses you over the next-best
alternative.

**Two generic logics (Porter, restated by Martin):**
- **Cost leadership** — win because you can profitably sell at a price
  competitors can't match. Requires scale economics and operational
  discipline.
- **Differentiation** — win because your offer is uniquely valuable to
  the chosen customer such that they will pay a premium. Requires
  brand, design, expertise, or proprietary access.

**Porter's warning, repeated by Martin:** "stuck in the middle" — trying
to do both — is the most common, and most expensive, failure mode.
Pick one knowing the trade-offs.

**Test it must pass:** if you replaced your company name with a
competitor's, would the How-to-Win still be true? If yes, it is not a
How-to-Win. It is a category description.

**Failure modes:**
- "Better service" — every competitor claims this
- "Higher quality" — relative to what, measured how?
- A list of features rather than a value proposition
- "We try harder" — sentiment, not strategy

## Framework 5 — Capabilities (Ch 5)

**What it is:** the **activity system** that must be true about your
organisation — uniquely — to deliver How-to-Win in Where-to-Play.

**Critical insight:** capabilities are **interlocking**, not standalone.
P&G's capability system (in own words) is consumer understanding + brand
building + innovation + go-to-market reach + global scale. No single
capability is the moat. The **system** is the moat, because the
interlocks are hard to copy.

**The activity-system map:** list the 4-6 capabilities, draw the
dependencies between them, and check which ones reinforce each other
(making them harder to copy in isolation).

**Test it must pass:** could a competent competitor build these
capabilities in 18 months? If yes, they are table stakes, not
capabilities. Real capabilities take years to build and have
interlocking dependencies.

**Failure modes:**
- "Great people" — everyone says this
- "Strong culture" — likewise
- Capabilities listed in isolation, not as a system
- Capabilities that don't actually map to How-to-Win

## Framework 6 — Management Systems (Ch 6)

**What it is:** the processes, measures, and incentives that make the
strategic choices stick and survive the daily operating pressure.

**The most-skipped layer.** Operators love Q1-Q4 because they're
interesting. Q5 is operational, dull, and therefore deferred. Result:
the cascade decays within 90 days.

**Three categories of management system:**
1. **Strategic review systems** — which meetings, which cadence, which
   evidence — keep the choices visible
2. **Measurement systems** — which metrics map onto Where-to-Play and
   How-to-Win — what gets measured aligns behaviour
3. **Incentive systems** — pay, recognition, promotion — reinforce the
   chosen behaviours and starve the unchosen ones

**Test it must pass:** for each strategic choice made in Q1-Q4, which
specific review, metric, or incentive defends it daily? If none, the
choice will not survive contact with the calendar.

**P&G example (in own words):** quarterly business reviews structured
around the cascade choices, not financial line items. The metric you
defend in the review is the metric that shapes the next quarter's
behaviour.

## Framework 7 — The Strategy Logic Flow (Ch 7)

**What it is:** a four-step analytical sequence Martin uses to test
whether a cascade is internally coherent.

1. **Industry analysis** — what makes money in this industry, for whom,
   under what conditions?
2. **Customer value analysis** — what does the chosen customer value,
   and where are they underserved today?
3. **Relative position analysis** — where are we vs competitors on the
   dimensions the customer values most?
4. **Competitive reaction analysis** — what will competitors do in
   response to our strategy, and does our strategy still work after
   they react?

**Reverse-engineering use:** take a competitor whose results you
admire. Walk this flow in reverse — infer their cascade from their
visible behaviour. Often more useful than analysing your own,
because you can see them clearly.

## Framework 8 — Possibility-Based Problem-Solving / "What Would Have
to Be True?" (Ch 8, and central to A New Way to Think)

**What it is:** Martin's signature reframe for any strategic decision
involving uncertainty between two or more paths.

**Standard question (the trap):** "Which option is right?" Invites
debate, defence, advocacy, and political coalition-forming. Slow,
expensive, often wrong.

**Martin's question:** "What would have to be true for each option to
be the best one?"

**Mechanism:**
1. List the strategic options. A, B, C.
2. For each, list the 4-6 conditions that would have to hold for it to
   be the best choice.
3. For each condition, mark it: known-true / known-false /
   uncertain-needs-testing.
4. Find the **single most-uncertain condition** that is also the
   **cheapest to test.**
5. Run that test. Update the cascade with what you learn.

**Why it works:** turns advocacy ("I believe in Option B") into inquiry
("here is what would have to be true; let's go check"). Defuses
political defence of pet options. Surfaces the real bet under each
option.

**The pre-mortem variant:** for the chosen option, ask "assume this
fails — what was the condition that did not hold?" The condition you
name is the one to monitor.

## A New Way to Think — additional frameworks (2022)

A New Way to Think is structured as 14 short chapters, each a critique
of a dominant management orthodoxy and a proposed replacement model.
The chapters most relevant to operator coaching:

### Framework 9 — Strategy ≠ Planning (Ch 1)

**The orthodoxy Martin attacks:** that strategy and planning are the
same thing, or that strategy is a sub-component of the annual planning
process.

**Martin's distinction:**

| Planning | Strategy |
|----------|----------|
| Comfortable | Uncomfortable |
| Activity-based | Choice-based |
| Adds (more more more) | Sacrifices (this not that) |
| Internally focused | Externally focused — customer chooses |
| Quantitative ledger | Qualitative bet on causation |
| Can be a spreadsheet | Cannot be a spreadsheet |
| Continuous improvement | Discontinuous change |

**The diagnostic question:** if the document feels safe to share with
the board, it's a plan. Strategy makes someone uncomfortable because
you might be wrong — and that's the point.

### Framework 10 — Competition ≠ Operational Effectiveness (Ch 2)

**Martin restating Porter:** being better at the same game as your
competitors is operational effectiveness. Choosing a **different game**
is strategy. Operational effectiveness eventually converges — everyone
adopts the best practice — and the only winner is the customer (lower
prices, no profit pool).

**Strategy starts with the observation:** the same game cannot be won
by being slightly better. The same game must be replaced with a
different game in which you can choose to play.

**Test:** if your description of "strategy" reads like "we will be the
best at [common industry activity]", you have an operational-
effectiveness initiative, not a strategy.

### Framework 11 — Customers ≠ Shareholders (Ch 3)

**Martin's argument (from Fixing the Game, restated):** companies that
explicitly prioritise customers outperform companies that explicitly
prioritise shareholders, on shareholder returns. Because customer
value drives revenue; revenue drives profit; profit drives share
price. Reverse the chain and you optimise for a side effect, not the
cause.

**Operational rule:** if a decision is being framed as "what will
investors think?" reframe it as "what does this do for the chosen
customer in the chosen arena?" The customer answer leads; the
investor answer follows.

### Framework 12 — Integrative Thinking / The Opposable Mind

**Source:** The Opposable Mind (2007), revisited in A New Way to Think.

**What it is:** the leader's capacity to hold two opposing models in
mind simultaneously, without lurching to one OR the other, until a
**third option** emerges that incorporates the salient features of
both.

**The four steps:**
1. **Salience** — pay attention to more features of the problem than
   the dominant logic admits. Most binary traps are caused by ignoring
   features that don't fit the model.
2. **Causality** — consider multi-directional and non-linear causal
   relationships, not just simple A-causes-B.
3. **Architecture** — hold the whole problem in view, not just the
   pieces, when designing the decision.
4. **Resolution** — refuse the binary; design a third option that
   captures the salient features of both opposing models.

**Operator cue:** when the team is split between A and B with strong
emotional advocates on each side, the integrative-thinking move is to
ask **"what would a Solution C look like that incorporated the strongest
points of both A and B?"**
