# The Value Builder System — The 8 Drivers in Depth

**Source:** John Warrillow's Value Builder System™ (valuebuilder.com), the synthesizing framework across *Built to Sell* (2011), *The Automatic Customer* (2015), and *The Art of Selling Your Business* (2021). The Value Builder questionnaire scores a business on these 8 drivers and produces a composite Value Builder Score (0-100). Warrillow's data claim: a score of 80+ produces offers ~71% above market for a business of comparable size in the same industry.

**Source treatment:** drivers described in own words for synthetic-coach use. The questionnaire is not reproduced. For the underlying questions and the official scoring methodology, go to valuebuilder.com.

---

## How the 8 drivers compound

Each driver is independent — strong scores on one don't compensate for weak scores on another. Buyers underwrite each dimension separately during diligence. A business strong on 7 drivers but weak on Switzerland Structure (single-customer concentration) gets discounted as if it were weak on the whole. The drivers compound multiplicatively, not additively.

The compounding works in both directions. A business that lifts all 8 drivers from "below average" to "above average" doesn't get 8 separate small premiums — it gets a step-change in multiple because each lift removes a discount the buyer was applying.

## Driver 1 — Financial Performance

**Definition:** Cleanliness, consistency, and trajectory of the financial record. Quality of books, repeatability of profit, absence of one-time anomalies.

**What raises it:**
- Review-grade or audit-grade financials (vs compilation-only).
- Three consecutive years of growing revenue AND growing margin.
- Quarterly profit consistency without big swings.
- Clean separation between business and personal expenses.
- Accrual-basis accounting (not cash-basis).
- Recognized accounting firm or fractional CFO involvement.

**What tanks it:**
- Cash-basis books in a business big enough for accrual.
- Owner add-backs above ~15% of EBITDA — the buyer's QoE will reverse them.
- Aggressive revenue recognition (booking vs delivery).
- Inconsistent month-to-month gross margin.
- Side businesses running through the same books.
- One-time gains or losses inflating trailing-12 EBITDA.

**How it shows up in due diligence:** buyer commissions a "Quality of Earnings" (QoE) report. QoE strips one-time items, normalizes owner compensation to market rate, produces "adjusted EBITDA." If reported EBITDA differs materially from QoE-adjusted, buyer reduces offer. Typical adjustment: 5-20% of reported EBITDA, compounded by the multiple (15% adjustment at 5x = 75% of one EBITDA turn off the deal).

## Driver 2 — Growth Potential

**Definition:** The buyer's view of how much more business they can extract from the asset after closing. Not your historical growth — their forward growth.

**What raises it:**
- Documented TAM with credible market-share math.
- Underpenetrated geography or customer segment with a plan to enter it.
- Adjacent product line that the existing customer base would buy.
- Demonstrated repeatable acquisition channel that has more capacity than current spend.
- Existing customers asking for more (proof of expansion runway).
- Operating leverage — proof that next $1M of revenue costs less to deliver than current revenue.

**What tanks it:**
- Saturated local market with no expansion plan.
- One geography, one customer type, one product.
- Founder is the salesperson — no transferable acquisition system.
- Declining or flat trailing growth.
- Industry headwinds (regulatory pressure, secular decline, technology displacement).
- Capacity constraint that requires capex to break.

**How it shows up in due diligence:** buyers ask for the three-year plan and stress-test it. They probe acquisition channel economics, customer concentration in growth, and the dependency of the plan on the seller staying involved. If the growth plan only works with the seller running it, the buyer either discounts the plan or demands an earnout tied to its execution.

## Driver 3 — Switzerland Structure

**Definition:** Independence from any one party. Named "Switzerland" because Switzerland's value comes from being neutral — not aligned with any single dominant interest. A business with concentration on customers, employees, or suppliers is not Switzerland.

**The 15% rule:** no single customer above 15% of revenue, no single employee above 15% of capability, no single supplier or platform above 15% of capacity.

**What raises it:**
- Top 10 customers under 50% of revenue (and top 1 under 15%).
- Multiple key employees who can each operate independently — no single point of failure.
- Diverse acquisition channels (no platform above 30% of new customer flow).
- Multiple suppliers for any critical input.
- Geographic and industry diversification in the customer base.

**What tanks it:**
- One customer above 20% of revenue (especially above 30%).
- One employee who, if they left, would take a chunk of revenue with them.
- One ad platform driving 50%+ of new business.
- One technology vendor with no substitute (proprietary integration that can't be moved).
- One referral source providing the majority of new business.

**How it shows up in due diligence:** buyers ask for customer-revenue history by name and for employee tenure / role / criticality maps. Concentration triggers either price reductions or aggressive earnout structures contingent on retention of the concentrated relationship. A 40%-concentration customer can take 30-50% off the offer.

## Driver 4 — Valuation Teeter-Totter

**Definition:** The cash-flow timing balance between you and your customer. Picture a seesaw — money owed to you sits on the buyer's end; money you owe sits on yours. Tilt determines value.

**What raises it:**
- Deposits or prepayments before delivery.
- Annual prepay billing (with a discount) instead of monthly.
- Credit-card-on-file auto-billing (eliminates collections cycle).
- Net-7 or net-15 payment terms (versus net-30 or net-60).
- Low or no inventory.
- Negative working capital cycle (paid before you pay your suppliers).
- Vendor terms longer than customer terms (banked cash spread).

**What tanks it:**
- Net-60 or net-90 customer terms.
- Large work-in-progress balances (delivered, unbilled, uncollected).
- High inventory days.
- Milestone billing where the milestones extend months out.
- Vendor terms shorter than customer terms (you pay before you collect).
- Aging receivables over 90 days exceeding 10% of total AR.

**How it shows up in due diligence:** buyers calculate the "working capital required" to run the business and either deduct it from the offer or peg it on the balance sheet at close. A business with tight cash flow timing requires less working capital, so more of the purchase price goes to the seller. Teeter-totter strength also lifts EBITDA quality — pre-paid revenue is more defensible than booked-but-uncollected revenue.

## Driver 5 — Recurring Revenue

**Definition:** The percentage and tier of revenue that arrives predictably without re-selling. See the Hierarchy of Recurring Revenue in `automatic_customer_frameworks.md` for the six-tier model.

**What raises it:**
- High % of total revenue from recurring contracts (target: 50%+ for a meaningful lift; 75%+ for a step-change).
- Long contract terms (multi-year > annual > monthly).
- Auto-renewal language with opt-out structure (versus opt-in renewal).
- Switching costs embedded in the product (integration, data, training).
- Low and stable churn (target: <10% annual gross churn).
- Net negative churn — expansion revenue from existing customers exceeds churn losses.

**What tanks it:**
- Project-based revenue with no recurring follow-on.
- Month-to-month with no contractual commitment.
- High churn (>20% annual) masked by aggressive new acquisition.
- "Recurring" labelled but actually re-quoted each period.
- Customer concentration inside the recurring base (one big subscriber walking out kills the multiple).

**How it shows up in due diligence:** buyers segment revenue into "recurring," "repeat-but-not-contracted," and "one-time." Each bucket is valued at a different multiple. Recurring contracted revenue can get 3-8x ARR. Repeat-but-not-contracted gets services-multiple treatment of trailing EBITDA. One-time gets discounted heavily. Knowing the bucket mix tells you the real exit math.

## Driver 6 — Monopoly Control

**Definition:** Structural defensibility. What stops a competitor from doing what you do for less. Not "we're better" — that's undefended. The question is: what's the moat?

**What raises it:**
- Proprietary methodology documented as IP (trademarked process names, copyrighted training materials).
- Patents on core technology.
- Brand authority — recognized name in the niche, earned media presence, founder as visible expert.
- Exclusive partnerships, suppliers, or distribution relationships.
- Network effects — value to each customer increases as more customers join.
- Switching costs — data lock-in, integration depth, contractual exit penalties.
- Geographic exclusivity or franchise-protected territory.
- High customer NPS (>50) signaling preference, not just acceptance.

**What tanks it:**
- Competing on price as a primary differentiator.
- "Better service" as the only differentiator — undefended.
- Reliance on the founder's personal reputation that doesn't transfer.
- Easy-to-copy product with no underlying IP.
- Long-tail customer base with no switching cost.
- Industry where pricing is set externally (commodity markets).

**How it shows up in due diligence:** buyers ask "what stops three of your top employees from leaving and starting a competitor?" — and they ask it of the employees, the customers, and you. The defensibility narrative has to hold up under cross-examination. Weak defensibility caps the multiple at industry median or below.

## Driver 7 — Customer Satisfaction

**Definition:** Repeat-purchase rate, retention, and quantified satisfaction (typically NPS or equivalent). The metric that tells the buyer whether the customer base they're acquiring will actually stay after the sale.

**What raises it:**
- Quarterly NPS measurement with documented trend (target: NPS >50).
- Repeat-purchase rate (target: >70% of customers buy again within 12 months for transactional businesses).
- Annual retention rate >90% for subscription businesses.
- Documented case studies and testimonials from named customers.
- Customer advisory board or formal feedback loop.
- Referral-driven new business (>20% of new customers from referrals).
- Low complaint volume, fast resolution, documented service-level metrics.

**What tanks it:**
- No measurement of customer satisfaction at all.
- High churn the founder explains away in narrative ("they were a bad-fit customer").
- Negative online reviews — Google, Yelp, BBB — that go unanswered.
- Customer concentration inside happy customers (top NPS is from your top 3 customers, not the base).
- Repeat-purchase rate below industry norm.
- Customer support delegated entirely to the lowest-cost option, no founder oversight.

**How it shows up in due diligence:** buyers conduct customer reference calls — they pick the customers (often the smaller ones, deliberately), and they ask whether the customer would buy again from the new owner. A bad reference call cycle can collapse a deal in week 4 of diligence. Strong satisfaction backed by data is the seller's best diligence weapon.

## Driver 8 — Hub & Spoke (Owner Independence)

**Definition:** How much the business depends on the founder. Founder is the hub; every operational decision is a spoke radiating from them. Cut the hub, the wheel collapses.

**What raises it:**
- Documented SOPs covering 80%+ of operational decisions.
- Trained #2 with explicit decision authority.
- Functional leads (ops, finance, sales, delivery) running their domains without founder approval on routine decisions.
- Founder absent from day-to-day customer relationships.
- Regular >2-week consecutive vacations without operational impact.
- Decisions made the same way whether founder is present or not.
- Pass the 90-day-offline test.

**What tanks it:**
- Founder approves every meaningful decision.
- Customers ask for the founder specifically.
- Sales is the founder's relationship business.
- Founder is the operational firefighter.
- No documented SOPs — processes live in the founder's head.
- Founder can't take a real vacation without being reachable.

**How it shows up in due diligence:** buyers ask "what happens if John gets hit by a bus tomorrow?" They probe actual vs nominal management authority. They model post-close transition risk. High owner-dependence forces one of three outcomes: massive earnout tying seller to a 2-5 year transition; price discount for transition risk; or deal collapses because the business is unsaleable — a job dressed as a company.

A founder who fails the 90-day-offline test is typically capped at 1-3x discretionary earnings (lifestyle-business multiples) instead of 4-8x EBITDA. The gap between those bands is often the entire reason to sell.

## How the composite score works

Each driver scores 0-10 (or equivalent). Sum across the 8 drivers, normalize to 100. Above 80 = top quartile, statistically associated with premium offers per Warrillow's data. Below 50 = currently unsaleable at a meaningful multiple — fix before going to market.

The score isn't a target on its own. It's a diagnostic. The point isn't to score 80 — the point is to identify which 2-3 drivers are dragging the multiple and fix them.

## How to use this file inside the /warrillow skill

1. When Ivan describes the business, run a quick informal score across the 8 drivers. Identify the 2 weakest.
2. Fix order is usually: Switzerland (Driver 3) > Hub & Spoke (Driver 8) > Recurring Revenue (Driver 5). These three move the multiple the most.
3. Financial Performance (Driver 1) is the gate — without clean books, no other driver score matters because the buyer can't trust any of it.
4. Point Ivan to valuebuilder.com for the official questionnaire and to the books for the full prose treatment.
