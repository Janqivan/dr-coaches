# Conversion-Centered Design — The 7 Principles, Deep

This is the operating system. Conversion-Centered Design (CCD) is the
discipline of designing campaign landing pages so the visitor does the
one thing the campaign exists to make them do. Not three things.
One thing.

Oli's original public framing (in the free ebook on unbounce.com) names
the seven as: **Focus, Structure, Consistency, Benefits, Attention,
Trust, Friction**. The synthetic coach uses an expanded operational
naming — **Attention, Context, Clarity, Congruence, Credibility,
Closing, Continuance** — because each name maps to a question you can
audit a page against. Either naming is fine. Same skeleton. Pick one
and stay consistent.

The principles are not a checklist you walk in any order. They stack.
You cannot earn Clarity if you have not earned Attention. You cannot
earn Closing if you have not earned Credibility. Skip a layer and the
ones above it leak.

---

## Principle 1 — Attention (Focus)

**Definition.** The page exists to focus the visitor on a single
conversion goal. Every pixel either serves that goal or it leaks
attention away from it.

**The operating rule.** Attention Ratio = total clickable elements on
the page divided by the number of campaign conversion goals on the
page. A real campaign landing page is **1:1**. If the page has a top
nav with 7 links, a footer with 12 more, three social icons, a chat
widget, two secondary CTAs, and one primary CTA — that's an attention
ratio of 25:1. You are paying for traffic and then offering it 24
escape routes.

**How to apply.**
- Decide the one conversion goal before you open the design file.
- Strip the global nav. Landing pages do not need it. The visitor did
  not arrive to browse. The visitor arrived from a specific source
  with a specific expectation.
- Strip the global footer. Replace it with a minimal footer:
  privacy/terms link, copyright, and nothing clickable that distracts.
- Kill the chat widget unless chat is the conversion goal.
- Kill social icons. Instagram and LinkedIn are not conversion goals.
- If a secondary CTA must exist, make it serve the same goal at a
  different commitment level (e.g., primary = "Book the consult",
  secondary = "Download the eligibility PDF first"). The secondary
  is still the same campaign.

**Typical mistake.** Treating the homepage as a landing page. The
homepage is many-to-many: many audiences, many goals, many links.
A landing page is one-to-one: one source, one audience, one goal.
Bolting a form onto the homepage and calling it a landing page is the
single most common Oli rant trigger.

**Audit question.** "If I deleted every clickable element on this page
except the one primary CTA, would the page still make sense?" If yes,
delete them.

**Example.**
- Bad: "EB-5 Visa Services" on a law firm page with full site nav,
  "About / Our Team / Other Visa Types / Blog / Contact" in the
  header, and a form labelled "Get in Touch" in the corner. AR ≈ 30:1.
- Good: A dedicated EB-5 page with no nav, headline "Get Your EB-5
  Eligibility Assessment in 48 Hours", one primary CTA "Start My
  Assessment", proof block, FAQ, footer with privacy only. AR = 1:1.

## Principle 2 — Context (Structure)

**Definition.** The page is structured so the visitor's eye follows
the argument in the order the argument needs to land. Hero → promise
→ proof → mechanism → offer → CTA. Not random. Not pretty-but-flat.
A persuasion sequence rendered as a page.

**How to apply.**
- **Hero**: headline + subhead + primary CTA + hero visual. The
  promise lands in the first 2 seconds.
- **Proof immediately under hero**: 3–6 logos, a star rating, or a
  one-sentence testimonial. Buys you the right to keep reading.
- **The "why this matters" block**: 2–4 benefit bullets, customer
  language, not feature language.
- **Mechanism / how it works**: 3 steps, max. People skip more than
  three.
- **Deeper proof**: case study, testimonial with face and result.
- **Objection handlers / FAQ**: written as the actual objections, not
  marketing-flavoured FAQs.
- **Final CTA**: identical to the hero CTA. Same words, same colour,
  same promise.

**Encapsulation.** Group related elements visually so the eye knows
what belongs to what. A testimonial floating in white space attached
to nothing has no power. The same testimonial inside a card next to
the relevant benefit doubles its power.

**Directional cues.** Arrows, lines, and especially eye-gaze in hero
images. If the hero photo features a person, their eyes should look
toward the headline or the CTA, not at the viewer and not out of the
page. This is from the Attention-Driven Design ebook.

**Typical mistake.** "Beautiful" pages with no narrative. Three
identical 3-column blocks of icons-and-text in a row, no hierarchy,
no argument. The page looks like a brochure and converts like a
brochure.

**Audit question.** "Read the page aloud as a sales pitch. Does it
make a coherent case in the order it appears?" If you have to jump
around, the structure is wrong.

## Principle 3 — Clarity

**Definition.** A visitor must understand what's being offered, who
it's for, and what to do next, within 5 seconds. If they can't, they
leave. The CRO industry has a term for the test: the **five-second
test**. Show the page for five seconds, take it away, ask the user
"what is this page selling and to whom?" If they can't answer, you
have a clarity problem, not a traffic problem.

**The headline/subhead flip.** Oli's standard fix. Take the headline
and the subhead. Swap them. Five-second test both versions. Keep the
one that wins. The reason this works is that designers default to
clever for the headline and descriptive for the subhead — and clever
loses to descriptive when the audience is cold. There is a public Oli
example where this flip moved correct comprehension on a five-second
test from 6% to 20% — same words, different order.

**Customer language, not brand language.** The headline is not the
place for your company tagline. It's the place for the customer's
own words about the outcome they want. If your customers say "get my
green card", the headline says "get your green card", not "navigate
the immigration process".

**Specificity beats cleverness.** "Save time and money" is not a
headline. "Cut your franchise discovery process from 9 months to 90
days" is a headline. Numbers, timeframes, named outcomes.

**Typical mistake.** Vague hero headlines that could belong on any
competitor's page. "Welcome to Smith Law", "Your Partner in Growth",
"The Future of [Industry]". Generic. Delete them.

**Audit question.** "If I covered the logo, would the headline tell
me which company this is, which audience it's for, and which outcome
they get?" If not, rewrite.

## Principle 4 — Congruence (Consistency)

**Definition.** Every element on the page — headline, subhead, hero
image, CTA copy, form label, button colour, footer microcopy — points
at the same conversion. No element is fighting another element. No
element is selling a different product.

**Inside-the-page congruence.**
- The headline names the outcome. The subhead justifies the outcome.
  The CTA completes the sentence "I want to ___ [outcome]".
- The hero image shows the outcome, the audience, or the mechanism.
  Not a stock photo of a handshake.
- The button copy matches the offer. "Start My Eligibility Assessment"
  matches "Get Your EB-5 Eligibility Assessment". "Submit" matches
  nothing.

**Source-to-page congruence (this is Message Match, see
`attention_ratio_and_message_match.md`).** The ad's promise, the ad's
words, and ideally the ad's visual flow into the page's hero. The
visitor should not have to re-orient when they land.

**Typical mistake.** A page that sells one thing in the headline,
proves a different thing in the testimonials ("they helped me with X"
when the page is about Y), and asks for the visitor's life story in
the form. The visitor's brain hits friction and bounces.

**Audit question.** "Does every element on this page support the one
goal? If I removed an element, would the page get worse?" If removing
the element makes the page tighter, the element was incongruent.

## Principle 5 — Credibility (Trust, Benefits, Design for Trust)

**Definition.** A cold visitor does not believe you. They are
correct not to. Credibility is the work of making the visitor's belief
in your promise rise faster than their natural skepticism.

**The credibility stack.**
- **Specific results, not generic results.** "We helped 47 EB-5
  applicants approve in 2024" beats "We've helped thousands of
  clients".
- **Named testimonials with faces.** A photo, a name, a role, a
  result. Anonymous testimonials are a tell.
- **Logos** of recognisable clients, publications, regulators, or
  partners. Logo rows immediately under the hero are standard for a
  reason.
- **Counter-objection proof.** If the objection is "this is too
  expensive", the proof block addresses ROI. If the objection is "this
  takes too long", the proof block addresses timeline.
- **Risk reversal.** Guarantee, refund policy, no-pay-if-no-result.
  Whatever is real for your offer. Put it next to the CTA.

**Visual trust signals.** Real photos of real people from your team.
Real customer photos. Real screenshots. Stock photography of smiling
diverse teams in glass-walled offices kills more conversions than it
helps because every visitor has been trained to see through it.

**Typical mistake.** Burying proof at the bottom of the page where
the visitor never scrolls, or building proof out of vague
superlatives ("trusted by industry leaders") with no actual logos
or names.

**Audit question.** "If the visitor's most cynical friend was looking
over their shoulder, which line on this page would the friend laugh
at?" Replace that line with a specific verifiable proof.

## Principle 6 — Closing (CTA, Reduce Friction)

**Definition.** The conversion event. The button, the form, the
microcopy around them. This is where pages with great everything else
still die.

**CTA copy rule.** The button completes the sentence "I want to ___".
- "I want to start my EB-5 assessment" → button reads
  "Start My EB-5 Assessment".
- "I want to download the franchise checklist" → button reads
  "Send Me The Checklist".
- "Submit", "Click Here", "Continue", "Get Started" — first-person
  outcome beats generic verb every time.

**Form rule.** The form's length matches the size of the ask. This is
Conversion Coupling — see `attention_ratio_and_message_match.md`.
- High-intent ask (book a consult): more fields are tolerated.
- Low-intent ask (download a PDF): one or two fields max.
- The friction of the form must match the perceived value of what's
  on the other side of it.

**Friction audit.**
- Every field must justify its presence. "Phone number" on a PDF
  download is friction with no payoff.
- "Company website" before "company name" is mental friction —
  visitors think in the order they speak.
- Dropdowns with 50 options are friction. Pre-populate, default-select,
  or remove the field.
- Two-column forms feel longer than one-column forms because the eye
  has to track sideways. One column unless you have a hard reason.
- Inline validation beats post-submit error pages. Always.

**Microcopy near the CTA.**
- "Takes 90 seconds" near a form converts. "We respect your privacy"
  near a form converts. "Required" labels in red do not.
- Trust microcopy below the button outperforms long privacy
  disclaimers.

**Typical mistake.** A page with one billion well-designed sections
above the fold, and a generic "Contact Us" form at the bottom labelled
"Submit". The conversion is the page. Treat it like it.

**Audit question.** "If I clicked the button right now, would the
copy on the button accurately describe what happens next?" If no,
rewrite it.

## Principle 7 — Continuance (Post-Conversion)

**Definition.** The campaign does not end when the visitor clicks
submit. The thank-you page, the confirmation email, and the next step
are all still part of the page's job. Most marketers stop designing
the moment the form fires. That's where the leak is.

**The thank-you page.**
- Confirm the conversion clearly. "Your assessment is on its way."
- Give them the next thing to do. Calendar booking, video to watch,
  PDF to read, calculator to use.
- Do not return them to the homepage. The homepage is many-to-many.
  They are still in the campaign. Continue the campaign.

**The confirmation email.**
- Same headline, same promise, same brand voice as the page.
- Sent within seconds. Inbox placement matters.
- Includes the next CTA, not just the PDF or the calendar link.

**The next step.**
- For high-intent conversions (consult booked): pre-meeting video,
  preparation checklist, calendar reminder, no-show prevention email
  sequence.
- For low-intent conversions (PDF downloaded): nurture sequence that
  matches the PDF's promise, not a generic "welcome to our newsletter"
  blast.

**Typical mistake.** Designing the page to a 10/10 and the thank-you
page to a 2/10. A blank "Thanks!" screen with no next step is a
campaign that paid for the lead and then deleted half its value.

**Audit question.** "What does the visitor see in the 5 seconds after
they click the button? Is that a designed experience or an
afterthought?"

## How to Run a CCD Audit (Operational Sequence)

1. Lock the one goal of the page. Write it down in one sentence.
2. Count the attention ratio. Number it.
3. Compare the ad/source to the headline. Message match: yes/no.
4. Five-second test the hero. Headline/subhead flip if needed.
5. Walk the structure. Hero → proof → benefit → mechanism → proof →
   FAQ → CTA. Note missing layers.
6. Test congruence of every element to the goal. Delete or rewrite
   anything fighting the goal.
7. Audit credibility. Specific, named, visual, counter-objection.
8. Audit the close. CTA copy, form length vs ask, friction microcopy.
9. Audit continuance. Thank-you page, email, next step.
10. Re-count attention ratio. It should be 1:1.

If steps 1–3 fail, do not bother with 4–10. The page is fundamentally
mis-conceived and needs to be rebuilt, not optimised.
