## Kennedy's worldview on long-form copywriting

Kennedy treats the sales letter as the operator's single highest-leverage marketing asset. One sales letter, written once, can be the engine of an entire business — running on autopilot, generating leads and sales for years. He has zero patience for the "people don't read long copy" complaint: people don't read what doesn't interest them, and they read every word of what does. Length is not the issue. Relevance is. The 28-step process below is Kennedy's discipline for producing the long-form copy that earns the read.

## Framework 1 — The 28-Step Copywriting Process

**What it is:** Kennedy's full discipline for producing a long-form sales letter. The 28 steps split into three phases: PREP (steps 1-12), WRITE (steps 13-22), REVISE (steps 23-28). The phases are sequential — never skip ahead.

**PHASE A — PREP (steps 1-12). 90% of the work happens before the first sentence.**

1. **Identify the prospect.** Avatar excavation — who specifically are you writing to? One person, named, with a job, age, income, lifestyle.
2. **Catalogue the prospect's hot buttons.** Their fears, frustrations, fantasies, secret desires, prior failed attempts. Capture verbatim language.
3. **Catalogue the prospect's objections.** Every reason they would say no — price, time, trust, skepticism, prior bad experience.
4. **Define the product / offer.** Features, benefits, price, components, terms.
5. **Translate features into benefits.** Every feature gets a "which means…" — what does the avatar actually get?
6. **Identify the unique mechanism.** Why does YOUR offer work when the competitors' don't?
7. **Gather proof.** Testimonials, case studies, third-party verification, demos, photos, before/after, named outcomes.
8. **Sharpen the offer.** Stack bonuses, build the guarantee, define the deadline, finalize the price anchor.
9. **Pick the prospect's dominant emotion.** Fear, greed, vanity, anger, hope, love. The dominant emotion guides voice and lead type.
10. **Decide the "big idea".** The single most powerful, specific, contrarian idea that the letter will hammer.
11. **Pick the lead type** (see Framework 3 below).
12. **Outline the letter.** Headline → lead → body → offer → close → P.S. Block out which proof goes where.

**PHASE B — WRITE (steps 13-22). Now you draft.**

13. **Write the headline.** Get to a candidate that promises the dominant benefit in the avatar's language. Write 20+ options; pick the strongest.
14. **Write the pre-headline / eyebrow.** The agitation or curiosity hook above the headline.
15. **Write the sub-headline.** Amplify or prove the headline.
16. **Write the lead.** Whatever lead type you chose, execute it with maximum energy. The lead earns the next paragraph.
17. **Write the problem agitation.** Restate the avatar's pain in their own language. Make them nod.
18. **Write the solution introduction.** Bridge from the problem to your offer's mechanism.
19. **Write the proof section.** Case studies, data, named results, third-party verification.
20. **Write the offer.** Components, bonuses, guarantee, price justification, price.
21. **Write the close.** Explicit ask. Exact instructions. No ambiguity.
22. **Write the P.S.** Restate the offer, the deadline, and the single biggest objection answered.

**PHASE C — REVISE (steps 23-28). Most copywriters skip phase C. Kennedy says phase C is where the letter is made.**

23. **Read it aloud.** If you stumble, the reader will. Cut or rewrite anything that doesn't flow.
24. **Cut every wasted word.** No paragraph earns its place unless it drives toward the close. Kill adjectives that don't add proof.
25. **Pressure-test the headline.** Would your avatar stop on it? Test alternatives. The headline does 80% of the work.
26. **Pressure-test the close and P.S.** These are the second- and third-most-read sections. Are they doing their job?
27. **Check the formatting.** Subheads, bullets, bolds, indents, Johnson box, hand-written notes in margin (in physical mail), red highlights. Visual interest sustains scanning readers.
28. **Run the 10 Rules check** (from the No B.S. Direct Marketing canon). Offer? Deadline? Tracking? Follow-up? Personality? Urgency? If any rule fails, fix it.

**Operator use:** when an operator says "I wrote a sales letter and it bombed", run the 28 steps. The failure point is almost always in PREP (steps 1-12), not in WRITE.

## Framework 2 — The Headline Writing System

**Kennedy's claim:** the headline does 80% of the work of the ad. A great body with a weak headline gets no read. A great headline with a mediocre body still pulls.

**The headline test (5 questions, all must be YES):**

1. Does it promise a specific benefit?
2. Does it speak to a specific avatar?
3. Does it create curiosity, urgency, or both?
4. Does it use the avatar's own language, not industry jargon?
5. Could the average reader of the right list NOT skip past it?

**Kennedy's headline types (proven openings):**

- **The "How To" headline.** "How to [achieve specific benefit] without [common obstacle]." — works because it promises a path.
- **The "Who Else Wants" headline.** Social proof embedded in the question. Implies others already got the result.
- **The "Secret Of" headline.** Curiosity-driven. Implies insider knowledge.
- **The "Reason Why" headline.** "7 reasons why [specific avatar] should [specific action]." — the listicle predecessor, still works.
- **The "Warning" headline.** Loss-framed. Triggers attention through threat.
- **The "If You Are X, Read This" headline.** Disqualifies the wrong reader and qualifies the right reader in one line.
- **The "Question" headline.** Forces the reader to mentally answer, which is a form of engagement.
- **The "Testimonial" headline.** Uses a customer's exact words, in quotes. High credibility, immediate proof.

**Operator rule:** write 20 headlines. Pick the best. Pressure-test it against the 5 questions. Rewrite until all 5 are YES.

## Framework 3 — Lead Types (the 6 entry points)

**What it is:** the six standard ways to open a long-form sales letter once the headline has done its job. Lead choice depends on (a) what the avatar already knows, (b) the dominant emotion of the offer, and (c) how skeptical the avatar is.

**The six lead types:**

1. **Offer Lead.** Open with the offer itself — strong when the avatar already knows and trusts you. Lowest energy, highest efficiency. Works for warm audiences.
2. **Promise Lead.** Open with the big benefit, then justify it. Strong when the avatar wants the result but is skeptical it's possible.
3. **Problem-Solution Lead.** Open with the avatar's pain, agitate it, then introduce the solution. Strong when the avatar is in active pain.
4. **Big Secret Lead.** Open with a piece of insider information the avatar doesn't have. Strong when the avatar is curious or feels stuck.
5. **Proclamation / Bold Promise Lead.** Open with a contrarian, polarizing statement that contradicts conventional wisdom. Strong when the avatar is tired of the same old advice.
6. **Story Lead.** Open with a narrative — a customer's journey, the operator's origin story, a third-party case. Strong for cold audiences who need to lower their guard before they hear an offer.

**Kennedy's selection rule:**
- Cold audience + skeptical + niche-burned → **Story Lead** or **Big Secret Lead**.
- Warm audience + previously bought → **Offer Lead**.
- Pain-driven avatar (medical, legal, financial distress) → **Problem-Solution Lead**.
- Curious avatar in a stale niche → **Proclamation Lead**.

**Operator use:** match the lead type to the avatar's awareness level and emotional state. Don't lead with the offer to a cold list. Don't lead with a story to a warm list that wants the deal.

## Framework 4 — The "You" Appeal

**Kennedy's principle:** the reader is a self-interested creature. Every sentence must answer "what's in it for me?" — implicitly or explicitly.

**The discipline:**

- Count the "you" references vs. the "we / I / our" references. Healthy ratio: 4-to-1 in favor of "you" or stronger.
- Translate every "we offer" / "our product" sentence into "you get" / "you'll discover" / "you'll experience".
- Every feature sentence ends with the "which means…" translation that converts the feature into a reader benefit.
- Every benefit is tied to the reader's existing desire, not the operator's pride in the product.

**The failure mode:** the operator writes about themselves — their history, their team, their process, their philosophy. The reader doesn't care. The reader cares about themselves. Kennedy's correction: write the letter as if the reader has one question — "what does this do for me?" — and you must answer it in every paragraph.

**Operator note:** the "you" appeal is the cheapest, fastest copy fix. Highlight every "we / our / I" in a draft and convert each one. The letter immediately gets stronger.

## Framework 5 — Objection Handling in Copy

**Kennedy's claim:** the buyer's objections do not disappear when you ignore them. They sit in the back of the reader's head, unrebutted, until the reader decides not to buy. The copy must handle every objection before the close, in the order the reader would raise them.

**The standard objection set:**

1. **"It's too expensive."** Handle with price anchoring (cost of NOT solving the problem, cost of alternatives), bonus stack (the bonuses alone justify the price), and payment terms (monthly, financing, instalments).
2. **"I don't have time."** Handle with effort minimization (the offer does the work for them) and time framing (this saves more time than it costs).
3. **"It won't work for me."** Handle with proof — diverse case studies covering avatar variants. "Here's a result from someone exactly like you."
4. **"I don't trust you / I've been burned before."** Handle with named third-party proof (credible testimonials, media mentions, named clients), the guarantee, and the operator's track record.
5. **"I'm not ready / I'll do it later."** Handle with the deadline and the cost-of-delay argument. Every week they wait, the problem compounds.
6. **"I need to think about it / ask my spouse."** Handle with the risk-reversal (guarantee makes it a no-risk decision) and the close that emphasizes urgency.

**The operator discipline:** list every objection the avatar would raise. Place each objection inside the copy at the point where the reader would mentally raise it. Answer it inline before moving forward. Never let an objection sit unhandled.

## Framework 6 — The Call-to-Action Framework

**Kennedy's rule:** ask for the order. Explicitly. Repeatedly. Specifically.

**The components of a Kennedy-grade CTA:**

- **The exact action.** "Click here." "Call this number." "Mail back the enclosed reply card." "Reply to this email with the word YES." Ambiguity kills response.
- **The specific outcome of that action.** What happens immediately after they click / call / mail. "You'll be taken to a secure order page where you can claim your spot."
- **The deadline.** Time, date, quantity, or status — something that makes "now" different from "later".
- **The risk reversal.** "If it doesn't work, you get every penny back, no questions asked." The guarantee restated at the CTA point.
- **The repetition.** The CTA appears multiple times — after the offer, after the proof, after the price, in the P.S. The reader who skips around must land on a CTA wherever they stop.

**The CTA failure modes:**
- Soft language: "consider", "explore", "discover" — these are not asks.
- One CTA only: most readers scan; one CTA = high chance they miss it.
- No deadline: every CTA without urgency decays in response by the hour.
- Multiple competing CTAs: "call OR email OR fill in the form" — give one path, not three.

**Operator note:** Kennedy's lean is for ONE primary CTA, repeated 4-6 times throughout the letter, with the same wording each time. Consistency = clarity = response.
