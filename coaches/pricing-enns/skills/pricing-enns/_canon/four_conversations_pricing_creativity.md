# The Four Conversations + Pricing Creativity — Working Map

Synthesised from publicly available Enns interviews, 2Bobs episodes,
winwithoutpitching.com articles, and his guest appearances. The Four
Conversations is the architecture of an expertise sale. Pricing
Creativity is the technique layer applied inside the third and fourth
conversations. This is a map — buy the books for the full text.

The fundamental claim: the sale of expertise is not a single
transaction. It is a sequence of four distinct conversations, in
order, each with its own objective, tone, and exit criterion. Each
conversation sets up the next. Skipping one breaks the others.

---

## Conversation 1 — The Probative Conversation

**Objective.** Move the seller, in the buyer's mind, from "vendor" to
"expert." Establish that you are worth a real meeting.

**Where it happens.** Without you in the room. Through writing,
podcast appearances, referrals, positioning content, talks, published
frameworks. The probative conversation is your reputation arriving
before you do.

**Tone.** N/A — you are not present. The tone is the tone of your
public work.

**Inputs.** A clear position. A vertical narrow enough to dominate.
Published thinking — articles, frameworks, case data — that
demonstrates expertise without selling.

**Exit criterion.** The buyer arrives at the Qualifying Conversation
already convinced of your expertise. They are not deciding whether
you are competent. They are deciding whether you fit.

**Failure mode.** The buyer arrives unconvinced. You spend the first
meeting selling your credentials. You are in a vendor posture by
default. The Value Conversation, three meetings later, is doomed.

**For Ivan.** The probative conversation for Digital Rocket happens
through verticalised publishing — immigration-specific benchmarks,
franchise-specific case studies, vocational-training playbooks. Not
"meta ads for everyone." The narrower the position, the louder the
probative signal.

## Conversation 2 — The Qualifying Conversation

**Objective.** Mutual vetting. Determine if there is a real fit between
the buyer's need and the expert's capability — and if so, what kind
of engagement makes sense.

**Where it happens.** First real meeting. Usually 30-45 minutes. Phone
or video. No deck.

**Tone.** Cautious discernment. Two professionals deciding whether to
proceed. Not "selling." Not "buying." Vetting.

**Core questions to ask.**

- Why are we having this conversation? (Why now?)
- What happens if nothing changes? (Cost of inaction.)
- What have you tried? What worked? What didn't?
- Who else is at the table on your side? (Decision process.)
- What is the rough budget zone? (Money early.)
- What's your timeline?

**Core questions to answer (about yourself).** Only when asked, and
only briefly. Position, vertical focus, the kind of buyer you serve
best, the kind you don't.

**Money in the Qualifying Conversation.** Surface a range. Not
"what's your budget" (vendor question). Something like: "engagements
of this shape typically fall in the X to Y range — is that a
realistic zone for you?" If the buyer flinches at the floor, the
deal is unqualified.

**Exit criterion.** Both parties agree this is worth a second
meeting, or both parties agree it isn't. Clean disqualification is
a win.

**Failure mode.** You skip to solutions. You talk about how you work
instead of whether to work together. You let money go unmentioned.
The next meeting is a pitch, not a Value Conversation.

## Conversation 3 — The Value Conversation

This is the conversation where most agencies fail. The Value
Conversation is where value pricing theory goes to die — Enns says
this publicly. The theory of value pricing is easy. The conversation
is hard because it requires the seller to stay off solutions and
keep the buyer talking about the future state and the value of
arriving there.

**Objective.** Uncover the value the engagement could create for the
buyer — in money — and the share of that value that constitutes a
fair fee.

**Where it happens.** Second meeting. Senior buyer in the room.
Sometimes a working session of 60-90 minutes.

**Tone.** Curious, slow, peer-level. The expert is leading. The buyer
is doing most of the talking about their business.

**The four sub-questions of the Value Conversation.**

```
Q1. What does the desired future state look like, in your words?
Q2. How will we measure that we got there? (The metric.)
Q3. What is that worth — in money — over the relevant time horizon?
Q4. Of that value, what is fair compensation to the firm that
    helps create it?
```

Each question takes time. None can be skipped. The seller's job is
to ask, listen, and reflect — not to propose a solution. Solutions
come in the Closing Conversation.

**The "magic question" (Enns, publicly, multiple interviews).** Once
the buyer has named the value, ask: "what would we do, and what
would we charge, if money were no object?" This answer is the high
anchor for the proposal. It is also often the option the buyer
ultimately picks, once the anchor is on the table.

**Budget as pinch point, not ceiling.** When the buyer names a budget
in the Qualifying Conversation, treat it as a known constraint —
not as the size of the prize. The Value Conversation transcends the
budget by surfacing value far larger than the budget. The proposal
then offers a floor option that fits the budget *and* an anchor
option that fits the value. The buyer chooses.

**Exit criterion.** You have, on paper or in your head:
- The buyer's stated desired future state.
- The metric.
- The value (in money, with the buyer's number, not yours).
- A rough range for the fee that you both understand is a share of
  that value.

**Failure mode (the most common one).** The seller jumps to solutions.
"Here's how we'd attack this" before "here's what it's worth." The
moment you describe deliverables, the conversation shifts from value
to cost, and the price gets anchored to the cost of the deliverables
— not the value created. Once that happens, value pricing is dead
for this engagement. Start over with the next prospect.

## Conversation 4 — The Closing Conversation

**Objective.** Help the buyer select among options and commit to a
path forward.

**Where it happens.** After the proposal is sent. Usually a 30-minute
call. Sometimes by email. Sometimes in person.

**Tone.** Advisory. You are the doctor helping the patient choose
between treatment plans. You are not "closing" in the sales sense —
you are guiding.

**The structure.** The proposal has already done most of the work.
Three options, named, with the anchor first. In the Closing
Conversation you:

1. Walk the buyer through the options.
2. Answer questions about scope, not price.
3. Ask which option fits their thinking, and why.
4. If they hesitate on the target option, move them down to the
   floor — never down the price column.
5. Confirm next steps and signature.

**The "options do the negotiating" principle.** When a buyer pushes
back on price, the response is not a discount. The response is:
"the X option is the one that fits that budget. Here's what it does
not include compared to the Y option." The buyer is choosing among
options, not negotiating a single price down.

**Exit criterion.** Signed agreement, or a clean decline. Slow yes is
worse than fast no — name the slow yes, name the decision needed,
move it forward or end it.

**Failure mode.** A single-price proposal. The whole conversation
becomes a negotiation on that one number. The price drops. Margin
drops. Respect drops.

## Pricing Creativity — Technique Layer

The Four Conversations is the architecture. Pricing Creativity is the
set of pricing techniques applied inside the third and fourth
conversations. The book has more — these are the moves Ivan needs to
internalise first.

### Technique 1 — Three Options With High Anchor

Never present one price. Three options:

```
Option A — Floor (the buyer's budget zone)
  What can be done at the stated budget. Bare minimum value creation.
  Honest. Calibrated. Not crippled — just narrower.

Option B — Target (the recommended)
  The middle option. Where most buyers land. The "smart choice."
  Designed to be the obvious answer for the modal buyer.

Option C — Anchor (the "money no object" version)
  The highest-value version. Often 2-4x the target. Real, deliverable,
  not theatrical. The honest answer to "what would we do if budget
  weren't a constraint."
```

The anchor pulls the average settled price up regardless of which
option the buyer picks. This is documented in Enns' public talks and
the 2Bobs episode "The Power of Options."

Lead with the anchor. Verbally. In the proposal document. In the
walkthrough. The first option named is the option the buyer compares
the others against.

### Technique 2 — Name The Options On Character, Not Price

Don't call them Bronze, Silver, Gold. Don't call them Basic, Pro,
Enterprise. Name them on the *character* of the engagement.

For Ivan (immigration vertical), examples:

```
"Pilot"      — 90-day test, narrow scope, one case type.
"Core"       — Full retainer, two case types, monthly cadence.
"Flagship"   — Full retainer + creative production + competitive
               intelligence + quarterly strategy.
```

Names like "Bronze/Silver/Gold" force the buyer to compare on tier.
Names on character force the buyer to compare on fit. Fit-comparison
holds price better than tier-comparison.

### Technique 3 — Detach Price From Cost

Never show line items. Never show hours. Never break the fee into
"strategy hours + creative hours + media management hours." The
moment you do, the buyer mentally divides the total by a reasonable
hourly rate and notices the gap. Value pricing dies in the line-item
breakdown.

Show: fee, what's included, what's not included. That's it.

### Technique 4 — Charge For Thinking

Strategy and diagnosis are paid work. The "free audit" in the sales
process is a Manifesto violation (Proclamation 8). The on-ramp to a
big engagement is a small paid diagnostic — fixed scope, fixed fee,
clear deliverable. The diagnostic is the work, not the sales activity.

For Ivan: replace "free audit" with "paid 30-day diagnostic" priced
at a meaningful fraction of the engagement fee (often $5K-$25K
depending on engagement size). Buyers who refuse to pay for the
diagnostic are not buyers — they are tire-kickers. Walk.

### Technique 5 — Buyer Types Require Different Proposals

Enns (publicly, 2Bobs "Selling to Different Buyer Types") identifies
five buyer types:

```
Convenience  — Wants the path of least resistance. Price secondary.
Relationship — Buys from people they trust. Repeat purchases.
Price        — Cheapest wins. Avoid this buyer if possible.
Value        — Buys on ROI. The ideal buyer for expert services.
Poker Player — Performs price resistance regardless of budget.
               Tests the seller.
```

The three-option proposal works for all five, but the *anchor* moves:

- Value buyer: lead with the anchor verbally; they often pick it.
- Relationship buyer: anchor exists, but the target is positioned
  as "what most of our clients in your industry pick."
- Poker player: anchor is non-negotiable. Hold it. They are testing.
- Price buyer: requalify. Often disqualify.
- Convenience buyer: target option is the path of least resistance.

### Technique 6 — Address Money Early And Often

Repeat from Manifesto Proclamation 9. The rule operationally:

- Money first mention: Qualifying Conversation, as a range.
- Money second mention: Value Conversation, as a share of value.
- Money third mention: proposal, as three options.
- Money fourth mention: Closing Conversation, as confirmation.

Four touches before signature. Buyers who can't talk money four
times are not buyers.

### Technique 7 — Hold The Price; Move The Options

When the buyer pushes back on price, the seller has two legitimate
moves:

```
Move 1 — Move them to a lower-tier option with less scope.
Move 2 — Walk away.
```

The seller has *no* legitimate move to discount the same option. A
discount on the same option is a confession that the price was
arbitrary. It destroys trust in the option structure and trains the
buyer to push next time.

### Technique 8 — The Proposal Does The Negotiating

A well-built three-option proposal removes most negotiation. The
buyer is choosing among options, not arguing about a number. The
seller's job in the Closing Conversation is to help the choice, not
to defend the number. If the seller is defending the number, the
proposal architecture failed earlier.

## The Value-to-Fee Math (Working Heuristic)

Enns is careful not to publish a single share-of-value formula
publicly, because it varies by industry, by repeatability, and by
risk. Working heuristic synthesised from his interview corpus:

```
Floor option    : ~1-3% of annual value created
Target option   : ~3-7% of annual value created
Anchor option   : ~7-15% of annual value created
```

For Ivan, an immigration firm engagement worth, say, $2M/year in
incremental signed retainers:

```
Floor   : ~$20K-$60K (pilot scope)
Target  : ~$60K-$140K (full retainer)
Anchor  : ~$140K-$300K (full retainer + production + intel + strategy)
```

These numbers will feel large compared to a "media buyer retainer."
That's the point. The fee is priced to the value of signed retainers,
not to the cost of campaign management.

## Common Diagnostic Patterns

When Ivan brings a stuck deal, run the diagnostic against these
patterns first.

**Pattern 1 — Proposal exploded on price.**
Likely cause: Value Conversation skipped. The price has nothing to
anchor against except the buyer's budget guess.
Fix: do not adjust the price. Go back to the buyer with the Value
Conversation. "Before we discuss price, I want to make sure we both
understand the value at stake. Can we walk through that?"

**Pattern 2 — Buyer keeps demanding line items.**
Likely cause: positioned as vendor, not expert. Buyer is in
procurement mode.
Fix: refuse line items. "Our fees are priced to outcomes, not to
inputs. Here is what the engagement does and does not include."
If the buyer cannot accept that, requalify.

**Pattern 3 — Single-price proposal, buyer haggling.**
Likely cause: missing option structure.
Fix: withdraw the proposal. Replace with three options. Re-present.
Tell the buyer "I think I gave you the wrong document — here are
the three options you should be choosing from."

**Pattern 4 — Deal stuck after good Value Conversation.**
Likely cause: missing Closing Conversation, or wrong buyer in the
Closing Conversation.
Fix: identify the actual decision-maker. Get them on a call. Walk
options. Ask which fits and why.

**Pattern 5 — "We need to think about it."**
Likely cause: missing real objection. Slow yes wearing a polite mask.
Fix: name it. "Slow yes is worse than fast no. What is the actual
hesitation?" Buyers who respect the seller answer this question.

## Final Principle — The Sale Of Expertise Is Different

Most sales training assumes you are selling a product or a
transactional service. The sale of expertise is structurally
different:

- The product is invisible until after the contract.
- The buyer cannot evaluate it the way they evaluate a product.
- The buyer must trust the seller's judgment, not the seller's
  inventory.
- Pricing reflects the value to be created, not the cost of
  production.

Everything in the Four Conversations and Pricing Creativity flows
from that one structural fact. Apply it to Ivan's agency the same
way Enns applies it to creative firms: the underlying product is
the same — expertise sold before it is delivered.
