# The Business of Expertise — Framework Map

Synthesis of David C. Baker's 2017 book "The Business of Expertise: How
Entrepreneurial Experts Convert Insight to Impact + Wealth," reconstructed
from his public corpus (2Bobs episodes, Punctuation articles, conference
keynotes, public interviews). Frameworks expressed in own words. No book text
reproduced.

This is the positioning + expertise canon. If the incoming question is about
who-you-are-in-the-market (vertical, horizontal, demographic, psychographic,
narrowing the niche, demonstrating expertise, T-shape, pattern matching) —
this is the file.

---

## 1. The Waterfall of Differentiation

Three questions, in strict order. Most firms answer them out of order or skip
the middle one entirely, which is why they sound interchangeable.

1. **Category.** What box does the IRS / Companies House put you in. The
   NAICS / SIC code on the tax return. This is not a positioning choice — it
   was decided when you filed. Stop trying to invent a "new category." You
   are a marketing agency, a PR firm, a design studio, a dev shop. Own it.

2. **Specialization.** This is the only step that produces pricing power.
   Pick exactly one of four depths (see framework 2). If you cannot finish
   the sentence "we are the firm that ____" without using the word "and,"
   you have not specialized.

3. **Secondary differentiators.** Process, philosophy, proprietary tools,
   culture, founder story. These are useful for narrowing the shortlist from
   ten to one once you are already on it. They are NOT load-bearing. Hanging
   your positioning on "we care more" is hanging it on a hook screwed into
   drywall — it pulls out under any real pressure.

**Sequence failure mode:** principals who lead with secondary differentiators
("we're scrappy, transparent, founder-led") are usually unpositioned at the
specialization layer. That is why the proposals lose to firms with worse
process and clearer focus.

Reference: Baker / Enns episode "The Waterfall of Differentiation,"
https://2bobs.com/podcast/the-waterfall-of-differentiation

## 2. Positioning Depth — Four Real Options

Specialization is not a single thing. There are four ways to be deep:

- **Vertical.** One industry, multiple disciplines. "We do everything for
  credit unions." Strength: easy to find clients (associations, trade pubs,
  conferences). Strongest assumption of agency-of-record (AOR) status, which
  is where the highest fees live. Weakness: capped TAM, sensitive to industry
  cycles.

- **Horizontal.** One discipline, multiple verticals. "We do paid social,
  for anyone serious." Strength: huge TAM. Weakness: clients are scattered,
  harder to find you, expertise demonstration has to do more work because
  there is no conference circuit doing the work for you.

- **Demographic.** A defined audience that cuts across verticals.
  Hispanic-market, Gen Z, women 50+, founder-led businesses under $5M ARR.
  Strength: combines some of the addressability of vertical with TAM of
  horizontal. Underused. Most principals never seriously evaluate it.

- **Psychographic.** A worldview or mindset across verticals. Mission-driven
  founders, design-first companies, conservative-Christian businesses,
  privately-held second-generation operators. Hardest to articulate, but when
  it works, it builds the strongest tribal pull and the most word-of-mouth.

**Baker's bias** (publicly stated on 2Bobs and elsewhere): he would
personally choose vertical, but advises ~85% of clients to start horizontal
because that is what they actually want and will commit to. The wrong answer
is "I'll figure it out as I go" — that is unpositioned with optimism on top.

References:
- "How to Make Horizontal Positioning Work," https://2bobs.com/podcast/how-to-make-horizontal-positioning-work
- "The Business of Expertise - Part 2," https://2bobs.com/podcast/the-business-of-expertise-part-2

## 3. The Positioning Sweet Spot — 10 to 200 Competitors, 2,000 to 10,000 Prospects

Baker's heuristic for whether you have narrowed enough.

- **Competitors.** Below 10, you have invented a category nobody is searching
  for. Above 200, you are still a generalist. Sweet spot: 10–200 real
  competitors who do roughly what you do.
- **Prospects.** Below 2,000, the TAM is too small to support a sales
  pipeline. Above 10,000, you have not narrowed enough — your buyer cannot
  self-identify.

If those two numbers don't both pass, the positioning is wrong shape, not
wrong color. Don't tweak the website. Reshape the position.

## 4. The Three S's — Specialization, Strategy, Sales

Order matters. Skip one and the next does not work.

1. **Specialization** — you have answered the waterfall, you have picked a
   depth, you have 10–200 competitors and 2,000–10,000 prospects.

2. **Strategy** — you reposition the entire firm on thinking, not doing.
   The website talks about the patterns you have seen, not the deliverables
   you produce. Every new client enters through a paid strategic engagement,
   not a free pitch followed by an SOW. This is what separates an expert firm
   from a vendor.

3. **Sales** — sales becomes about qualifying out, not closing in. You want
   more opportunity than capacity, because the delta is your ability to say
   no, and saying no is what protects price.

Doing this in reverse (sales first, then strategy talk, then specialization
when you have time) is how most agencies stay stuck at $2–5M with thin
margins for a decade.

## 5. Pattern Matching — The Real Substance of Expertise

Expertise is not what you know. It is what you have seen often enough to
spot the next one. Pattern matching only happens at depth: you must have
worked with enough of the same kind of client to spot what is repeated and
what is anomaly.

Operational consequence: if your client list is 30 different industries,
you have 30 sample sizes of 1. You are not an expert at anything. You are
a generalist with experience. Those are different products.

This is the deepest argument for narrowing. It is not a marketing argument
("you'll be easier to refer"). It is an epistemic argument ("you will
literally know more than you currently know").

## 6. The T-Shaped Expert

The principal is wide AND deep. Deep in the specialization (vertical /
horizontal / demographic / psychographic). Wide enough across business,
finance, operations, technology, behavioral economics to map insight from
adjacent domains onto the deep core.

The failure modes:
- All wide, no deep — generalist principal, will lose to specialists.
- All deep, no wide — technician principal, will produce great work and
  bad pricing, because there is no cross-domain insight to charge for.

The hiring consequence: the senior people you hire fill the deep stem. The
principal must keep filling the horizontal bar by reading, advising in
adjacent fields, sitting on boards outside the core vertical.

## 7. Saying No — The Most Underused Skill

In Baker's framing, the inability to say no is the single biggest blocker on
a firm reaching elite EHR. Three layers:

1. **Saying no to prospects who don't fit.** Wrong vertical, wrong size,
   wrong philosophy. Saying yes to misfits trains your team to deliver
   slower and pricier than your specialization would allow.

2. **Saying no to scope.** Inside an engagement, the moment you say "sure,
   we can do that too" without repricing, you are eroding EHR in real time.

3. **Saying no to existing clients you should fire.** Most firms have never
   fired a client. The target is 5–15% of clients fired per year — not as
   punishment, as portfolio rebalancing.

If the principal says "I haven't fired anyone in 12 months," the diagnosis
is usually demand-constrained masking as principal-emotionally-attached.

## 8. Demonstrate Expertise, Do Not Claim It

Claiming expertise on the website ("we are experts in X") is a tell that
you are not. Real expertise is demonstrated through published work —
the more insight given away for free, the less effort it takes to convert
on the back end. The mechanism:

- Weekly written insight (Baker's own newsletter, started 1996, hits ~13,000
  agency principals weekly — this is his proof, not a metaphor).
- Speaking at events where your specialization congregates.
- A book or long-form artifact every 3–5 years that becomes the calling
  card.
- A podcast or interview presence in the specialization's media.

The unlock: the better the positioning, the easier it is to give insight
away. A generalist has nothing specific to publish because their "audience"
is too diffuse.

## 9. The Client Portfolio Is A Portfolio

Think like a fund manager. Each client is a position. You weight, rebalance,
exit. The discipline:

- **Concentration limit.** No single client above ~25% of AGI (15% is
  safer). Above that, you are not the principal of an agency, you are an
  employee of that client with extra steps.
- **Vintage.** Track clients by year of acquisition. The clients you took
  on three years ago at lower fees and worse fit are dragging the average
  EHR down. They are the rebalancing target.
- **Quality drift.** Each year the portfolio should be incrementally better
  by fit, fee, and pattern density. If it is not, you are not curating, you
  are accumulating.

Reference: "Six Hidden Benefits of Client Concentration,"
https://2bobs.com/podcast (search title)

## 10. Strategy Before Implementation — The Paid Diagnostic Front Door

A core operational shift from the book and the podcast: new clients should
enter through a paid strategic engagement, not a free proposal followed by
implementation.

The mechanics:
- Diagnostic engagement: 4–8 weeks, fixed fee ($25K–$100K depending on firm
  scale and vertical), focused on patterns and recommendations.
- Output: a written diagnostic with prioritized recommendations.
- Optional follow-on: implementation of recommendations, at full standard
  fees, with the diagnostic having already qualified fit on both sides.

Why this works: it sells thinking, not doing. It filters tire-kickers. It
eliminates the free-pitch labor sink. It anchors the relationship as
strategic from day one, which is what protects fees later.

The principal's resistance is usually fear that prospects won't pay for
diagnosis. The reality (per Baker's advisees): when positioning is real,
the conversion is fine. When positioning is fake, no front door works.
