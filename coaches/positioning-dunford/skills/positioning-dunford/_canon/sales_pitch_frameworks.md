## What this book is, and what it replaces

*Sales Pitch* is the operational sequel to *Obviously Awesome*. The first book installs positioning as a strategic artifact. This one installs the sales narrative that ships on top of that positioning. The replacement target is the "demo-driven pitch" — the slide deck that opens with company background, dives into a feature tour, and asks the buyer to assemble the value story themselves. Dunford's argument: that pitch loses to any seller who frames the market for the buyer first.

The pitch structure has two halves: a **Setup** that aligns the buyer to your worldview, and a **Follow-Through** that proves you deliver differentiated value inside that worldview.

> "The setup is where we give customers a way to think about the entire market and get them aligned with our point of view."
> — adapted from *Sales-First Storytelling* on Dunford's Substack
> https://aprildunford.substack.com/p/sales-first-storytelling

## Pitch map (the operator's view)

The book runs ~9 chapters, but the operational asset is a single sales-narrative storyboard. Each "step" below maps to one section of that storyboard.

| Step | Section | Job inside the pitch |
|------|---------|----------------------|
| 1 | Insight | Reframe how the buyer should think about the market |
| 2 | Alternatives | Honest tour of what else they could do |
| 3 | Perfect-World Setup | Define what an ideal solution would look like |
| 4 | Introducing Us | The category and one-line of what you are |
| 5 | Differentiated Value Themes | 2-4 value themes only you can deliver |
| 6 | Proof | Evidence each value theme is real |
| 7 | Objection Handling | Pre-empt the predictable pushbacks |
| 8 | The Ask | Bridge to pricing, next step, decision |

## Framework 1 — Why "We're Better at X" Pitches Lose

**What it is:** the default failure of feature-first sales decks. Seller opens with company logo, mission, history. Then a feature tour. A competitor comparison chart reads "we have A, B, C — they don't." Buyer leaves unable to repeat the value story.

**Why it fails:**
- Buyer has no frame for *why* features A, B, C matter
- Comparison charts trigger skepticism — every vendor's chart has this shape
- "Better at X" is a fight on the dominant player's turf; they win on their own turf by default
- The pitch never reframes what the right solution looks like, so buyer defaults to the incumbent's criteria

**The replacement:** lead with insight that reframes the buying criteria. Buyer should leave with a different idea of what a good solution looks like — and that new shape should be the shape of YOUR product.

**Failure mode the seller will recognize:** demo went well, buyer said "this is great," then the deal stalls because the buyer can't articulate to their boss why you instead of the incumbent.

## Framework 2 — The Setup vs. The Follow-Through

**What it is:** the two-act structure organizing the pitch.

**Act 1 — Setup (steps 1-3):**
- Goal: align buyer to your worldview of the market BEFORE talking about your product
- Three moves: deliver an insight, walk alternatives honestly, paint the perfect-world solution
- Ends when buyer is nodding — "yes, that's how I'd evaluate this"

**Act 2 — Follow-Through (steps 4-8):**
- Goal: prove your product is the obvious instance of the perfect-world solution
- Five moves: introduce in category terms, deliver value themes, prove each, handle objections, bridge to the ask

**Execution rule:** don't show product until Act 2. Demoing inside Act 1 reverts the buyer to feature-checklist mode; you lose the frame.

**Failure mode:** truncating Act 1 because the seller is uncomfortable not talking about the product. The Setup feels like "not selling" but it's where the deal is won.

## Framework 3 — Step 1: The Insight (the opening reframe)

**What it is:** the one-thing-the-market-doesn't-yet-understand that you, as the seller, see clearly. The insight is your point of view about why the obvious approaches don't work, or why a new approach is now possible.

**Mechanism:** the insight is not a stat, not a trend, not a hook. It's a frame. A frame says: "here is how to think about this whole problem." The frame, if accepted by the buyer, makes your unique attributes obviously valuable.

**Three insight archetypes:**
1. **Market shift insight** — something changed in the world (regulation, tech, buyer behavior) that makes the old approach obsolete
2. **Hidden cost insight** — the obvious approach has a cost buyers underestimate
3. **False trade-off insight** — buyers think they have to choose between A and B; you reveal A and B were never actually opposed

**Execution rule:** the insight must be debatable but defensible. If it's universally agreed, it's not an insight — it's table stakes. If it's indefensible, the buyer dismisses you in step 1.

**Failure mode:** dressing up a feature claim as an insight. "Most CRMs are slow" is not an insight; it's a feature complaint. "Sales teams now win on speed of follow-up, not on relationship depth" — that's an insight (or at least an argument).

---

## Framework 4 — Step 2: Alternatives (the honest tour)

**What it is:** an explicit walk through what the buyer could do, INCLUDING do-nothing, the dominant incumbent, and the in-house build. Treated honestly — each gets a fair pros/cons read from the seller.

**Mechanism:** when the seller volunteers a fair view of the alternatives, the buyer trusts the seller. That trust is the currency the seller will spend in steps 5-8.

> "The competitive alternatives... are often things that don't look like direct competitors."
> — Dunford, on positioning mistakes
> https://www.aprildunford.com/post/an-introduction-to-positioning

**Execution rule:** show real alternatives, not strawmen. If the buyer can name a competitor you didn't list, you've lost the frame. If your "comparison" is "us vs. a worse version of us," the buyer detects the trick.

**Failure mode:** turning the alternatives section into a competitor trash-talk slide. That triggers the buyer's defense mechanism — they'll mentally argue for the competitor you just attacked.

---

## Framework 5 — Step 3: The Perfect-World Setup

**What it is:** the section where the seller defines what an ideal solution to this problem would look like, in the buyer's terms, based on the insight from step 1.

**Mechanism:** the seller is implicitly setting the buying criteria. By describing the perfect world, the seller is teaching the buyer what to evaluate against. Done right, the perfect-world description is the silhouette of the seller's product — but framed entirely as buyer-side outcomes, not features.

**Execution rule:** the perfect world must logically follow from the insight (step 1) and address the gaps exposed by the alternatives (step 2). If a buyer can imagine a different "perfect world" that excludes you, you haven't done step 1 hard enough.

**Failure mode:** describing a perfect world that's actually a feature list with bullet points. The buyer should hear outcomes ("we'd close cases in days, not weeks; the ops team wouldn't touch every record"), not capabilities ("AI-powered workflow automation").

---

## Framework 6 — Step 4: Introducing Us (the category line)

**What it is:** one sentence — at most two — that names the market category and your one-line difference. This is where the product finally enters the pitch.

**Mechanism:** the category line is a frame anchor. It tells the buyer what bucket to put you in (so they don't have to invent one) and what makes you the specific instance of that bucket worth their time.

**Format:** "[Product] is a [market category] that [one-line of the differentiated promise the perfect-world section just primed]."

**Execution rule:** the category claim must be one the buyer recognizes (or quickly accepts) — and the differentiated promise must connect directly to the perfect-world section the buyer just nodded along to.

**Failure mode:** burying the category. If the buyer leaves not knowing what category you're in, every downstream comparison goes wrong. They'll invent a category for you — and it'll be the wrong one.

---

## Framework 7 — Step 5: Differentiated Value Themes (2-4 of them)

**What it is:** the 2-4 outcomes you deliver that no listed alternative can match. Each is a value theme — a grouping of related unique-attribute-plus-buyer-outcome statements under a single buyer-meaningful headline.

> "Differentiated value is what we can do for a customer that no other alternative solution can."
> — Dunford, paraphrased across multiple talks
> https://businessofsoftware.org/talks/your-differentiated-value/

**Mechanism:** value themes do the work of compression. The seller doesn't recite 30 features; they group features under 2-4 buyer-side themes that each map back to the perfect world.

**Why 2-4:**
- Fewer than 2: looks one-dimensional, easy to commoditize
- More than 4: buyer can't remember any of them after the call

**Execution rule:** each value theme must be (a) tied to a unique attribute, (b) translated to buyer-side outcome, (c) impossible for the listed alternatives to claim honestly.

**Failure mode:** value themes that any competitor could write on their homepage. "We're easy to use" is not a value theme. "Your ops team configures the workflow without involving engineering" might be.

---

## Framework 8 — Step 6: Proof (per value theme)

**What it is:** the evidence layer. For each differentiated value theme in step 5, you give the buyer reason to believe it's real.

**Proof types (rank-ordered by buyer trust):**
1. Customer outcome data — a named customer, a real number, a verifiable result
2. Live product demo of the specific capability (not a feature tour — a *proof* demo)
3. Third-party analyst, press, or industry report
4. Detailed customer story with the buyer's archetype
5. Founder-as-expert credentialing (only useful if the founder is genuinely a category authority)

**Execution rule:** each value theme gets its OWN proof. A single customer logo that "proves" all four themes is not proof — it's wallpaper.

**Failure mode:** stacking proof at the end of the pitch as a logo wall. The buyer scans it, sees nothing connected to the specific themes, and discounts all of it. Proof must travel WITH each theme, not in its own slide.

---

## Framework 9 — Step 7: Objection Handling Baked In

**What it is:** the predictable objections — pricing, switching cost, security, integration, "we'll build it ourselves" — get addressed inside the pitch BEFORE the buyer raises them.

**Mechanism:** an objection raised by the buyer is hostile data. The same objection volunteered by the seller is trust-building data. Volunteering creates the impression that the seller has thought through what the buyer would worry about.

**Execution rule:** list the 3-5 objections that ALWAYS come up in your pipeline. Bake the response into the pitch as a "the question I usually get here is X — here's how we handle it" beat. Don't bury objection-handling for a Q&A at the end.

**Failure mode:** treating objections as a Q&A problem instead of a pitch-design problem. If your last 10 deals all stalled on the same objection, that objection belongs in slide 12, not in an email response three weeks later.

---

## Framework 10 — Step 8: The Ask / Bridge to Pricing

**What it is:** the explicit next-step request. Not a "we'd love to follow up" — a specific, named, time-bound ask that matches where you are in the sales motion.

**Three ask archetypes by stage:**
1. **Discovery ask** — "I'd like to bring in [specific stakeholder] for a working session next week"
2. **Mid-cycle ask** — "Let me get you a tailored pricing proposal by Friday — does that work?"
3. **Late-cycle ask** — "Here's what I'd need from you to sign by month-end"

**Execution rule:** the ask must logically follow the proof. If the proof was thin, asking for a contract is jarring. If the proof was heavy, asking for "another meeting" leaks momentum.

**Failure mode:** asking for the wrong commitment at the wrong stage. Most sales pitches over-ask early (asking for a decision before proof has landed) or under-ask late (asking for "more time to think" when the deal is hot).

## Framework 11 — Pitch Storyboard as a Leadership Alignment Artifact

**What it is:** the 8-step storyboard is not just a sales deck. It's the cross-functional alignment doc.

**Mechanism:**
- Sales runs the storyboard verbally
- Marketing converts it into homepage, demo video, email sequence
- Product validates that the roadmap still serves the differentiated value themes
- Customer success onboards buyers in the same frame they bought in

**Execution rule:** ship as a one-page bullet-point artifact. Every leadership-team member should recite the 8 sections from memory.

**Failure mode:** sales has one deck, marketing has another, the website tells a third story. Buyers experience fracture and revert to the incumbent's frame.

---

## Framework 12 — When to Use This Pitch Structure (and When Not To)

**Use it when:**
- B2B considered purchase (sales cycle >2 weeks)
- Buyer is comparing 2+ alternatives
- Buying committee involved
- Deal size justifies a real sales conversation
- Differentiated value requires explanation

**Don't use it when:**
- Self-serve PLG signup, no sales conversation
- Pure transactional sale, well-understood category
- Renewal of existing contract (different motion)
- Sub-$1K MRR product (pitch length kills CAC)

**Failure mode:** running the full 8-step pitch on a $99/mo SaaS signup. The Setup-and-Follow-Through is calibrated for considered B2B; on small transactions the buyer wants the product, not the worldview.
