# The Art of Selling Your Business — Framework Map

**Author:** John Warrillow
**Book:** *The Art of Selling Your Business: Winning Strategies & Secret Hacks for Exiting on Top* — Greenleaf Book Group, 2021

**Source treatment:** Framework structures described in own words for synthetic-coach use. The book is not reproduced. Each framework is mapped to a section of the book and described mechanically. ## What this book solves

The earlier two books (*Built to Sell*, *The Automatic Customer*) are about engineering the business so it CAN sell. *The Art of Selling Your Business* is about actually running the sale process — picking buyers, structuring deals, surviving diligence, and negotiating the terms that determine what you actually walk away with.

The single hardest insight in the book: **the headline price is the least informative number in the deal.** Two $10M offers can produce wildly different cash-in-pocket outcomes depending on cash-at-close ratio, earnout structure, escrow holdback, working-capital peg, and tax treatment. A skilled seller can extract more cash from a $7M offer with the right structure than from a $10M offer with the wrong one.

## Framework 1 — The Four Offer Structures

**What it is:** Every M&A offer falls into one of four buckets. Knowing which determines what you're actually being offered.

**The four:**
1. **All cash at close** — full price wired at closing minus escrow holdback. Lowest headline, highest certainty. Rare above ~$5M.
2. **Cash + earnout** — partial cash at close (60-75%), balance tied to performance milestones over 1-5 years. Most common. Transfers risk back to seller.
3. **Cash + seller financing (seller note)** — buyer pays cash plus a promissory note. Seller becomes a lender. Carries default risk if buyer fails post-close.
4. **Cash + rollover equity** — seller keeps a minority equity stake in the acquired entity (often a "majority recap" with PE). Creates a "second bite" but ties seller liquidity to buyer's outcome.

**Mechanism:** each structure shifts a different risk back to the seller. Cash is risk-free. Earnouts shift performance risk. Seller notes shift credit risk. Rollover equity shifts business-continuity risk.

**Execution rule:** convert every offer into "cash-equivalent at close." Discount earnouts by your honest probability of milestone achievement (30-50% for stretch targets). Discount seller notes by default risk. Discount rollover by faith in the next exit.

**Failure mode:** comparing headline-to-headline. A $10M deal at 50% cash with a 5-year earnout often produces less cash than an $8M deal at 90% cash.

## Framework 2 — Asset Sale vs. Stock Sale

**What it is:** The legal structure of what gets transferred. Two fundamentally different deals with very different tax and liability outcomes.

**The two:**
- **Asset sale** — buyer purchases specific assets (contracts, IP, equipment, customer list, brand) and assumes specific liabilities. Legal entity stays with seller. Default for sub-$50M deals.
- **Stock sale** — buyer purchases the entire legal entity, taking all assets AND all liabilities. Default in larger/strategic deals where contracts can't easily reassign.

**The trade-off:**
- **Buyer prefers asset sale** — step up the basis (depreciation shield), leave behind unknown liabilities, cherry-pick what to take.
- **Seller prefers stock sale** — lower capital-gains treatment, clean break from contingent liabilities, often a higher headline price.

**Mechanism:** the structural choice is worth real money. A stock-sale election can be worth 5-15% of deal value in tax differential to the seller.

**Execution rule:** model both scenarios with your tax advisor before LOI. Lock structure (and tax allocation if asset) in the LOI.

**Failure mode:** treating "asset vs stock" as a lawyer's detail. It's a price decision that gets executed through legal documents. Founders who don't engage early surrender 10%+ of value.

## Framework 3 — Earnout Structure and the Earnout Trap

**What it is:** A diagnostic on whether an earnout is achievable or just a discount disguised as upside.

**The trap mechanics:** after close, the seller no longer controls the business but is still measured on it. Buyer priorities (integration, cost-cutting, repricing) conflict with seller earnout targets (revenue growth, customer count). Disputes over what "counts" are the #1 source of post-close lawsuits.

**Five design rules to negotiate hard:**
1. **Short duration** — 12-24 months max.
2. **Simple, single-variable metric** — revenue beats EBITDA (EBITDA is buyer-controlled — they can load corporate overhead onto your unit).
3. **No carve-out clauses** that let buyer exclude initiatives, products, or customers.
4. **Operating autonomy** — written guarantee you run the unit as a standalone P&L through earnout.
5. **Acceleration triggers** — if buyer sells, fires you, changes comp, or pivots, earnout accelerates and pays in full.

**Mechanism:** without these protections, the earnout becomes an option the buyer can deactivate.

**Execution rule:** negotiate earnout language as hard as headline price. Trade structure concessions for cash-at-close lift wherever possible.

**Failure mode:** accepting an earnout as written because the headline is attractive. Most disappointed sellers can trace it to earnout fights they lost because the language wasn't tight at signing.

## Framework 4 — Escrow Holdbacks and Reps & Warranties

**What it is:** The portion of the headline price held back at close to indemnify the buyer against breaches of the seller's reps and warranties.

**Typical structure:** 10-20% of purchase price held in escrow, 12-24 months. Released to seller if no claims; reduced by documented claims if any.

**Common rep breaches that trigger claims:** undisclosed liabilities, tax exposure, customer/employee misrepresentation, IP ownership disputes.

**Two shifts to know:**
- **Rep & warranty insurance (RWI)** — for larger deals, insurance substitutes for the holdback. Premium 2-4% of policy limit. Often buyer-paid.
- **Indemnity caps** — total seller liability typically capped at 10-20% of price (lower with RWI). Negotiate this hard — uncapped indemnity has clawed back full purchase prices.

**Mechanism:** holdback is the buyer's insurance against the seller having lied or been wrong. Size and duration reflect risk perception.

**Execution rule:** front-load disclosure. The disclosure schedule is your shield — anything disclosed there cannot be a basis for a claim. Over-disclose.

**Failure mode:** treating the disclosure schedule as lawyer-only. Founders who skip the review lose holdback to claims preventable by five more sentences.

## Framework 5 — The 10 Drivers of Sale Multiple

**What it is:** The ten conditions that lift the multiple a buyer pays. Overlaps with the 8 Drivers of Company Value but framed for the live sale process.

**The ten:**
1. **Recurring revenue %** — higher predictable recurring = higher multiple.
2. **Customer concentration** — no customer above 15%.
3. **Owner independence** — 90-day-offline test passes.
4. **Documented growth runway** — written plan with proof of execution capability.
5. **Defensibility** — a named monopoly source (IP, brand, network effect, switching cost).
6. **Clean financials** — review-grade or audit-grade, 3 years deep.
7. **Trailing growth** — 20%+ YoY in last 2-3 years (high-growth bucket gets premium multiples).
8. **Margin profile** — gross margin at or above industry top quartile.
9. **Management depth** — a #2 plus at least one functional head who'll stay post-close.
10. **Diversified distribution** — no single channel above 30% of new revenue.

**Mechanism:** each driver shifts the buyer's underwriting in your favour. Strong on most = premium. Weak on structural ones (concentration, owner-dependence) = discount.

**Execution rule:** run the checklist 12-18 months before any process. Fix what's fixable, disclose what isn't.

**Failure mode:** running a process without running the checklist on yourself first. Buyers will find every weakness; you should know them first.

## Framework 6 — Buyer Type Selection

**What it is:** The first strategic choice — which type of buyer to court. Determines process, timeline, and realistic multiple range.

**The three types:**
- **Strategic buyer** — corporate acquirer who values your business for what it adds to theirs (customers, IP, geography, capability). Pays the highest multiple because they value synergies. Slowest diligence (3-9 months). Most likely to demand earnouts.
- **Financial buyer** — PE, family office, search funds. Values cash flow. Pays market multiple. Often keeps seller on 1-3 years. Heavy but predictable diligence.
- **Individual buyer** — owner-operator, search fund, MBA buyer. Wants a job + a return. Lowest multiple. Fastest on small deals. Highest emotional volatility.

**Mechanism:** different buyers underwrite different things. A strategic underwrites synergies that don't exist for a financial. Same business, different value.

**Execution rule:** identify the most plausible buyer type before hiring a banker. Banker selection follows buyer selection.

**Failure mode:** running a "broad process" when your business is only strategic to a narrow set. Wide processes burn months and signal weakness when most invitees decline.

## Framework 7 — Broker / Banker vs Direct Sale

**What it is:** Whether to hire an intermediary or run it yourself.

**Hire an M&A advisor when:** deal size > ~$3-5M, multiple plausible buyers (broker creates a competitive process), unknown buyer universe, or you can't absorb 6-12 months of distraction.

**Run it direct when:** deal size < ~$2M (broker fees eat too much), single specific buyer already interested, or business too small for a competitive lift.

**Broker fee structures:**
- **Lehman scale** — declining % (5% of first $1M, 4% next, down to ~1%). Sub-$10M deals.
- **Flat percentage** — 5-8% of deal value. Mid-market default.
- **Retainer + success fee** — monthly retainer ($5-20K) credited against success fee. Aligns effort.
- **Minimum success fee** — broker's floor. Negotiate aggressively.

**Mechanism:** a good broker creates a competitive process. Competitive bids typically lift price 15-30% above a single-buyer unsolicited offer.

**Execution rule:** taking a single inbound offer without a broker-run process leaves 15-30% on the table.

**Failure mode:** hiring a broker for a deal too small to get their focus. Sub-$3M deals get assigned to junior staff and end up worse than founder-run direct processes.

## Framework 8 — Due Diligence Preparation (the diligence box)

**What it is:** The pre-process work that determines how cleanly diligence runs.

**The data room — what every buyer asks for:**
- **Financial** — 3 years monthly P&L, balance sheets, cash flow. Audit or review opinion if available.
- **Legal** — formation docs, cap table, material contracts, IP filings, employment agreements, leases.
- **Customer** — top-20 customer revenue history, churn analytics, NPS, contract terms.
- **Operations** — org chart, key-person agreements, SOPs, vendor contracts.
- **Tax** — 3 years of returns + any audits, sales-tax registrations, payroll-tax compliance.
- **HR** — employee roster, comp bands, equity grants, any disputes.

**Mechanism:** a clean data room signals a clean business. A messy one signals hidden problems and triggers diligence price reductions.

**Execution rule:** build the room 12 months before market. Have an outside CPA do a "quality of earnings" (QoE) review BEFORE the buyer's QoE — find the adjustments yourself.

**Failure mode:** assembling reactively during diligence. The standard "diligence haircut" is 10-20% of the LOI price.

## Framework 9 — Working Capital Peg

**What it is:** A buried negotiating point that frequently costs sellers 5-10% of headline price if mishandled.

**Mechanically:** buyer requires business delivered with a "normal" amount of working capital (cash + receivables + inventory − payables) at close. If actual closing working capital is BELOW the agreed peg, seller writes buyer a cheque for the shortfall. If ABOVE, buyer pays seller the excess.

**The trap:** how "normal" is calculated. Buyers propose pegs based on 12-month averages, often well above actual operating need.

**Execution rule:** negotiate the peg in the LOI. Insist on a peg reflecting ACTUAL operating need, not historical averages. Define what's included (deferred revenue is the most contested item).

**Failure mode:** waving off working capital as "lawyer stuff." Sellers routinely sign LOIs with vague language and discover at closing they owe the buyer $200K-$1M to "true up" the peg.

## Framework 10 — The Post-LOI Re-Trade Defense

**What it is:** Defense against the most common buyer tactic — re-trading the price downward between LOI and closing.

**The buyer's playbook:** LOI signed → exclusivity period (60-120 days) → during diligence, buyer "discovers" issues → issues become re-trade ammunition → seller is exhausted, committed, has no alternative → accepts re-trade.

**The seller's defenses:**
1. **Short exclusivity** — 45 days max where possible. Each extra week is leverage to the buyer.
2. **Pre-diligence everything** — anticipate issues; disclose upfront so they can't become re-trade ammunition.
3. **Maintain backup buyers** — at least one credible alternative kept warm through exclusivity.
4. **Document buyer commitments** — every "we're committed at this price" should be in writing.
5. **Be willing to walk** — the credible threat is the only real defense.

**Mechanism:** re-trades work via asymmetric commitment. The seller is psychologically committed; the buyer is psychologically fresh. That asymmetry is the seller's vulnerability.

**Execution rule:** assume a re-trade attempt. Build walk-away leverage during exclusivity.

**Failure mode:** treating LOI signing as "deal done." Deal done is wire received. Until then, every day is negotiation.
