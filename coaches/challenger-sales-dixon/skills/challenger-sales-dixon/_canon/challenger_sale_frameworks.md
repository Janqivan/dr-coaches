## Research backdrop (the operator's view)

The Challenger Sale is built on a CEB (Corporate Executive Board, now Gartner) study of more than **6,000 sales reps across 83 companies** plus a parallel study of **600+ complex purchases** and **700+ individual stakeholders** inside B2B buying committees. Two findings drove the books:

1. **Sales rep behavior clusters into 5 profiles.** Some profiles consistently outperform in complex B2B. The "best practice" most sales orgs hire and train for — the Relationship Builder — is the worst-performing profile in complex deals.

2. **The customer side of B2B has shifted from individual buyer to consensus committee.** Average buying group sizes climbed past 5.4 stakeholders (and have continued upward since). Selling to one champion stopped working.

The Challenger Sale (2011) addresses the rep side. The Challenger Customer (2015) addresses the customer side. They are operationally one system.

## Framework 1 — The 5 Rep Profiles (Challenger Sale, Ch 1-2)

The CEB study clustered every rep across 44 attributes into one of five dominant profiles. Each profile shows up in roughly equal proportion across the rep population. The distribution of **stars** (top performers) is what differs.

| Profile | What they do | Share of all reps | Share of stars |
|---------|--------------|-------------------|----------------|
| **Hard Worker** | Self-motivated, persistent, accepts coaching | ~21% | ~21% |
| **Lone Wolf** | Self-confident, instinctive, ignores process | ~18% | ~25% |
| **Relationship Builder** | Accessible, generous, builds advocates | ~21% | ~7% |
| **Problem Solver** | Detail-oriented, reliable, post-sale focused | ~14% | ~14% |
| **Challenger** | Debates, teaches, takes control | ~27% | ~39% |

**Top finding:** in complex B2B specifically, the Challenger share among stars climbs to **54%**, and the Relationship Builder share drops to **4%**.

**The counter-intuitive conclusion:** the profile that most sales organizations actively hire for and train into — the friendly, accommodating Relationship Builder — is the worst performer in the complex deals that matter most.

**Failure mode:** orgs reading this as "fire the Relationship Builders". Dixon's actual recommendation — most reps can be coached toward Challenger behaviors. The profile is behavioral, not innate.

## Framework 2 — Teach-Tailor-Take Control (Challenger Sale, Ch 3-5)

The three behaviors that distinguish the Challenger from the other four profiles. All three together — not any one alone — produce the result.

**Teach** — bring a perspective on the customer's business they didn't already have. Not a feature deck. Not a value prop. An **insight** about their world that reframes how they think about the problem they're trying to solve. The teaching pitch leads the customer toward a conclusion that uniquely points to the rep's offering.

**Tailor** — calibrate the teaching pitch to the specific stakeholder. A CFO doesn't hear what a COO hears. A Managing Partner doesn't hear what a Director of Operations hears. The Challenger knows whose mind they're trying to change, and tunes the message to the level and outcome that stakeholder owns.

**Take Control** — Challengers don't avoid the awkward parts of the sale. They discuss money early. They push back on customer assumptions. They tell the customer when the customer is wrong. They create **constructive tension** — productive disagreement that moves the conversation forward.

**Operator distinction Dixon draws:** Teach-Tailor-Take Control is a behavior pattern, not a personality. The Relationship Builder fails because they Tailor without Teaching and never Take Control. The Lone Wolf Takes Control but doesn't Teach the broader committee.

**Failure mode at DR:** reps who "Teach" with a feature comparison ("we run ads better than your last agency"). That's not a Teach. A Teach reframes the customer's metric — "you're measuring CPL; the metric that actually predicts revenue is cost per signed case, and here's why your current setup can't measure it."

## Framework 3 — The Commercial Insight (Challenger Sale, Ch 4-5)

The Teach pitch isn't generic education. It's a structured insight with four required properties.

1. **It leads to your unique strength.** The customer's logical conclusion from the insight points at your offering. If a competitor could close on the same insight, it's not a commercial insight — it's content marketing.

2. **It challenges customer assumptions.** Disrupts the status quo. Names a thing the customer is currently doing that's no longer working. The insight pitch is uncomfortable for the customer to hear. If it's comfortable, it's not a commercial insight.

3. **It catalyzes action.** Quantifies the cost of inaction. Without urgency, the customer files the insight under "interesting" and does nothing.

4. **It scales.** Built once, used many times across the rep team. Per-deal insights don't scale and rep adoption collapses.

**Construction sequence (the Dixon recipe):**
1. Start with the unique strength your firm has that competitors don't.
2. Reverse-engineer the customer assumption your unique strength refutes.
3. Build the data point (research, benchmark, study) that proves the assumption is wrong.
4. Quantify what it costs the customer to keep operating on the wrong assumption.
5. Land on a logical next step that involves your offering.

**At DR:** the "CPL is the wrong metric, cost per signed case is the right metric" pitch is a textbook commercial insight. It refutes the assumption every law-firm MP brings to the table, quantifies the cost (signed cases divided by ad spend, not leads divided by ad spend), and lands on a system (Case Flow) that measures the right thing.

## Framework 4 — Constructive Tension (Challenger Sale, Ch 5)

The behavioral surface of "Take Control". The Challenger creates productive disagreement with the customer — not for sport, but to move the customer's thinking.

**Mechanism:** customers default to the conversation patterns they've had with prior reps. If every rep agrees with them, they don't update their thinking. The Challenger interrupts this pattern. Disagrees on the data. Pushes back on a stated plan. Asks an uncomfortable question.

**Three constructive tension moves:**
1. **Disagree on the cause.** "You think your conversion is down because of ad cost. The data on your account suggests it's a landing-page sequence problem. Want to look?"
2. **Disagree on the metric.** "You're measuring CPL. The metric that predicts your firm's revenue isn't CPL. It's cost per signed case."
3. **Disagree on the timeline.** "You said you wanted to start in Q3. If we wait until Q3, you miss the EB-5 window. You should start now."

**Hard rule:** constructive tension requires data, not opinion. The Challenger is not contrarian for its own sake. They disagree because the evidence supports a different position.

**Failure mode:** disagreement without data = the Lone Wolf, not the Challenger. The Challenger always brings the receipt.

## Framework 5 — Mobilizer / Talker / Blocker (Challenger Customer, Ch 1-3)

The 2015 follow-up book. Built on parallel CEB research into **B2B buying committees** — the average committee at the time of study was **5.4 stakeholders** (and has grown since to 6.8+ per the later research). The implication: selling to one champion is operationally impossible. You're selling to a consensus.

The research clustered customer-side stakeholders into 7 profiles. Three matter most:

**Mobilizers (the people who can drive consensus internally):**
- **Go-Getter** — motivated by improvement. Says "this is broken, let's fix it." Acts.
- **Teacher** — motivated by sharing ideas. Already evangelizing internally. Network-rich.
- **Skeptic** — motivated by risk reduction. Demanding. Hardest to win. Highest conversion rate once won — because they've already battle-tested the idea internally.

Mobilizers are who you sell THROUGH. They do the internal work the rep can't do.

**Talkers (the people who feel like champions but aren't):**
- **Friend** — agreeable, accessible, generous with information. Loves the meetings. Signs nothing.
- **Climber** — using your deal as career capital. Will dump you if the political winds shift.
- **Guide** — loves teaching you about their org. Powerless inside it.

Talkers are the comfort trap. The rep feels like they're "in good shape" because the Talker is responsive and pleasant. The deal goes dark because the Talker can't drive consensus.

**Blockers:**
- Protect the status quo, the incumbent vendor, the existing process. Disagree on principle. Will actively work against the deal.

**The hard test:** ask your "champion" to take an action that requires internal political capital — schedule a meeting with the CFO, get the COO's reaction on a draft proposal, push for a decision date. If they punt, they're a Talker. If they execute, they're a Mobilizer.

**At DR:** the immigration-law Managing Partner who loves Ivan's pitch but "needs to discuss with my partner" three times in a row — that MP is almost always a Talker, and the partner is the actual Mobilizer (or the Blocker).

## Framework 6 — The Consensus Sale (Challenger Customer, Ch 4-6)

The implication of 5.4 stakeholders: you cannot win the deal by winning one mind. You win the deal by enabling your Mobilizer to win 4-6 other minds in their absence.

**The shift:** the rep's job changes from selling to the customer to **enabling the Mobilizer to sell internally.**

**The Mobilizer arsenal Dixon prescribes:**
1. **A customer-facing tool the Mobilizer can use without you.** A scorecard, a benchmark, a diagnostic. Something they can run on their own org.
2. **Pre-built objection responses for stakeholders not in the room.** "When your CFO asks about payback, here's the math; when your COO asks about disruption, here's the timeline."
3. **An insight package that pre-empts the most likely committee objection.** Most often: "this is too risky" or "we should wait."
4. **Coaching the Mobilizer on internal selling.** Who to talk to first. In what order. What to lead with for each.

**The failure mode that loses these deals:** rep sells beautifully to the Mobilizer, then waits. Mobilizer goes back into the org, faces resistance, can't articulate the insight, deal dies in committee. The rep didn't arm them.

**At DR:** when Ivan's prospect says "send me a deck I can share with my partner" — that's a Mobilizer asking for the arsenal. The deck Ivan sends needs to do the selling without Ivan in the room. Not a capabilities deck. A teaching deck that delivers the commercial insight in a form the Mobilizer can present.

## Framework 7 — The Three Worlds of Selling (Challenger Customer, Ch 7-8)

The buying committee can only commit to change when they reach consensus across three "worlds":

1. **The supplier world** — what your firm does, how it's different, why it works.
2. **The customer's business world** — how this fits into their operating reality.
3. **The customer's individual world** — what this means for the individual stakeholder's role, career, comfort.

Most reps sell only in world 1. Mobilizers operate across all three. The rep's job is to arm the Mobilizer to translate world-1 (supplier) insights into world-2 (business) and world-3 (individual) impact.

The committee never moves if any one stakeholder hears world-3 risk loud enough to override worlds 1 and 2. The Skeptic Mobilizer is uniquely good at running this calculus across the whole committee — which is why Skeptics convert at the highest rate.
