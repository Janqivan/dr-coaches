## Framework 1 — Pull Labor Out of COGS (SN1, foundation)

**What it is:** for services businesses, reject the standard chart of accounts that buries direct labor inside Cost of Goods Sold. Move all labor — direct, management, sales — to its own grouping below a redefined Gross Profit line.

**Why it matters:** with labor inside COGS, "Gross Profit" is contaminated. You can't measure labor productivity against a GP figure that already contains the labor. Pulling labor out reveals the real GP — what the business produces before paying any humans — and then labor can be measured against it.

**Mechanism:**
1. Revenue stays at the top.
2. Subtract only non-labor direct costs (subcontractors paid as pass-through, ad spend pass-through on retainer-plus-media, software-as-COGS, third-party data).
3. Result = true Gross Profit (services definition).
4. Below GP, group all labor in three buckets: Direct, Management, Sales.
5. Below labor, group operating overhead (rent, software, insurance, G&A).
6. Result = pretax profit (before market-based owner comp adjustment).

**Failure mode:** running services-business decisions on a product-business chart of accounts. The numbers look fine and the business goes broke anyway.

## Framework 2 — Labor Efficiency Ratio (LER) (SN1, master metric)

**What it is:** Gross Profit divided by Total Labor. The single most important productivity number in any services business.

**The 2:1 floor:** every $1 of labor must produce at least $2 of gross profit. Below that, the seat — or the business — is structurally unprofitable.

**Targets:**
- 2:1 = floor. Below this, you're losing money on labor.
- 2.5:1 = healthy.
- 3:1 = strong.
- 3:1 to 4:1 = world-class services operator.

**Why a ratio, not a dollar amount:** the LER stays meaningful as the business scales. A $1M firm at 2.5:1 and a $30M firm at 2.5:1 are equally healthy on labor productivity. Dollar-amount targets break at scale; ratios don't.

**Failure mode:** measuring labor productivity by revenue-per-head. Revenue-per-head ignores the GP line — a firm with 50% revenue pass-through to media can post a strong revenue-per-head and still have a broken LER.

## Framework 3 — The Three LER Buckets (SN2)

**What it is:** total labor is meaningless unless split into the three buckets that each do a different job in a services business.

**Direct labor LER** = GP / direct delivery labor. The people doing the client work. Target 2.5:1+.

**Management labor LER** = GP / management labor. The people running the people. This bucket is leverage — it shouldn't deliver, it should multiply Direct labor's output. Target should be high; management is overhead that earns its place only by raising Direct LER.

**Sales labor LER** = GP / sales labor. The people generating new revenue. Target depends on sales cycle and deal size, but if Sales LER drags total LER below 2:1, the sales org is the bottleneck — not delivery.

**Why split:** a great Direct LER hides a bloated Management LER. A great Management LER hides a broken Sales LER. The blended number lies. Each bucket has to be solved separately, with different interventions.

**Failure mode:** "our LER is 2.3:1, we're fine" — when Direct is 3:1, Management is 0.8:1, Sales is 1.5:1. The Management overhead is killing the firm and the blended ratio hides it.

## Framework 4 — Market-Based Owner Compensation (SN1)

**What it is:** before measuring profit, pay yourself the market salary for the job you actually do — based on what you'd have to pay a non-owner CEO / president / operator of similar size and complexity.

**Mechanism:**
1. Define the role you actually perform (CEO of $X-revenue services firm in Y region).
2. Look up the market salary for that role at that size in that geography.
3. Run owner comp into the labor line at that number, regardless of what you currently pay yourself.
4. Then calculate profit on what's left.

**Why:** owners who under-pay themselves create the illusion of profit. Investors, lenders, and the owner themselves get a false read on the business. The day the owner steps back or wants to sell, the buyer plugs in market comp and "profit" disappears. Better to know now.

**Where it hides losses:** owner takes $60K in pay where market is $250K → business shows $190K of "profit" that's actually $190K of suppressed owner compensation. The firm is losing money. The owner is funding it.

**Failure mode:** treating low owner pay as virtuous restraint. It's a measurement error, not a discipline.

## Framework 5 — The 10-15% Pretax Profit Rule (SN1)

**What it is:** after market-based owner comp, healthy services businesses produce 10-15% pretax profit. Below 10% — broken model. Above 15% — usually means owner is still under-paid OR the business is under-investing.

**The band:**
- **Below 10%:** the business doesn't have the margin to weather a downturn or fund growth. Re-examine LER (probably below 2:1 in at least one bucket) or pricing.
- **10-15%:** the healthy band. Margin to reinvest, build Core Capital, weather quarters, take distributions.
- **Above 15%:** check the owner comp number — usually under-stated. Or the firm is starving the team / capacity / growth investments. Profit at the cost of future earning power is a worse trade than it looks.

**Why this band specifically:** services businesses with sub-10% pretax can't generate the cash to fund Core Capital, distributions, and reinvestment simultaneously. Above 15%, you've usually picked one of the three to sacrifice without realizing it.

**Failure mode:** chasing 25% pretax by under-investing in management hires or under-paying senior team members. The number looks great this year. The talent leaves next year.

## Framework 6 — Core Capital Target (SN1)

**What it is:** the cash fortress baseline. Two months of operating expenses, held in cash, free and clear of any debt or line of credit, not in receivables or WIP.

**The math:**
- Average monthly operating expenses (full opex, not just G&A) x 2 = Core Capital Target.
- "Free and clear" = no LOC outstanding, no debt tied to it, immediately available.

**Why two months:** it's the minimum runway to absorb a major client loss, a downturn quarter, a payroll surprise, a tax bill, or a working-capital squeeze from a single large invoice going late. Below 2 months, every operational decision gets contaminated by cash fear — you can't make rational pricing, hiring, or firing decisions when you're worried about Friday's payroll.

**Sequencing rule:** until Core Capital Target is hit, distributions and aggressive reinvestment both wait. Once it's hit, distributions and reinvestment can resume — but never to a level that breaks Core Capital again.

**Failure mode:** treating the line of credit as "cash equivalent." It isn't. A bank can pull a LOC at the exact moment you need it most.

## Framework 7 — The Four Forces of Cash Flow (SN2)

**What it is:** every dollar of pretax profit fights four forces before it becomes distributable cash to the owner.

**The four forces:**
1. **Profit retention** — what you choose to keep inside the business for reinvestment.
2. **Capitalization** — debt service and Core Capital build. Below Core Capital Target, this force eats first.
3. **Tax** — federal, state, payroll, and (in non-US jurisdictions) corporate / dividend / VAT depending on country structure.
4. **Working capital** — funding receivables growth, WIP, payroll-ahead-of-collections. A growing services business eats cash through working capital even when profitable.

**Why it matters:** owners look at the P&L, see profit, and expect that profit to be distributable. The four forces consume most of it before it gets to the owner's pocket. A 12% pretax firm that's growing 30% YoY may distribute 0% — all of it gets eaten by working capital and tax.

**Operator move:** model the four forces explicitly before any growth decision. "If I take this on, what does it do to working capital? Can Core Capital absorb the working-capital draw? What's the tax hit on the extra profit?"

**Failure mode:** confusing profit with cash. They're not the same number. They're often not even the same sign.

## Framework 8 — The Black Hole ($1M-$5M) (SN2)

**What it is:** the services-business valley between $1M and $5M revenue. Too big to be lean (you've hired ops, management, infrastructure), too small to be optimized (you don't have the volume to absorb the overhead efficiently).

**What happens here:**
- Management labor goes on the books before the GP exists to support it.
- Management LER drops sharply.
- Total LER drops below 2:1 even if Direct LER is still healthy.
- Owner comp gets squeezed to maintain "profit."
- Distributions disappear.
- Core Capital erodes if it was ever built.

**Why most firms die here:** the owner responds to the LER drop by chasing more revenue. More revenue, at the same broken LER, makes the hole deeper. The right move is the opposite — fix LER first, revenue second.

**Crabtree's exit path:**
1. Ruthless LER discipline by labor bucket — find the seats below 2:1.
2. Owner comp held at market (don't raise it to celebrate growth).
3. Core Capital Target maintained throughout (no pulling from cash to fund growth).
4. No headcount additions without GP math attached to the seat.
5. Pricing review — most Black Hole firms are under-priced relative to the management overhead they now carry.

**Failure mode:** "we just need to get to $10M and we'll be fine." No — at $10M with the same broken LER, you'll have $10M of broken LER.

## Framework 9 — Rolling-Twelve P&L (SN2)

**What it is:** a 12-month trailing P&L updated every month, used as the primary operating dashboard instead of fiscal-year YTD.

**Why rolling twelve beats YTD:**
- YTD hides trend reversals. A great Q1 and declining Q2-Q3 still look fine in the YTD column. Rolling twelve shows the slope.
- Rolling twelve normalizes for seasonality (always 12 months, regardless of where in the fiscal year you are).
- Rolling twelve is decision-grade data. YTD is tax-filing data.

**How to read it:**
- Compare the last 12 months to the previous 12 months on every line.
- Watch the slope, not just the absolute numbers.
- LER, GP, pretax all get rolling-twelve treatment.

**Failure mode:** running operations on YTD numbers, finding out in February that the back half of last year was bleeding.

## Framework 10 — Salary Cap and Productivity Per Labor Dollar (SN2)

**What it is:** the operating rule that says — for any given level of GP, there's a maximum total labor cost the business can sustain at 2:1 LER.

**The math:** if GP is $4M, total labor cap is $2M. Period. Any combination of Direct + Management + Sales labor that exceeds $2M breaks the 2:1 floor.

**The operator's job:** allocate that $2M across the three buckets in the proportion that maximizes productivity. Most services firms over-allocate to Management once they cross $1M revenue, breaking the cap without realizing it.

**The "cheapest expensive thing":** a low-LER seat the owner tolerates because firing feels worse than the financial pain. Crabtree's view — the financial pain is real and compounding, the firing pain is one-time. Run the math, then do the hard thing.

**Failure mode:** adding seats because "we need more capacity" without checking whether existing capacity is producing 2:1. Adding labor to a sub-2:1 system makes the system worse, not better.
